### Study 1: Delivery subscriptions nearly double spending — and sometimes grow the whole category
**Work ID:** W7222938569 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. 90.4% attributed to both platforms** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Researchers analyzed individual-level transaction data covering Postmates Unlimited ... and DoorDash's DashPass ... Signing up was associated with a 90.4% increase in spending at the subscribing platform"
- **Abstract says:** "signing up for Postmates Unlimited is associated with a 90.4% increase in spending at the focal firm ... DashPass results are broadly similar"
- **Problem:** The 90.4% estimate is specifically for Postmates Unlimited; the digest reads as if it covers signing up for either program.
- **Suggested fix:** "Attribute the 90.4% to Postmates Unlimited and note DashPass results were 'broadly similar'."

**Issue B. Dropped DashPass boundary finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "evidence of category expansion rather than pure share-stealing"
- **Abstract says:** "DashPass results are broadly similar, but customers with prior Postmates subscription experience show little increase in category spending"
- **Problem:** The abstract names subscription experience as a condition that limits category expansion; the digest omits it, strengthening the expansion story.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add that customers who had already held a delivery subscription showed little additional category spending."

**Issue C. Mechanism not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "this suggests their biggest effect may be habit formation that grows total category demand"
- **Abstract says:** "these findings suggest subscriptions can both expand markets and capture share, depending on prior platform usage and subscription experience"
- **Problem:** 'Habit formation' is the digest's own mechanism; the abstract makes no claim about habit and explicitly frames the result as both expansion and share capture.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say the lift persisted after cancellation without naming habit formation as the mechanism, and keep the abstract's 'both expand and capture' framing."

**Issue D. Association presented as personal prediction** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "If you pay for a delivery membership, expect to order far more than you did before — and to keep ordering more even after you cancel."
- **Abstract says:** "signing up ... is associated with a 90.4% increase in spending at the focal firm"
- **Problem:** Observational, matched DiD associations for two platforms are translated into a confident forecast for any individual reader.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as 'subscribers in this data spent far more, on average, and the lift persisted after cancellation'."

### Study 5: Planes got cleaner, emissions still rose: what carbon pricing could cost an island tourism economy
**Work ID:** W7223185322 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Omitted regional value-added figure** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "tourism demand fell as much as 29.4% and accommodation-sector value added by up to 24.9%"
- **Abstract says:** "tourism demand falls by up to 29.4%, and the regional and accommodation-sector value added by up to 5% and 24.9%, respectively"
- **Problem:** Dropping the up-to-5% hit to overall regional value added leaves readers with only the sector-specific worst case, overstating economy-wide exposure.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add that overall regional value added falls by up to 5% in the same scenario."

**Issue B. Scenario conditions conflated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "under a more price-responsive demand scenario with damage-based carbon valuations, tourism demand fell as much as 29.4%"
- **Abstract says:** "Under the main case, with a moderate demand response, historical carbon-price benchmarks generate limited regional effects, whereas higher damage-based valuations imply greater adjustment. Under the more responsive demand scenario, tourism demand falls by up to 29.4%"
- **Problem:** The abstract presents demand responsiveness and valuation basis as separate dimensions; the digest fuses them into a single scenario.
- **Suggested fix:** "Describe the two levers separately: damage-based valuations imply greater adjustment, and the more responsive demand scenario yields the 29.4% figure."

### Study 7: When hidden fees sting: blaming a human hurts more than blaming the algorithm
**Work ID:** W7214557291 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal language for correlational mediation** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "perceived deception translated into a sense of betrayal, which produced anger, which in turn drove dissatisfaction — deception did not directly cause anger or dissatisfaction on its own"
- **Abstract says:** "perceived deception was consistently associated with betrayal but not directly with anger or dissatisfaction. Anger was the most consistent direct correlate of dissatisfaction, while betrayal was indirectly associated with dissatisfaction through anger"
- **Problem:** The abstract uses strictly associational wording for the SEM paths; 'translated into', 'produced', 'drove' and 'cause' imply a tested causal chain.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use 'was associated with' / 'was indirectly linked to' language for the betrayal–anger–dissatisfaction sequence."

**Issue B. Unmeasured outcome added** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the emotional sequence is betrayal first, anger second, and only then the decision never to return"
- **Abstract says:** "perceived deception ... betrayal, anger, and dissatisfaction"
- **Problem:** The studies measured dissatisfaction, not return or repurchase decisions.
- **Suggested fix:** "End the sequence at dissatisfaction rather than a decision never to return."

### Study 9: Shy shoppers are more price-conscious — and more paralyzed by too much choice
**Work ID:** W7223569994 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Scale developed vs. validated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Researchers developed and validated a context-specific "consumer shyness" measure for apparel retail"
- **Abstract says:** "validating and contextualizing an existing consumer shyness scale within a setting characterized by high levels of social visibility"
- **Problem:** The contribution was validation and contextualization of an existing scale, not development of a new one.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say researchers validated and contextualized an existing consumer shyness scale for apparel retail."

**Issue B. Explanatory leap in 'Why it matters'** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "a sizable segment of shoppers responds to price cues and gets overwhelmed by assortment not because of budget or taste, but because the store is a socially uncomfortable place"
- **Abstract says:** "Consumers with higher levels of consumer shyness tend to exhibit stronger brand and price consciousness, as well as greater confusion from overchoice"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports correlations between a trait measure and self-reported decision styles; it does not establish that social discomfort rather than budget or taste drives price consciousness, nor does it size the segment.
- **Suggested fix:** "Note that shyness correlates with price consciousness and overchoice confusion, without asserting cause or segment size."

### Study 10: Why a platform might hand its influencer rivals better tools — and make advertisers pay for it
**Work ID:** W7220981580 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Dropped boundary condition** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Counterintuitively, enabling the feature can pay off even for free: the platform loses some advertising share but can raise its own ad prices enough to more than compensate."
- **Abstract says:** "even when the platform cannot charge the influencers for using the feature, it can still benefit if the advertisers' valuation of the platform's service is not significantly lower than that of the influencer's service"
- **Problem:** The free-enablement result holds only under a stated condition on advertisers' relative valuations, which the digest omits.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add that this holds when advertisers value the platform's own ad service roughly as highly as the influencer's."

**Issue B. Overstated offset** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "can raise its own ad prices enough to more than compensate"
- **Abstract says:** "the platform can potentially offset this loss by raising its prices"
- **Problem:** The abstract says the loss can potentially be offset, not that price increases more than compensate.
- **Suggested fix:** "Write 'can potentially offset that loss by raising its prices'."

### Study 11: The Caitlin Clark bump: sponsors gained 0.13% on game days — then gave it back
**Work ID:** W7212373919 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Win effect described per-game rather than as winning percentage** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "with bigger gains when her team won"
- **Abstract says:** "The winning percentage of Clark's team has a positive effect on the returns generated for sponsors."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports a team-level winning percentage effect on sponsor returns, not a game-by-game win/loss contrast. As written, a reader would think each individual victory produced a measurably larger return.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "with larger returns for sponsors of a higher-winning team" or "returns were higher when the team's winning percentage was higher.""

**Issue B. Headline timing of the return window** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "sponsors gained 0.13% on game days — then gave it back"
- **Abstract says:** "an abnormal return of 13-basis points (0.13 percent) on the trading day following a game"
- **Problem:** The abnormal return occurs on the trading day after a game, not on game day itself; the body text gets this right but the headline does not.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change headline to "sponsors gained 0.13% the day after games — then gave it back.""
