### Study 1: Copywriters who hate your brand write duller ads — and they do it on purpose
**Work ID:** W7221130306 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Unsupported supporting claim** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Emotional advertising generally outperforms purely functional advertising on long-term brand effects, so a creative team's private dislike of a client brand may quietly push the work toward the weaker end of that spectrum."
- **Abstract says:** "The abstract reports only that creators with negative brand attitudes use more functional and fewer emotional elements, and explains the suppression mechanism; it makes no claim about the relative effectiveness of emotional vs. functional advertising."
- **Problem:** The premise that makes the finding consequential is imported from elsewhere and is unverifiable from this abstract alone.
- **Suggested fix:** "Attribute the effectiveness premise to prior literature outside this paper, e.g. "if, as other research suggests, emotional advertising tends to outperform functional advertising on long-term brand effects...""

### Study 3: Collaboration fatigue is real: why young Chinese consumers are turning on co-branded launches
**Work ID:** W7221096517 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Causal and non-configurational framing of a qualitative/fsQCA result** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "pacing, release density and the gap between promotional claims and actual product experience each drive backlash independently"
- **Abstract says:** ""Study 2 identifies equifinal, largely conjunctural configurations associated with high CNA" and the study examines "how and under what conditions co-branding in collaboration-saturated markets is associated with negative consumer responses"; release density and aligning promotional claims appear as practical recommendations, not as independently tested drivers."
- **Problem:** Asserts independent causal effects for factors the abstract treats as conjunctural configurations and as associations only; it also contradicts the digest's own later statement that "resistance comes from combinations."
- **Suggested fix:** "Rewrite as "governance, pacing, symbolic coherence and experiential costs appear in the configurations associated with high resistance, usually in combination rather than singly.""

**Issue B. Detail not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the collaboration costs more, means less, and delivers a worse experience than the plain version of the product"
- **Abstract says:** "The abstract names "practical value distortion, conflict of meaning interpretation and emotional experience split" and "experiential costs," but says nothing about price premiums versus the standard product."
- **Problem:** "Costs more" is added colour not supported by the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "Drop the price claim or recast as "the practical value feels distorted and the experience falls short of the promise.""

### Study 5: Ethical scandals ignite boycotts; product failures mostly just annoy people

### Study 6: Price promotions may generate more real sales — and less brand-switching — than standard models assume
**Work ID:** W7220913319 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Set identification stated as identification** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "showing that consumers' discount factor can be identified from the model's own built-in restrictions without the extra assumptions about storage costs that prior work relied on"
- **Abstract says:** ""the DDC has built-in exclusion restrictions that set-identify the discount factor without requiring any auxiliary assumptions... such structure... can be included to achieve point identification and improve statistical efficiency""
- **Problem:** The abstract's result is set identification without auxiliary assumptions; point identification still requires the extra structure. The digest flattens this distinction.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say the built-in restrictions "set-identify" the discount factor, with the storage-cost assumptions optional for point identification and efficiency."

**Issue B. Over-reading a model parameter as observed shopper behaviour** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The estimated discount factor near 0.96 is evidence that ordinary shoppers are strategic: when detergent goes on sale they are, in effect, calculating future consumption and storage, not just grabbing a bargain."
- **Abstract says:** ""we find a mean discount factor estimate of $0.957$, rejecting myopic models of choice in favor of the dynamic model.""
- **Problem:** The estimate indicates that a forward-looking model fits the data better than a myopic one; it is not direct evidence of conscious calculation by individual shoppers.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as "purchase patterns fit a forward-looking model better than a myopic one — behaviour consistent with stocking up when prices dip.""

### Study 7: Not all social proof helps: testimonials that look paid for can actively reduce repurchase
**Work ID:** W7216479640 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Intention reported as behaviour in headline** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "testimonials that look paid for can actively reduce repurchase"
- **Abstract says:** "testimonials "when seen as commercially motivated... independently trigger skepticism and weaken repurchase intention""
- **Problem:** The outcome measured was stated repurchase intention in a cross-sectional survey, not repurchase; the body caveat notes this but the headline does not.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add "intention" to the headline: "...can actively reduce repurchase intention.""

**Issue B. Untested comparison and outcome swap** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "the moment a testimonial reads as bought, it doesn't just stop working — it makes people trust the brand less than if there had been no testimonial at all"
- **Abstract says:** "commercially motivated testimonials "independently trigger skepticism and weaken repurchase intention""
- **Problem:** The abstract reports skepticism and weakened repurchase intention; it does not report a brand-trust outcome, nor a comparison against a no-testimonial control.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rewrite as "when a testimonial reads as commercially motivated, it was associated with more skepticism and weaker repurchase intention.""

### Study 8: The cup is the product: café packaging design shapes buying intent — differently for Starbucks and Luckin
**Work ID:** W7220832664 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Difference in degree reported as absence of effect** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "status signaling pays off for premium brands and barely registers for budget ones"
- **Abstract says:** ""All three design facets significantly influenced hedonic and utilitarian values... Symbolism and utilitarian value effects were stronger for Starbucks than for Luckin.""
- **Problem:** The abstract reports a stronger effect for Starbucks, not a negligible one for Luckin; symbolic design was significant in both groups.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rewrite as "symbolic design mattered more for the premium brand than the budget one, though it registered for both.""

**Issue B. Overstated consumer-facing claim** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "something that disappears entirely when the brand is a budget one"
- **Abstract says:** ""All three design facets significantly influenced hedonic and utilitarian values" in both brand contexts, with symbolism simply stronger for Starbucks."
- **Problem:** "Disappears entirely" contradicts the reported finding that symbolic design was significant for Luckin customers too.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "something that matters less, though not nothing, when the brand is a budget one.""

**Issue C. Added significance qualifier** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "All three design facets fed both value routes for both brands, but symbolic design and utilitarian value mattered significantly more for Starbucks customers."
- **Abstract says:** ""Symbolism and utilitarian value effects were stronger for Starbucks than for Luckin.""
- **Problem:** The abstract describes the multigroup difference as "stronger" without labelling it statistically significant.
- **Suggested fix:** "Drop "significantly" or say "were stronger for Starbucks customers.""

### Study 10: Modest brands outsell boastful ones — unless the brand is luxury
**Work ID:** W7203440056 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Attenuated reported as eliminated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "for luxury brands boastful messaging matched brand identity and the gap between the two styles disappeared"
- **Abstract says:** ""differences between message types are attenuated for luxury brands, allowing greater flexibility in message strategy""
- **Problem:** Attenuated means reduced, not eliminated; "disappeared" overstates the moderation.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "the gap between the two styles narrowed, giving luxury brands more latitude.""

**Issue B. Headline implies market sales** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Modest brands outsell boastful ones — unless the brand is luxury"
- **Abstract says:** ""brands using self-effacing rather than self-enhancing messages elicit stronger consumer attitudes and purchasing behaviors, even in the presence of monetary tradeoffs" — across four studies."
- **Problem:** Purchasing was measured within experimental tasks (as the digest's caveat notes); "outsell" suggests real-world sales performance.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "Modest brand messages win more purchases in experiments — unless the brand is luxury.""

### Study 11: When AI writes your brand's social stance, consumers smell inauthenticity
**Work ID:** W7203997372 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Untested comparison with non-activism content** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "AI-drafted product copy is fine, AI-drafted moral conviction is not, and the discomfort shows up as a worse feeling about your own purchase decision."
- **Abstract says:** "Across five studies, we find that AI-created activism elicits greater negative affect, ultimately reducing decision comfort. This occurs because consumers perceive these messages as less authentic."
- **Problem:** The research examined only brand activism (value-laden) messages; it did not test ordinary product copy, so the claim that AI-written product copy "is fine" is not supported by this study.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to: "The study tested only activism messages, so it shows a penalty for AI-written moral statements — it does not tell us how AI-drafted product copy fares.""

**Issue B. Quality claim not tested** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the penalty appears to be about perceived authenticity, not output quality"
- **Abstract says:** "This occurs because consumers perceive these messages as less authentic."
- **Problem:** The abstract identifies perceived authenticity as the mechanism but reports no measure or comparison of output quality, so ruling quality out is an inference.
- **Suggested fix:** "Drop "not output quality" or say "the measured mechanism was perceived authenticity.""

**Work ID:** W7219659105 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

### Study 14: Mystery boxes work — but only for the right kind of shopper
**Work ID:** W7203651996 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Stimuli specifics not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "They manipulated or measured two epistemic motivations (need for cognition, the enjoyment of effortful thinking; and need for cognitive closure, the desire for a quick, definite answer)"
- **Abstract says:** "Across six studies, this research demonstrates that motivational congruence elevates purchase intentions, trust and perceived self-efficacy."
- **Problem:** The abstract does not state whether the epistemic motivations were manipulated or measured, nor that mystery boxes, surprise bundles or blind drops were the stimuli — these are illustrations, not reported design details. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say the abstract describes "uncertainty marketing" tactics generally and note that the specific offers and whether traits were manipulated are not specified."

### Study 15: "Love it, hate it, can't quit it": ambivalence isn't a phase of social media use, it's the structure
**Work ID:** W7220944415 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Over time" implies longitudinal evidence** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "co-occur within the same accounts and do not resolve over time"
- **Abstract says:** "mixed emotions and ambivalent evaluations frequently co‐occur within the same accounts and persist across contexts, including discourse about quitting"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports persistence across contexts, not across measured time; "over time" suggests longitudinal tracking that is not described.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "persist across contexts, including when people talk about quitting.""

**Issue B. Stronger claim than abstract makes** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "net-benefit models — which assume consumers settle into either liking or disliking a platform — miss this persistent ambivalence"
- **Abstract says:** "we show convergent evidence that net‐benefit models do not fully capture SMU outcomes"
- **Problem:** The abstract says such models "do not fully capture" outcomes; "miss" overstates it, and the parenthetical characterization of what those models assume is the digest's own gloss.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "do not fully capture this persistent ambivalence.""

### Study 20: Consumers forgive AI-designed products — if they believe the brand is driven by passion, not profit
**Work ID:** W7202384182 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline implies an unreported baseline penalty** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Consumers forgive AI-designed products — if they believe the brand is driven by passion, not profit"
- **Abstract says:** "Results indicate that AI-designed products receive more favourable evaluations when associated with passion-driven rather than profit-driven brands."
- **Problem:** "Forgive" presumes AI design is penalized by default; the abstract reports only a relative difference between passion- and profit-framed brands, not an overall AI-versus-human deficit.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as "AI-designed products are judged more favourably when the brand reads as passion-driven rather than profit-driven.""

**Issue B. Disclosure inference** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "this suggests disclosure isn't inherently damaging — what matters is whether consumers already read the brand's motives as sincere"
- **Abstract says:** "Two experiments were conducted using stimuli of wedding jewellery and craft beer... Results indicate that AI-designed products receive more favourable evaluations when associated with passion-driven rather than profit-driven brands."
- **Problem:** The studies described AI involvement within experimental stimuli; the abstract does not test a disclosure decision or compare disclosing versus not disclosing.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say the study varied whether a product was described as AI- or human-designed, and avoid framing the result as evidence about disclosure strategy."
