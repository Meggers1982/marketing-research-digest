### Study 2: AI chatbots now out-score humans on textbook marketing knowledge
**Work ID:** W7211986357 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Free" utility not supported** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "has effectively become a free, on-demand utility"
- **Abstract says:** "they now provide reliable, on-demand access to much of the discipline's foundational knowledge"
- **Problem:** The abstract says "reliable, on-demand access" and says nothing about cost; leading commercial LLMs are often paid, so "free" is an addition.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "an on-demand utility" or "cheap, on-demand access"."

### Study 3: In a 50,000-product test, the first item people saw shaped what they wanted next
**Work ID:** W7210288362 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Hedge dropped from the substantive result** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "entirely incidental initial products — ones with no logical bearing on the decision — shaped participants' subsequent preferences"
- **Abstract says:** "Results show that entirely incidental initial products can shape participants' subsequent preferences."
- **Problem:** The abstract's "can shape" is a demonstration of possibility; the digest states it as a general occurrence, and the headline ("shaped what they wanted next") carries the same firmness.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "can shape participants' subsequent preferences" in the body and soften the headline accordingly."

**Issue B. "Anchoring effect" label added** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "It both documents a real anchoring effect at unusual scale"
- **Abstract says:** "Results show that entirely incidental initial products can shape participants' subsequent preferences."
- **Problem:** The abstract never names anchoring or characterizes the size of the effect; the label and "at unusual scale" are the digest's interpretation.
- **Suggested fix:** "Describe it as an incidental-exposure effect observed in a large-scale stimulus set, without naming a mechanism the abstract does not."

### Study 6: On China's RED, the recommendation algorithm didn't move buyers — influencer expertise and user-made content did
**Work ID:** W7214221690 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline overstates a null mediation path** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the recommendation algorithm didn't move buyers"
- **Abstract says:** "KOL expertise and content co-creation significantly enhance SP, whereas user–platform interaction and AR do not."
- **Problem:** The tested null is algorithmic recommendation's effect on social presence within an SEM, not a direct test of whether the algorithm moves purchases; the headline reads as a stronger behavioral claim than the model supports.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to something like "the recommendation algorithm didn't build social presence — influencer expertise and user-made content did"."

**Issue B. Authors' boundary hedge omitted** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "It suggests that on community-driven commerce platforms, algorithmic reach alone may not convert — the persuasive work is done by credible people and content that shoppers help create."
- **Abstract says:** "Rather than claiming a general superiority of human over technological factors, it specifies the boundary conditions under which technical stimuli fail to translate into SP"
- **Problem:** The abstract explicitly declines to claim human factors beat technological ones in general; the digest's summary edges toward that broader claim.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add that the authors frame this as specific boundary conditions on when technical stimuli fail, not a general superiority of human over algorithmic factors."

### Study 9: Treated badly by a human? Consumers start preferring the bot
**Work ID:** W7214431211 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Firmer wording than abstract's association language** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Perceived discrimination consistently increased preference for AI-based service"
- **Abstract says:** "Across the three studies, perceived discrimination was associated with greater preference for AI service providers"
- **Problem:** The abstract deliberately uses "was associated with" across the three designs (one of which is recall-based); "consistently increased" reads as an established causal effect throughout.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "was consistently associated with greater preference for AI-based service"."

**Issue B. Direct-effect moderation nuance omitted** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the cultural-tightness moderation was inconsistent across the AI-ethicality pathway"
- **Abstract says:** "Cultural tightness consistently strengthened the social-comfort pathway, whereas its moderating influence on perceived AI ethicality and the direct effect was less consistent."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports inconsistency for both the AI-ethicality pathway and the direct effect; only the former is mentioned.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add "and for the direct effect" to the caveat."

### Study 10: AI wrote more code. Then the bottleneck moved to the humans reviewing it.
**Work ID:** W4400488941 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Net positive effect omitted** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "it shows that the return on AI is capped not by how fast work gets generated but by how fast an organization can review and integrate it"
- **Abstract says:** "Overall, Copilot still increases the amount of code that is integrated in a timely manner, but the benefits are unevenly distributed."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports that, despite longer coordination times, Copilot still raised timely integrated output. Omitting this makes the finding read as if review friction cancelled the gains, which could lead readers to the wrong conclusion about AI's net return.
- **Suggested fix:** "State that Copilot still increased the volume of code integrated in a timely manner overall, with coordination costs and uneven distribution as the qualifier."

**Issue B. Open-source volunteers framed as production teams** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "It is one of the first large-scale looks at generative AI inside real production teams"
- **Abstract says:** "collaborative open-source software (OSS) development, where developers voluntarily contribute and collaborate on projects"
- **Problem:** The setting is voluntary open-source collaboration, not commercial production teams; the body claim sits awkwardly with the digest's own caveat about transfer to commercial teams.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "inside real collaborative open-source projects"."

### Study 11: Food delivery apps are pulling people away from the restaurants on their own street — especially in lower-income neighborhoods
**Work ID:** W7214553827 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal language contradicts the caveat** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "delivery platforms don't just add convenience, they measurably redistribute foot traffic away from neighborhood catchments unless local retail is dense enough to hold it"
- **Abstract says:** "greater food-delivery and instant-retail availability is consistently associated with lower physical-visit localisation… SHAP interaction analyses show that this negative platform–localisation association is conditional on physical retail provision"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports associations from observational location-based service data; "measurably redistribute" asserts a causal transfer of foot traffic, directly contradicting the digest's own caveat that the associations are correlational. The headline ("are pulling people away") carries the same problem.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to associational terms, e.g. "neighbourhoods with more delivery availability show measurably fewer visits staying local" and soften the headline to "Food delivery apps are linked to fewer visits to nearby restaurants.""

**Issue B. Prescriptive framing from a single observational study** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "agglomeration is the defense against platform leakage — an isolated storefront in a delivery-saturated area is far more exposed than the same store inside a dense cluster. Retailers should be modeling platform penetration alongside traditional catchment metrics when signing leases"
- **Abstract says:** "positive interaction contributions between platform-service availability and retail agglomeration emerge where restaurant and shopping-mall densities are high, consistent with a buffering role of dense local retail clusters"
- **Problem:** The abstract describes an interaction pattern "consistent with" a buffering role; the digest converts this into a settled leasing playbook and a quantified comparison ("far more exposed") not present in the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "Frame as a hypothesis: "the pattern is consistent with dense retail clusters buffering platform leakage, which retailers could test against their own catchment data.""

### Study 13: Adding "imagine it's yours" cues to sponsored posts beat pumping up the offer
**Work ID:** W7213237840 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline implies an untested comparison** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Adding "imagine it's yours" cues to sponsored posts beat pumping up the offer"
- **Abstract says:** "the effectiveness of sponsored posts can be enhanced not by intensifying individual stimuli but by embedding ownership activation cues that reframe how these stimuli are interpreted"
- **Problem:** This is the authors' practical implication, not a reported result of a direct contest between ownership cues and intensified incentives; "beat" suggests a measured horse race that the abstract does not report.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to, e.g., "Ownership cues lifted responses to sponsored posts — the authors argue reframing beats intensifying the offer.""

**Issue B. Impulse-purchase inference** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "That mental rehearsal, not the discount, may be what nudges an impulse purchase."
- **Abstract says:** "The expected moderating effects of impulsive buying tendency were not supported… measures cognitive, affective, and behavioural outcomes"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports evaluations and intention-level outcomes, and the one impulse-related variable tested (impulsive buying tendency) did not moderate; attributing an impulse purchase to the cue overreaches, even hedged.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say the cues improved how people evaluated the post, and note the study found no role for impulsive buying tendency."
