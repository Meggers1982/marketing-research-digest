### 1. Mobile coupons work harder before shoppers reach the mall than inside it

**Journal:** *European Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-26
**Work ID:** W7223707755 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ejm-12-2024-1010

**The study:** Researchers analyzed consumers' mobile coupon-click behavior for stores in a multi-store shopping mall, comparing clicks made outside the mall with clicks made once shoppers were already inside, then built a game-theoretic model of coupon issuing and targeting decisions. For experiential-product stores, coupons clicked outside the mall were linked to a stronger purchase response than inside-mall clicks — consistent with outside coupons pulling traffic in rather than just converting shoppers already there. The modeling then shows that because outside-mall coupons draw foot traffic that benefits neighboring stores, individual retailers can free-ride, and may rationally decline to issue coupons even when everyone couponing together would leave all of them better off.

**Why it matters:** Location-based mobile targeting is usually judged on the issuing store's own conversion rate, which systematically undervalues coupons that bring people through the door and into the whole center; mall operators have a concrete reason to subsidize or coordinate coupon programs rather than leave them to tenants.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Test geofencing radius as a strategic variable, not a technical setting: compare pre-arrival targeting against in-store targeting and measure the halo on neighboring tenants, not just your own till. For landlords and center managers, the free-riding result suggests a case for centrally funded or coordinated coupon campaigns — worth piloting in one property before rolling out.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The offers pinging your phone while you're still driving to the mall are doing a different job than the ones that appear once you're inside — the first are designed to get you to come at all, which is also why the ones you receive on the way can be the more generous.

**Caveats:** Empirical component is observational click-and-purchase data from a single mall setting, so the outside-versus-inside difference is an association rather than a randomized test; the core strategic conclusions come from a mathematical model with assumptions about spillovers and competition; the authors note generalizability to other geographies, multiple malls and competitive conditions is untested.

### 2. The shoppers who use cross-border platforms most are the ones who most oppose regulating them

**Journal:** *International Journal of Consumer Studies* | **Published:** 2026-10-20
**Work ID:** W7223071223 | **DOI:** 10.1111/ijcs.70260

**The study:** Using 1,500 survey responses from South Korean adults aged 20–59 collected through the OpenSurvey panel, researchers examined attitudes to South Korea's 2024 cross-border e-commerce regulatory initiative — a measure walked back within days amid public backlash. Using probit models, propensity score matching and an instrumental-variable check, they found that consumers with direct experience buying from overseas platforms were roughly 9–11 percentage points more likely to oppose the regulation; awareness of the regulation itself also predicted opposition. The pattern suggests experienced buyers judge rules against their own accumulated platform knowledge rather than welcoming paternalistic protection.

**Why it matters:** It reframes consumer-protection rules as a political risk for platforms and regulators alike: the heaviest users of a channel form the most organized opposition to restricting it, which helps explain how a safety-framed measure can collapse in days.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For platforms and trade bodies facing regulatory threat, the most credible advocates are existing customers, not lobbyists — but the finding also warns brands that "we're protecting you" messaging lands badly with experienced buyers, who read it as condescension. Worth testing how your own regulatory and trust-and-safety communications read to your heaviest users.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Experience with a marketplace, including its risks, appears to make people trust their own judgment over a government's — a reminder that consumers who have been burned by a cheap overseas order often conclude they can manage the risk themselves rather than that someone should ban it.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional online panel survey in a single country, so causation cannot be established despite matching and IV checks; measures attitudes toward one specific 2024 policy rather than behavior; subgroup differences by income and age were not statistically significant in formal interaction tests.

### 3. Shoppers Can't Measure Air Quality — But Their Noses Decide How Long They Stay

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing* | **Published:** 2026-10-01
**Work ID:** W7222049986 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretai.2026.09.006

**The study:** Across five scale-development studies plus three validation studies published in the *Journal of Retailing*, researchers built and tested "AirQual," a 26-item measure of how customers perceive store air — breathability, circulation, freshness, humidity, scent and temperature. In Study 2, conducted with café customers, perceived air quality predicted how long people lingered, and dwell time in turn predicted how much they spent; an objective air quality sensor reading predicted neither. Study 3 replicated the pattern with supermarket shoppers, and Study 4 added experimental validation.

**Why it matters:** Retailers spend on HVAC and air monitoring against engineering benchmarks, but the data suggest it is the subjective experience of the air — not the instrument reading — that translates into dwell time and basket size.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Retail and facilities teams typically report air quality as a compliance number. This research suggests testing a customer-perception measure alongside the sensors, and treating air as part of the atmospherics budget next to lighting, music and scent — then checking in your own stores whether perceived freshness moves dwell time and spend.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Why you leave one store feeling you could browse all afternoon and bolt from another within two minutes: stuffy, humid or oddly scented air registers as discomfort long before you consciously name it, and it quietly shortens your visit.

**Caveats:** Scale-development research; the dwell-time-to-spending link comes from a single café study with correlational structure, and the broader program is concentrated in a limited set of categories and countries. Objective air measurement relied on one indicator, which may understate what sensors can capture.

### 4. Loyalty Members Write Glowing Reviews — and Less Useful Ones

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Behaviour* | **Published:** 2026-10-26
**Work ID:** W7223652730 | **DOI:** 10.1002/cb.70265

**The study:** Researchers analyzed 153,525 product reviews from the Chinese e-commerce platform JD.com, comparing reviews written by paid membership-program customers with those from non-members, and ran two follow-up lab experiments. Member reviews carried higher star ratings, more emotional intensity and more concrete language — all of which were associated with lower helpfulness votes. Separately, a visible membership badge raised perceived reviewer expertise and made the same review seem more helpful, but that credibility boost was outweighed by the content effect, so member reviews were on net rated less helpful.

**Why it matters:** Membership programs are sold as retention engines, but they appear to distort the review ecosystem that other shoppers depend on — tilting content toward enthusiastic, low-diagnostic praise.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Platform and CRM teams should audit whether their loyalty tiers are inflating ratings and hollowing out review usefulness, and test design fixes: badge display as a credibility cue, prompts that push members toward comparative or trade-off detail, and separating member from non-member review feeds.
- **For general readers and consumers:** When you scan reviews before buying, the five-star rave from a badged "premium member" may tell you less than a plainer review from an ordinary buyer — the people most invested in a platform tend to be its most enthusiastic and least discriminating critics.

**Caveats:** Field evidence is correlational and from a single platform in one country (JD.com); membership is self-selected, so happier customers may both join and rate higher. Supporting experiments were lab-based.

### 5. Bank Branches Earn Their Keep as Reassurance, Not Technology Showrooms

**Journal:** *International Journal of Bank Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-26
**Work ID:** W7223672553 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ijbm-08-2025-0678

**The study:** A three-part study of retail banking combined focus groups (n=20), scenario-based experiments with 298 customers comparing conventional versus technology-enhanced branches, and topic modeling plus machine learning on 363,184 Google reviews of bank branches. Staff competence and human interaction (SOCIALIZE), professional atmospherics (SURROUND) and local embeddedness (SITUATE) consistently drove loyalty and word of mouth; innovation-heavy branch experiences added little beyond a functional baseline. In naturally occurring reviews, the human-interaction dimension was the strongest predictor of star ratings.

**Why it matters:** Banks closing or "reimagining" branches as lifestyle and tech spaces may be spending against the dimensions customers care least about, while the costly, visible physical branch itself functions as a trust signal.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Before the next branch refit, test whether coffee bars, tablets and interactive displays move loyalty at all relative to investment in staff expertise and a credible, professional-looking space — and consider evaluating branches on reassurance and referral metrics, not just transactions per square foot.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The reason a physical branch still reassures people has little to do with what happens inside it: an expensive building on a local high street signals a bank that is not going anywhere, which is why competence at the counter beats gadgets in the lobby.

**Caveats:** Experimental component used hypothetical branch scenarios rather than real banking behavior, with moderate sample size; Google review analysis is observational and reviews skew toward extreme experiences. Dimension labels come from a proprietary experience framework.

### 6. "Would You Like to Donate?" Works Better When the Charity Is Local

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-10-27
**Work ID:** W7223898707 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105187

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 356 U.S. consumers about checkout charity campaigns and modeled responses to retailer partnerships with local versus global charities using partial least squares structural equation modeling. Local charity partnerships were associated with stronger perceived social connection to beneficiaries and greater perceived donation impact, which in turn predicted higher donation likelihood, checkout satisfaction and attitude toward the retailer. The effects were stronger among consumers with a strong local identity.

**Why it matters:** Checkout charity asks can irritate shoppers and dent store evaluations; the locality of the beneficiary appears to be a cheap lever that improves outcomes for both the cause and the retailer.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Retailers running national charity tie-ins could test regionalized or store-level beneficiaries against a single global partner, measuring not only donation take-up but checkout satisfaction and store attitude — the finding implies the ask itself feels less intrusive when the cause is nearby.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Most people say they want to help the greatest need, yet psychological distance wins at the register: a cause you can picture down the road feels more impactful than one halfway around the world, even when the opposite is true.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey of a single U.S. sample with no observed donation behavior — intentions and evaluations only, and causation cannot be established. Results rest on significant paths in a structural model.

### 7. For Gen Z, Talking to the Shopping App Beats Typing — Especially for Treats

**Journal:** *International Journal of Retail & Distribution Management* | **Published:** 2026-10-26
**Work ID:** W7223671637 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ijrdm-07-2025-0504

**The study:** Two laboratory experiments with Generation Z consumers compared voice and text interfaces for low-involvement online purchases, analyzed with PLS-SEM. Voice interfaces produced significantly higher perceived convenience and enjoyment, and therefore greater perceived value, which fed through to search satisfaction and purchase intention. Product type mattered: the link from perceived value to satisfaction was stronger for hedonic items than for utilitarian ones in voice-enabled shopping.

**Why it matters:** Voice commerce has underdelivered against years of hype; this points to where it may actually pay off — young shoppers, routine low-stakes baskets, and pleasure purchases rather than functional ones.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Quick-commerce and e-retail teams could run live A/B tests of voice search against text for repeat and impulse categories with younger cohorts, rather than rolling voice across the whole catalog — and watch whether the enjoyment advantage survives outside a lab, where background noise and privacy concerns bite.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If you find yourself happily reordering snacks by voice but switching back to typing when buying a kettle, that is the pattern: speaking is faster and more fun, but people still want to read and compare when the decision has consequences.

**Caveats:** Two lab experiments with Gen Z participants; sample sizes are not reported in the abstract, outcomes are purchase intentions rather than actual purchases, and findings are limited to low-involvement categories in a single research context.

### 8. Smart Shopping Carts Don't Empower Everyone — and Can Tire People Out

**Journal:** *International Journal of Retail & Distribution Management* | **Published:** 2026-10-23
**Work ID:** W7223273403 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ijrdm-09-2025-0755

**The study:** A three-study program on smart-retail technology in DIY stores surveyed 309 Chinese customers (Study 1), replicated with 273 Australian customers with multigroup comparison (Study 2), and ran a 2x2 vignette experiment manipulating empowerment and trust cues in both countries (Study 3). The sense of empowerment that smart carts and in-store tech are supposed to create was weaker in China and showed signs of decision fatigue in Australia; trust appeared to substitute for empowerment in the Chinese sample, while "flow" — absorption in the shopping task — worked consistently in both markets.

**Why it matters:** Retailers deploying smart carts, scanners and in-aisle apps typically justify them on customer control and self-service; the evidence suggests control may not be the mechanism that drives satisfaction and loyalty, and may backfire in high-choice categories.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Test the framing of in-store tech, not just the functionality: design that creates smooth, absorbing journeys may outperform design that piles choices and data onto the shopper, and the right emphasis — trust cues versus control cues — may differ by market.
- **For general readers and consumers:** More information and more control in the aisle is not automatically better. In a category as choice-heavy as DIY, a cart that tells you everything can leave you more exhausted than empowered.

**Caveats:** Two cross-sectional surveys (causation cannot be established) plus a hypothetical vignette experiment rather than real store behavior; limited to DIY retail in two countries. The authors themselves flag the counterintuitive results as needing replication.

### 9. The teaser ad that only works if people finish it

**Journal:** *Journal of Interactive Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-22
**Work ID:** W7223280551 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10949968261503210

**The study:** In a randomized field experiment with a book retailer, more than 109,000 customers were assigned either to an interactive "blind lead-in" display ad that withheld the product until users clicked through a multi-stage sequence, or to a static ad that showed the product immediately. On average, the teaser format produced no lift in purchase conversion, because most people never reached the reveal. But using random assignment as an instrument, customers who actually completed the sequence showed a 46.6% lift in long-term purchase conversion — roughly 437 completions per incremental purchase. Completion itself depended on creative design: early informational cues and social cues placed before the final stage raised completion, while visual and textual complexity late in the sequence dropped it.

**Why it matters:** Interactive ad formats are often sold on engagement metrics, but this shows the engagement only pays off if users finish the journey — and that average conversion can look flat while a real effect hides inside the completers.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit your interactive ad formats for completion rate, not just impressions or clicks, and test whether moving informational and social cues earlier in the sequence lifts completion; this is real field evidence from one retailer, so the economics (437 completions per incremental sale) need to be recalculated for your own margins before scaling.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Those ads that hide what they are selling until you tap through are a deliberate design; most people bail before the reveal, and the small minority who play along are measurably more likely to buy.

**Caveats:** Single firm (a book retailer) and a single category; the completion-based effect is estimated among compliers via instrumental variables rather than directly randomized; the supplementary mechanism study is separate from the field data.

### 10. Political signals in a store make customers more uncomfortable than rainbow flags or religious symbols

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-10-14
**Work ID:** W7221815419 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105160

**The study:** Three preregistered scenario experiments placed participants in a coffee shop whose staff and physical environment displayed ideological cues — political, religious, LGBTQ+, environmental or lifestyle. Political cues generated more discomfort than environmental and lifestyle cues, though not significantly more than religious or LGBTQ+ cues. When matching political cues appeared on both employees and the store environment, discomfort rose further and stated store choice fell, with discomfort statistically accounting for part of the link to avoidance.

**Why it matters:** Retailers and service brands are increasingly asked to display values, and this suggests the specific content and the number of touchpoints carrying it both shape customer reaction — political signaling appears to be the riskiest category and stacking it across touchpoints compounds the effect.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Treat values signaling as a servicescape design decision with a dose-response element: test whether concentrating a message on one touchpoint versus saturating staff and store changes comfort and visit intent among your actual customer base — noting these are hypothetical coffee shop scenarios, not till receipts.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Shoppers do read the pins, posters and flags in a cafe as statements about who belongs there, and the research suggests the discomfort comes less from disagreement itself than from feeling surrounded by the message.

**Caveats:** Scenario-based experiments with stated intentions rather than observed purchases, as the authors explicitly note; single service category (coffee shops); cue content is likely to be culturally specific and the sample's political composition shapes what "political" means here.

### 11. A smiley face on the packaging makes dieters think the food is more fattening

**Journal:** *Psychology and Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-16
**Work ID:** W7222498555 | **DOI:** 10.1002/mar.70279

**The study:** Across five studies, researchers tested how smile cues — smiling faces in food marketing and packaging — affect consumers' calorie estimates. For indulgent foods, or foods that could be framed as indulgent, smile cues raised perceived indulgence, which in turn raised calorie estimates, and this effect was strongest among consumers high in dietary restraint or whose restraint had been situationally activated. When restraint was activated, participants were less likely to choose the smile-cued food.

**Why it matters:** A cheerful, friendly design element that is supposed to make food more appealing can backfire with exactly the health-conscious segment many brands are courting, by making the product read as a guilty pleasure.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you market to health-minded or calorie-tracking shoppers, run a packaging test in which the only variable is the smiling face or cheerful mascot — it may be inflating perceived calories and suppressing choice in that segment while helping with indulgence-seeking buyers.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Your calorie guesses are being nudged by graphics, not nutrition: the same snack can feel heavier simply because the wrapper looks happy, and people on diets are the most susceptible.

**Caveats:** Experimental studies, likely online panels, with perceptions and choice measures rather than real retail purchases; effect is specific to foods that can be construed as indulgent; sample sizes and country not specified in the abstract.

### 12. People invest less money when they are in a crowd

**Journal:** *Psychology and Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-14
**Work ID:** W7221805592 | **DOI:** 10.1002/mar.70286

**The study:** Three studies examined how the physical environment around a person using a mobile trading app affects their investing. Crowded surroundings were associated with both a lower likelihood of investing and smaller amounts invested, with the effect running through reduced feelings of pleasure; feelings of dominance additionally shaped the amount invested.

**Why it matters:** Neo-brokers have moved investing onto phones used on trains, in cafes and in offices, meaning the ambient environment — not just the interface — may now be a variable in financial decisions and conversion rates.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For app-based financial and high-consideration purchases, consider context-aware, privacy-conscious design — the implication worth testing is whether reducing felt exposure (screen privacy, simplified flows, resumable sessions) recovers conversions among users transacting in public, while being careful not to manipulate people into larger trades.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If you have ever felt oddly reluctant to hit "buy" on your phone in a packed train carriage, that instinct is measurable — crowding dampens the pleasure of the decision and shrinks the sums people are willing to commit.

**Caveats:** Crowding was likely manipulated via imagined or depicted scenarios rather than observed in real trading environments; investment amounts are stated rather than actual money at risk in at least some studies; sample composition and country not specified in the abstract.

### 13. Webrooming wins the next sale; online-to-offline wins the relationship

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-10-05
**Work ID:** W7220357245 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105147

**The study:** Researchers ran a quasi-experiment in an omnichannel sports retail setting with 166 sports-product consumers, comparing how different channel configurations (such as webrooming — researching online, buying in store — and online-to-offline journeys) affected repurchase intention, satisfaction and brand loyalty. Outcomes differed sharply by strategy (partial eta-squared of 0.45–0.52): webrooming scored highest for repurchase intention (M=4.94), while the online-to-offline configuration produced the highest satisfaction and loyalty (both M=4.96). Customer experience and brand trust moderated every effect, and the gaps between strategies were widest among shoppers with less experience and lower trust. Results held after post-stratification weighting.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the channel mix a retailer pushes should depend on the goal: digital research paths drive the next transaction, while integrated physical touchpoints do the heavier work of building satisfaction and loyalty.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Retailers running a single omnichannel playbook may be optimizing for the wrong outcome. The pattern here is worth testing in your own data: route high-intent, already-loyal shoppers through efficient webrooming flows, but steer new or low-trust customers into journeys that end in a store or physical touchpoint, where the study found the biggest relational payoff.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For shoppers, this maps a familiar habit — scouting online then buying in person — onto what retailers get out of it, and hints that the in-store leg of a purchase is what actually builds attachment to a brand rather than just completing a transaction.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=166) spread across multiple channel conditions; quasi-experimental rather than fully randomized; outcomes are self-reported intentions and attitudes, not observed repurchases; limited to sports products and a single retail context.

### 14. Push notifications work — until they don't: the point where reminders start backfiring

**Journal:** *Information Systems Research* | **Published:** 2026-10-23
**Work ID:** W7223280000 | **DOI:** 10.1287/isre.2024.0917

**The study:** Researchers analyzed more than 200 push campaigns and 60 million user-campaign observations from a leading U.S. mobile-offer marketplace, pairing the observational data with controlled experiments on the same platform. Push notifications sharply lifted same-day click-throughs to the advertised merchant, with results varying by merchant type, user type, message wording and send timing. Notifications also lifted engagement with merchants that weren't advertised, inside and outside the promoted category — the nudge redirected attention across the whole marketplace. But repeated exposure produced diminishing returns, and at high frequencies engagement actually fell.

**Why it matters:** Push is one of the few attention levers a retailer or platform owns outright, and this is rare large-scale evidence on both its spillover benefits and its tipping point into fatigue.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** The halo finding is the commercial story: a push promoting one merchant appears to warm up traffic to others, which means notification value should be measured at portfolio level, not just by the advertised partner's click-through. Equally important, test your own saturation curve — the study suggests there is a frequency beyond which each extra message costs engagement rather than buying it.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Those phone buzzes do two different jobs: some genuinely inform you about an offer, others simply remind you the app exists. Knowing which is which — and noticing when a brand has crossed from helpful into noise — is a decent rule for deciding whose notifications to switch off.

**Caveats:** Single platform (a U.S. mobile-offer marketplace) and single country; observational analysis plus platform experiments, so results may not generalize to other app categories; same-day click-through is a short measurement window.

### 15. Consumers are barely better than a coin flip at spotting a dishonest ad claim

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing Communications* | **Published:** 2026-10-22
**Work ID:** W7223392067 | **DOI:** 10.1080/13527266.2026.2751068

**The study:** In a large randomized experiment, participants judged marketing claims as honest or deceptive. They defaulted to believing: claims were judged honest 56.4% of the time, and overall accuracy at separating truth from lies was just 58.9%, with better accuracy on truthful claims than deceptive ones. Two marketing-specific factors — the presence of a brand name and the belief that a claim could be verified before purchase — both increased this truth bias and shifted detection accuracy.

**Why it matters:** The regulatory and industry assumption that an alert consumer can police deceptive advertising doesn't hold up; branding and the mere feeling that a claim is checkable make people more credulous, not less.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If audiences accept claims by default, the risk calculus around aggressive copy changes: the market will not catch you, but regulators and class actions might, and brand presence amplifies the trust you are borrowing. Worth pressure-testing whether your substantiation standards match the credulity your brand actually commands rather than the skepticism marketers assume.
- **For general readers and consumers:** "Buyer beware" is poor consumer advice, because people believe claims by default and believe them even more when a familiar brand is attached or when they assume they could check later — a check most shoppers never actually make.

**Caveats:** Experimental judgments of claims rather than real purchase decisions; sample composition and recruitment source are not specified in the abstract.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- The review ecosystem keeps turning out to be shaped by who is writing, not just what is written. "Loyalty Members Write Glowing Reviews — and Less Useful Ones" (W7223652730) lands squarely alongside the previous digest's "AI review summaries didn't silence shoppers — they wrote more reviews, and better ones" (W7220470690): both show platform design choices quietly changing the diagnosticity of user content, in opposite directions.
- Badges as credibility cues recur. The visible membership badge in W7223652730 raised perceived reviewer expertise, echoing the attention-and-selection effect of the platform verification badge in "A little blue check moved eyeballs — and choices" (W7221510551) — and in both cases the badge carries no quality information of its own.
- Technology-forward customer experience keeps underdelivering against the plainer alternative. "Bank Branches Earn Their Keep as Reassurance, Not Technology Showrooms" (W7223672553) and "Smart Shopping Carts Don't Empower Everyone" (W7223273403) extend the pattern set by "Simple beats smart: basic rule-based chatbots outperformed LLM bots" (W7214950393) and the modest, conditional avatar lift in "Giving your shopping assistant a face" (W7215066966).
- Channel investments substitute rather than compound. "Webrooming wins the next sale; online-to-offline wins the relationship" (W7220357245) sits naturally beside "On B2B platforms, a strong online reputation and a strong trade-show presence cancel each other out" (W7220830415): different channel combinations produce different outcomes, and more is not additive.

**Advances**
- Spillover measurement gets harder evidence. Last run's "paid search carried e-commerce revenue" (W7220946924) argued that one channel's spend feeds another's credit. This run pushes the same idea across firms: "Mobile coupons work harder before shoppers reach the mall than inside it" (W7223707755) shows coupons generating traffic that neighboring tenants capture, and "Push notifications work — until they don't" (W7223280000) shows a push for one merchant lifting engagement with unadvertised merchants across a marketplace.
- Engagement-versus-sales divergence moves from observational to randomized. "In live shopping, the host's mood moves sales — and the sidekick may be costing you money" (W7220454801) flagged that engagement and revenue can pull apart; "The teaser ad that only works if people finish it" (W7223280551) is a 109,000-customer field experiment showing a flat average conversion result concealing a 46.6% lift among completers.
- Consumer vulnerability gets a number. The conceptual case in "Scams are now a marketing problem" (W7211892755) rested on persuasion knowledge and vulnerability theory; "Consumers are barely better than a coin flip at spotting a dishonest ad claim" (W7223392067) supplies the empirical floor — 58.9% accuracy, with brand presence increasing credulity rather than skepticism.

**Counterpoints**
- On who should do the protecting: W7211892755 argued for consumer empowerment as a complement to regulation. "The shoppers who use cross-border platforms most are the ones who most oppose regulating them" (W7223071223) suggests the people with the most platform experience actively resist protective rules, which complicates any policy built on the assumption that informed users welcome guardrails.
- On friction: "Tell shoppers they're building a collection" (W7221306245) found that effortful search can add value, while "Smart Shopping Carts Don't Empower Everyone" (W7223273403) found piling information and control onto DIY shoppers produced fatigue, with absorption ("flow") working better than empowerment. Together they sharpen the distinction between pleasurable effort and cognitive load.

**New themes**

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Five studies in this batch find that the environment surrounding a shopper — not the product, price or interface — is doing measurable work on how long they stay and how much they commit, and that retailers are measuring it with the wrong instruments. Perceived air quality predicted café dwell time and spend while the actual sensor reading predicted neither (W7222049986); crowded physical surroundings cut both the likelihood and the size of mobile investments (W7221805592); political cues stacked across staff and store raised discomfort and lowered store choice (W7221815419); smart carts produced fatigue rather than empowerment in DIY aisles (W7223273403); and bank branches earned loyalty through staff competence and a credible professional space, not interactive displays (W7223672553).

**Why pitch this now:** Banks and chains are mid-cycle on branch closures and "experiential" refits justified by footfall and transactions per square foot, and at the same time phone-based buying and trading have moved high-stakes decisions into train carriages and coffee shops — so ambient context is now a variable in channels that were designed as if it weren't.

**Angle:** A reported feature on atmospherics as an underinstrumented P&L line: what retail and facilities teams currently measure (sensor compliance numbers, feature checklists, square-foot productivity) versus what appears to move dwell, basket size and loyalty — written for retail, banking and marketing decision-makers planning store and branch capital spend, with the honest caveat that much of this evidence is correlational or scenario-based and needs replication in live estates.

**Potential headlines:**
- What stores measure about their own environment, and what actually moves spending
- The air in your store beats the gadgets in it
- Your next refit budget is probably funding the wrong half of the experience

**Potential outlets:**
- **Retail Dive** — store-format capital spending and experiential refits are its operational beat, and this gives a measurement-based challenge to both.
- **American Banker** — the branch study speaks directly to its audience's live argument about whether to close, shrink or "reimagine" branches, and the reassurance framing is a fresh case for keeping them.
- **Fast Company** — design-led readership that will run the sensory and servicescape material, including the political-cue dose-response finding.
- **MIT Sloan Management Review** — built for the gap between what managers instrument and what the evidence shows, and tolerant of the correlational caveats this batch requires.

- **The shoppers who use cross-border platforms most are the ones who most oppose regulating them (Work ID W7223071223):** Why a safety-framed e-commerce rule collapsed in days, and what it says about the political constituency platforms can mobilize, for a policy-and-commerce feature or a business news analysis section.
