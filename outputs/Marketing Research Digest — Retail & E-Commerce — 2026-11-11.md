### 1. Boomers, Gen Z and online reviews: the generation gap barely exists

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-11-10
**Work ID:** W7225751353 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105208

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 1,028 Czech online panelists spanning Baby Boomers, Gen X, Millennials and Gen Z, asking each to rate purchase intentions after reading ten fictitious product reviews built around classic decision biases — anchoring, attribute emphasis, positive framing, review recency and hindsight cues. A handful of cohort differences reached nominal significance (mostly Boomers versus Gen Y and Z on positive anchoring and design-focused reviews), but every effect size was vanishingly small (η²H = 0.005–0.009), and the positive-framing difference disappeared after correcting for multiple comparisons. The authors' own conclusion: generational membership tells you almost nothing about how someone responds to review cues.

**Why it matters:** Generation-based segmentation is a staple of retail and e-commerce strategy, yet this study finds no basis for building separate review interfaces or persuasion tactics for Boomers versus Gen Z. Budget spent on age-cohort targeting of review presentation may be better spent on cues that move everyone.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit where your team assumes a "Gen Z shops differently" premise and ask what evidence supports it. This study suggests review-interface personalization by birth cohort is unlikely to pay off; test behavioral or need-state segments against age cohorts in a live A/B experiment before committing design resources.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The stereotype that older shoppers are more gullible about online reviews — or that younger ones are savvier — gets little support here. Anchoring on a high first number, glowing framing and design-focused praise nudge people of all ages in broadly similar ways.

**Caveats:** Online panel with hypothetical purchase-intention measures rather than real purchases; single country (Czech Republic) quota sample; stimuli were fictitious reviews and the attribute-emphasis conditions confounded information content with framing, so no clean causal mechanism; the authors themselves describe it as exploratory descriptive evidence.

### 2. Head office calls it "augmentation." Store staff hear "replacement."

**Journal:** *Journal of Service Research* | **Published:** 2026-11-03
**Work ID:** W7225231563 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10946705261498173

**The study:** Researchers conducted 52 expert interviews across a retail hierarchy — 27 headquarters managers, 11 store managers and 14 frontline employees — to understand how customer-facing service digitalization is understood at each level. The framing gap was systematic: HQ described digital service as an ongoing journey that augments human staff, while frontline employees described the same initiatives as a fixed destination that substitutes for them. Store managers sat in between as translators who could either reinforce or defuse the mismatch, and the study identifies four alignment mechanisms (including psychological safety, deliberate redundancies and nudging) that buffer the damage to service interaction quality.

**Why it matters:** Retailers routinely blame failed self-service and digital service rollouts on technology or customers, when the breakdown may start with how differently stores and head office interpret the same project. Diagnosing that gap before launch is cheaper than relaunching.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Before the next self-checkout, clienteling app or in-store AI rollout, survey what frontline staff believe the project is for — and whether they think it ends with them. Practical levers worth testing: HQ staff working store days, temporary staffing redundancies during transition, and formally resourcing store managers as translators rather than just enforcers.
- **For general readers and consumers:** When the in-store tech feels half-hearted or staff seem reluctant to help you use it, the problem is often not laziness but a workforce that reads the same device as a notice of its own obsolescence.

**Caveats:** Qualitative interview study with no quantitative outcome measures and no causal test of the proposed alignment mechanisms; participant firms and countries are not specified in the abstract; findings are retail-specific and reflect expert accounts rather than observed customer service outcomes.

### 3. Get the omnichannel journey right and satisfaction with the whole retailer is still higher a year later

**Journal:** *International Journal of Retail & Distribution Management* | **Published:** 2026-11-02
**Work ID:** W7225122614 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ijrdm-09-2025-0731

**The study:** Combining a survey of 420 shoppers with omnichannel experience, a follow-up measurement one year later, and a supplementary experiment with 80 participants, researchers tested a chain running from a retailer's omnichannel capability through three experience outcomes — fulfillment, fluency (how seamless the journey feels) and flexibility (switching channels at will) — to satisfaction with the specific journey. Satisfaction with a single journey at the first time point predicted broader satisfaction with the retailer as a whole a year later.

**Why it matters:** It supports treating channel integration as one organizational capability with measurable downstream payoff, rather than a set of separate channel projects, and suggests journey-level satisfaction scores have predictive value for brand-level standing.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Consider instrumenting journey-level satisfaction (fulfillment, fluency, flexibility) as a leading indicator rather than relying on annual retailer-level satisfaction tracking — but validate the one-year link in your own customer base before using it in forecasting or incentives.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A single frustrating click-and-collect or returns experience may color how you feel about a retailer long after you have forgotten the specific order.

**Caveats:** Primarily cross-sectional survey with structural equation modeling, so causal direction is inferred rather than established; the supplementary experiment is small (N=80 total); country and retail category not specified in the abstract; self-reported satisfaction rather than observed spending or retention.

### 4. Subsidize pickup, not delivery: airport e-commerce data shows where discounts actually pay off

**Journal:** *Internet Research* | **Published:** 2026-10-30
**Work ID:** W7224921670 | **DOI:** 10.1108/intr-09-2025-1430

**The study:** Researchers analyzed six months of transaction data from the online shopping channel of a major international airport in China, building an unbalanced panel of 88 stores and estimating fixed-effects models of how sales responded to subsidy intensity across three fulfillment modes. Channel subsidies lifted online sales overall, but the effect was strongest for in-store pickup, next strongest for instant delivery, and weakest for home delivery. Dining outlets responded more strongly than retail stores for pickup and instant delivery, and spatial spillovers between stores were statistically insignificant.

**Why it matters:** Promotional budgets in omnichannel retail are usually set at the channel level, but this field evidence suggests the same discount buys very different sales depending on how the order is fulfilled.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Retail and marketplace operators could break subsidy performance out by fulfillment mode rather than treating online promotion as one line item, and test whether shifting spend toward pickup and fast local delivery produces more incremental sales; the paper also suggests reducing fulfillment uncertainty by displaying preparation times, store congestion and historical delivery times.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For travelers and shoppers, it shows discounts are not neutral: platforms price-steer you toward the fulfillment method that is cheapest and fastest for them, which is often walking to the counter yourself.

**Caveats:** Single platform, single airport in China, 88 stores over six months; a time- and space-constrained captive-audience setting that may not generalize to mainstream e-commerce; observational panel data rather than a randomized subsidy experiment.

### 5. Seven years of survey waves suggest shoppers who notice a retailer's inclusion efforts trust it more

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-11-09
**Work ID:** W7225631783 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105203

**The study:** Using a repeated cross-sectional survey fielded annually across seven waves from 2019 to 2025, researchers tested whether consumer awareness of retailers' diversity, equity, inclusion and accessibility (DEIA) initiatives was linked to trust and loyalty. Awareness was positively associated with trust in every annual sample, and trust was strongly associated with loyalty throughout, though the size of the awareness effect varied by year and by the form of diversity in question. The authors interpret DEIA as a market signal whose effects reach beyond the groups the initiatives target.

**Why it matters:** As many retailers quietly retreat from DEI commitments, this gives a longitudinal counterpoint: the trust pathway held up across seven years of shifting political climate, but only among consumers who were actually aware of the initiatives.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Visibility is the variable: the association runs through awareness, so brands maintaining DEIA programs may need to embed them in the customer experience rather than in corporate reports, and the authors warn that different forms of diversity are not interchangeable in consumer response.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For shoppers, it is a reminder that trust in a store is built out of small signals you may barely register consciously, from accessible store layouts to who is visible on staff.

**Caveats:** Repeated cross-sectional survey, not a panel, so it cannot establish causation and cannot track individuals over time; self-reported awareness, trust and loyalty rather than observed spending; country and retailer context not fully specified in the abstract.

### 6. Vague DEI statements win shoppers over directly; specific ones work through a different route

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing Management* | **Published:** 2026-11-04
**Work ID:** W7221486834 | **DOI:** 10.1080/0267257x.2026.2757003

**The study:** Across two experiments, researchers tested how consumers react to retailers' diversity, equity and inclusion statements that varied in specificity. Unspecific DEI statements produced a directly positive consumer response, whereas specific statements worked indirectly, by reducing the psychological distance consumers felt and lowering dissociative concerns about being linked with a group they do not identify with.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the wording choice retailers agonize over is not a simple safe-versus-bold trade-off: specificity can still pay off, but only if the messaging first closes the perceived distance between the shopper and the group being named.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Worth testing in live creative: pair specific DEI commitments with cues that reduce psychological distance, and compare against broad inclusive language, rather than assuming named-group messaging will alienate the general customer base.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The finding exposes a quiet piece of shopper psychology: people often respond less to the cause itself than to whether it makes them feel close to, or set apart from, the people it is about.

**Caveats:** Two scenario-based experiments measuring responses to statements rather than real shopping behavior; sample sizes, country and recruitment source are not reported in the abstract.

### 7. "You must buy this": pushy livestream sellers work better when viewers feel ignored

**Journal:** *Journal of Research in Interactive Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-28
**Work ID:** W7224102790 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jrim-03-2026-0146

**The study:** Five studies, including three using a 2 (assertive vs. non-assertive language) by continuous (perceived interactivity) between-subjects design plus a fully factorial fourth study and a boundary-condition web study, tested how livestream shoppers react to commanding sales language. When viewers perceived low interactivity with the streamer, assertive language increased trust in the product and downstream purchase responses; when interactivity was high, the advantage disappeared. Trust in the product mediated the effect, and time pressure acted as a theory-driven boundary condition.

**Why it matters:** Livestream selling is a large and fast-growing e-commerce format, and this suggests the familiar hard-sell script is not universally effective but contingent on how connected viewers feel to the host.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Streamers and the brands hiring them could match tone to room size and engagement level, testing assertive scripts in large, low-interaction broadcasts and softer language when hosts are actively responding to comments.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For viewers, it is a useful self-check: commanding language is most persuasive precisely when you are a passive member of a big audience, which is exactly when you have least ability to question the claim.

**Caveats:** All five studies are experiments, apparently online, using simulated livestream stimuli rather than real purchases; sample sizes and country not stated in the abstract; stated responses rather than observed sales.

### 8. Not all small brands are small in the same way: what wine data says about stocking the long tail

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing Management* | **Published:** 2026-11-10
**Work ID:** W7225744799 | **DOI:** 10.1080/0267257x.2026.2760838

**The study:** Researchers linked Wine Enthusiast critic review data with US household panel purchase data covering roughly 80 winery brands per year from 2017 to 2021, then applied the established NBD-Dirichlet classification framework to sort brands into filler, niche, and change-of-pace roles based on penetration and purchase frequency. Brands that got reviewed tended to sit in more peripheral market positions and were bought disproportionately by heavy category buyers. Critically, review activity itself showed no association with stronger market share.

**Why it matters:** Retailers and category managers face a crowded tail of small brands and little guidance on which to stock; the finding that critical acclaim does not translate into share, and that peripheral brands play distinct behavioral roles, gives buyers a cleaner basis for assortment decisions than press coverage.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For category managers: treat critic reviews as a signal of who a brand appeals to (heavy, engaged buyers) rather than as a predictor of volume, and consider classifying tail brands by purchase-behavior role — filler, niche, change-of-pace — before culling or expanding the assortment. Worth replicating in your own category with your own panel or loyalty data before rewriting planograms.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For shoppers: the wine that scored 92 points is not necessarily the wine most people are buying. Critical praise clusters around brands that serve enthusiastic, high-frequency buyers looking for variety — which is a different thing from a brand being broadly popular or widely available.

**Caveats:** Single category (US wine) and a single review source; about 80 brands per year over five years; observational panel data, so the link between reviews and market position is associational, not causal.

### 9. When a traditional B2B firm launches a marketplace, two very different futures follow

**Journal:** *Journal of Product Innovation Management* | **Published:** 2026-11-01
**Work ID:** W7225100025 | **DOI:** 10.1111/jpim.70063

**The study:** Researchers conducted a comparative case study of five marketplace platforms launched by established business-to-business firms in the European industrial products industry, examining how managers interpreted and executed the shift from a conventional pipeline model. They found two divergent pathways: "pipeline-enhancing" transitions, where managers saw the platform as a way to strengthen the existing business and ended up with interconnected models, and "pipeline-transcending" transitions, where managers used consumer marketplaces like Amazon as their mental reference point, creating tensions that resolved into two separate, independent businesses. Which path unfolded depended largely on which customer segments the firm served.

**Why it matters:** Incumbents across industrial and wholesale sectors are launching marketplaces without a clear view of whether the platform will feed the core business or compete with it; this work shows the outcome is substantially shaped by how leadership frames the opportunity at the outset.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For B2B leaders planning a marketplace: audit the reference point your team is using. If executives are benchmarking against consumer marketplaces, expect cognitive tension with the legacy sales model and plan for organizational separation rather than assuming integration will work. The customer segment you serve is an early predictor of which path you are on.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Limited direct relevance for consumers, though it helps explain why some manufacturers' online storefronts now stock rivals' products while others stay strictly own-brand.

**Caveats:** Five qualitative cases in one industry and one region (European industrial products); no quantitative outcome measures, so the framework is descriptive rather than predictive of financial performance.

### 10. How you announce your relationship online may shape what you buy next

**Journal:** *Psychology and Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-11-10
**Work ID:** W7225714650 | **DOI:** 10.1002/mar.70305

**The study:** Across four experiments, researchers tested whether the way people disclose a romantic relationship on social media — a "soft launch" (subtle, partially obscured) versus a "hard launch" (explicit announcement) — spills over into consumption preferences. Soft launches increased preference for minimalist products, apparently because they activate a broader preference for subtle self-presentation. The effect strengthened among people with higher relationship privacy orientation and disappeared when a marketplace urgency cue was present.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that a consumer's self-presentation mindset, primed by something as incidental as a social media post, can carry over into aesthetic and product choices — a potentially targetable state rather than a fixed trait.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For brands in fashion, beauty, home and jewelry: minimalist versus maximalist appeals may land differently depending on the self-presentation mode a consumer is in. The finding that an urgency cue wiped out the effect is the most actionable detail — scarcity messaging appears to override subtle-aesthetic preferences, which is testable in live creative and promotional experiments.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A reminder of how porous our choices are: the register in which you present yourself online — understated or declarative — appears to bleed into what you find appealing in a shop window shortly afterward.

**Caveats:** Four experiments with likely online panel participants; preferences and scenarios rather than observed purchases; sample sizes and nationality not stated in the abstract; effect sizes and durability beyond the lab unknown.

### 11. China's ESG gap: firms follow the public on social issues and ignore regulators on the environment

**Journal:** *The International Review of Retail Distribution and Consumer Research* | **Published:** 2026-10-29
**Work ID:** W7224526178 | **DOI:** 10.1080/09593969.2026.2749003

**The study:** Researchers built a "structural deviation index" measuring the distance between three things: what Chinese policy documents emphasize, what the public actually searches for (using Baidu Index data), and what companies actually do. They applied it to ESG ratings for 3,030 listed firms from 2019 to 2023, across the environmental, social and governance dimensions separately. The Policy–Corporate gap was consistently the widest, and the environmental dimension showed the greatest misalignment despite being the loudest policy priority — while firms tracked public attention much more closely on social issues.

**Why it matters:** It gives a data-backed picture of where corporate sustainability claims are substantive and where they are symbolic: companies appear to pick the cheapest legitimacy available, favoring governance tweaks and visible social initiatives over costly environmental transformation.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For retail and consumer brands operating in or sourcing from China, the finding is a warning about the credibility of partners' ESG scores — and an opportunity. If most firms are concentrating effort where public attention is, brands that invest in the under-served environmental dimension have a differentiation claim few competitors can match. Worth auditing whether your own ESG communications track search-driven public concern rather than actual impact.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Public attention appears to function as a real governance force: the issues people search for are the issues companies move on. That suggests consumer concern is not wasted noise, but it also means the problems nobody is searching about — often the environmental ones — quietly go unaddressed.

**Caveats:** Single country (China) and a single five-year window; relies on third-party ESG ratings and Baidu search volume as proxies for corporate performance and public concern, both imperfect; observational and correlational — it documents gaps rather than proving why firms behave this way.

### 12. Household mess isn't a failure — researchers find families use clutter as a working tool

**Journal:** *Journal of Business Research* | **Published:** 2026-10-16
**Work ID:** W7222556775 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jbusres.2026.116581

**The study:** Researchers conducted photo-elicitation interviews with 22 households, asking family members to document and discuss order and disorder in their own homes. They found that mess is not a static condition or a personal shortcoming but an emergent state produced by the interplay of people, objects, spaces and daily rhythms — and identified three recurring types (transient, functional and conducive disorder) along with three ways families handle them: restoring, tolerating and concealing.

**Why it matters:** Much of the home-organization and storage industry sells against mess as a problem to be eliminated, but this evidence suggests some disorder is actively useful — supporting household flow, efficiency and family harmony — which reframes what "good" storage and organizing products should actually do.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For home, storage and organization retailers: there may be a product and messaging gap around designing for mess rather than against it — flexible storage, designated "functional mess zones," and concealment solutions that let families tolerate disorder without shame. Guilt-based marketing ("get organized") may be addressing a need customers don't actually have in parts of their homes.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The pile on the kitchen counter may be doing a job. The study suggests families often leave things out because it keeps the household running, and that the tidiest home isn't necessarily the best-functioning one — useful permission for anyone who feels judged by organizing content online.

**Caveats:** Small qualitative sample (22 households) in a single study; interpretive rather than statistical, so findings cannot be generalized to populations or quantified; no purchase behavior observed, and practical retail implications are the authors' inference rather than tested outcomes.

### 13. Why people rent luxury handbags: status in Australia, sustainability in China

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Behaviour* | **Published:** 2026-10-19
**Work ID:** W7223000149 | **DOI:** 10.1002/cb.70249

**The study:** Researchers surveyed current luxury rental customers in two markets — 251 Australian renters and 348 Chinese renters — and used PLS-SEM with multi-group comparison to test what drives repeat rentals. Both sustainability motivation (intrinsic) and social recognition motivation (extrinsic) fed into consumers' sense of fit with the brand's personality and user imagery, which in turn drove brand experience and intention to rent again. The cross-country split was the striking part: sustainability motivation mattered more to brand experience among Chinese renters, while social recognition drove brand congruity more strongly among Australians.

**Why it matters:** Luxury brands weighing whether to enter or expand rental channels get a concrete signal that the same service needs different positioning by market — green credentials in one, visible status in another.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you are building a rental or resale arm, consider testing market-specific messaging rather than a single global sustainability narrative; this survey suggests the status pitch and the eco pitch are not interchangeable across countries, though it measures stated intentions rather than booked rentals.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Renting a designer bag is not purely a thrift move — people are still buying into the brand's identity, and what they tell themselves about why they are doing it differs sharply depending on where they live.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey of self-selected renters, so causation cannot be established; outcome is re-rental intention, not observed behavior; only two countries; structural model results.

### 14. Shoppers' mood changes how much augmented reality actually sells

**Journal:** *Journal of Global Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-11-05
**Work ID:** W7225315487 | **DOI:** 10.1080/08911762.2026.2754739

**The study:** Nearly 1,000 respondents (N=956) were randomly assigned to use one of three real augmented reality apps — Lesslens, Room Planner, or Wanna Kicks — and then surveyed about continued use and purchase intention. Using a combination of PLS-SEM, multi-group analysis, necessary condition analysis, and fsQCA, the authors found that intention to keep using the AR tool was the key link to purchase intention, and that the shopper's mood moderated the relationship between AR features and buying intent.

**Why it matters:** AR try-on is expensive to build, and this suggests its commercial payoff depends less on feature richness than on whether users want to come back to the tool — and on their emotional state while using it.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Rather than benchmarking AR purely on realism or feature count, consider instrumenting your own app for repeat usage and testing whether placement in a positive, low-pressure browsing moment lifts conversion; the evidence here is intention-based, so treat it as a hypothesis for A/B testing.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Virtual try-on feels like an objective tool for checking fit, but the research suggests your mood while using it quietly shapes whether you end up buying.

**Caveats:** Outcome is stated purchase intention, not actual purchases; randomized app assignment but a survey-based measurement model; single study, apps span unrelated categories (eyewear, furniture, sneakers); country context not clearly specified.

### 15. The wedding purchase retailers keep getting wrong: bridal lingerie in India

**Journal:** *Qualitative Market Research An International Journal* | **Published:** 2026-11-06
**Work ID:** W7225430644 | **DOI:** 10.1108/qmr-06-2026-0170

**The study:** Fifteen engaged or recently married Indian women who had recently shopped for bridal lingerie were interviewed in depth, face-to-face or online, and analyzed through the Cognitive-Motivational-Relational Theory of Emotion. Shoppers described feeling anticipation and confidence alongside embarrassment generated by cultural taboos, conservative family involvement, and the retail environment itself — appraisals that ran simultaneously rather than sequentially, producing emotional ambivalence.

**Why it matters:** It identifies the store experience as an active source of shopper discomfort in a high-margin category, pointing to fixes — private fitting, trained staff, discreet online fulfillment — that are operational rather than promotional.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you sell in an emotionally loaded category, audit the in-store journey for moments that trigger embarrassment rather than only optimizing assortment and price; this qualitative work suggests retail design, not demand, is the binding constraint in some markets.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A purchase meant to feel celebratory often arrives tangled with family expectations and shop-floor awkwardness — a reminder of how much of shopping is social negotiation rather than personal choice.

**Caveats:** Small qualitative sample (n=15), single country, no quantification or causal claim; findings are interpretive and not generalizable.

## Spelling

American English used throughout; journal titles, framework names (NBD-Dirichlet, DEIA, Cognitive-Motivational-Relational Theory of Emotion) and the authors' own quoted terms ("pipeline-enhancing," "pipeline-transcending," "structural deviation index") are left as published.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- Technology-led customer experience keeps underperforming against the human and behavioral basics. "Head office calls it 'augmentation.' Store staff hear 'replacement.'" (W7225231563) and "Shoppers' mood changes how much augmented reality actually sells" (W7225315487) extend the pattern from "Smart Shopping Carts Don't Empower Everyone" (W7223273403) and "Bank Branches Earn Their Keep as Reassurance, Not Technology Showrooms" (W7223672553): the constraint is rarely feature richness, it is whether staff and shoppers want to use the thing at all.
- Review signals keep failing to mean what the industry assumes. "Not all small brands are small in the same way" (W7225744799) finds critic acclaim unrelated to market share, sitting alongside "Loyalty Members Write Glowing Reviews — and Less Useful Ones" (W7223652730): in both, the loudest voices in a review ecosystem map onto a narrow, highly engaged buyer segment rather than the broad market.
- Values signaling inside the store continues to be a measurable commercial variable. "Seven years of survey waves suggest shoppers who notice a retailer's inclusion efforts trust it more" (W7225631783) and "Vague DEI statements win shoppers over directly; specific ones work through a different route" (W7221486834) pick up where "Political signals in a store make customers more uncomfortable than rainbow flags or religious symbols" (W7221815419) and "'Would You Like to Donate?' Works Better When the Charity Is Local" (W7223898707) left off — with psychological distance surfacing again as the mechanism in both W7221486834 and W7223898707.
- Ambient and incidental states keep moving purchase decisions. Mood moderating AR conversion (W7225315487) and soft-launch self-presentation spilling into minimalist product preference (W7225714650) continue the thread from "People invest less money when they are in a crowd" (W7221805592) and "Shoppers Can't Measure Air Quality" (W7222049986).

**Advances**
- Omnichannel moves from cross-sectional snapshot to a one-year horizon. "Get the omnichannel journey right and satisfaction with the whole retailer is still higher a year later" (W7225122614) adds a longitudinal link to the channel-configuration comparison in "Webrooming wins the next sale; online-to-offline wins the relationship" (W7220357245), and supports treating journey-level satisfaction as a leading indicator rather than a channel-by-channel score.
- Promotion measurement gets a fulfillment-level cut. "Subsidize pickup, not delivery" (W7224921670) refines the portfolio-level logic of "Mobile coupons work harder before shoppers reach the mall than inside it" (W7223707755) and "Push notifications work — until they don't" (W7223280000): the same discount buys different volumes depending on how the order is filled.
- Live commerce gains a boundary condition. "'You must buy this': pushy livestream sellers work better when viewers feel ignored" (W7224102790) specifies when the hard sell pays, adding to the engagement-versus-sales divergence established by the mall-coupon and push-notification work and by earlier live-shopping findings.

**Counterpoints**
- Spillovers do not always appear. W7223707755 and W7223280000 both found promotion in one place lifting traffic elsewhere; the airport study (W7224921670) found spatial spillovers between stores statistically insignificant, which suggests the halo depends on the physical and competitive layout rather than being a general property of promotion.
- Cohort segmentation takes a direct hit. "Boomers, Gen Z and online reviews: the generation gap barely exists" (W7225751353) finds effect sizes near zero for age cohort, which complicates the framing of "For Gen Z, Talking to the Shopping App Beats Typing" (W7223671637) — the voice-commerce result may be about low-involvement, hedonic baskets rather than about Gen Z as such.
- On friction and control: the previous run's smart-cart fatigue result (W7223273403) argued against piling effort on shoppers, while "Household mess isn't a failure" (W7222556775) finds consumers actively tolerating disorder because it works — both point away from "optimize everything" as a design default, from opposite directions.

**New themes**

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** This batch quietly dismantles the demographic premise of retail segmentation and replaces it with situational state. Generational membership explained almost nothing about response to review cues, with effect sizes near zero (W7225751353), while what did predict behavior in other studies was transient context: mood moderated whether AR try-on converted (W7225315487), perceived interactivity decided whether assertive livestream selling worked or backfired (W7224102790), and how someone had just presented a relationship online shifted their preference toward minimalist products (W7225714650) — with cross-market differences in luxury rental motivation (W7223000149) suggesting that where someone shops matters more than how old they are.

**Why pitch this now:** Cohort targeting is being rebuilt into retail media networks and AI-driven personalization engines just as signal loss pushes brands toward whatever demographic proxies they can still buy, making this the moment to ask whether the variable being bought has any predictive power at all.

**Angle:** A reported feature on the evidence gap under age-cohort targeting — what "Gen Z shops differently" is actually based on, what the state-based alternatives would require retailers to measure, and why state-based targeting is harder to buy and easier to abuse — written for marketing and retail decision-makers, with a consumer-facing strand on how much of what you buy is set by the mood you happened to be in.

**Potential headlines:**
- Age cohort predicts almost nothing about how people shop online
- The most useful thing to know about a shopper is their mood, not their birth year
- Your segmentation model may be sorting customers by the one variable that doesn't move

**Potential outlets:**
- **Marketing Week** — runs sustained, evidence-led arguments about segmentation and effectiveness orthodoxy, and its readers buy cohort targeting every quarter.
- **MIT Sloan Management Review** — suited to the gap between what managers instrument and what the research supports, and tolerant of the exploratory caveats in the generational study.
- **Marketing Brew** — covers the mechanics of audience buying and retail media, where cohort segments are the product being sold.
- **Vox** — its consumer-behavior reporting would carry the general-reader version on how situational the "self" marketers target really is.
