### 1. The most politically extreme customers are your most loyal — and the least likely to notice a price hike

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-18
**Work ID:** W7213535017 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00222429261492328

**The study:** Published in the *Journal of Marketing*, this research introduces political extremism — how far toward either end of the ideological spectrum a consumer sits, regardless of direction — as a distinct dimension of polarization affecting post-purchase behavior. Across a multi-study program, consumers with more extreme political identities showed higher repurchase likelihood and notably weaker sensitivity to price increases by brands they had chosen, even when comparable alternatives were available. The mechanism was a belief in the superiority of their own consumption choices; effects strengthened when the brand faced consumption-domain threats (product failures, competitor launches) but faded when the threat was political (for example, discovering the brand's politics clashed with their own).

**Why it matters:** It reframes political polarization as a loyalty and pricing variable rather than only a left-versus-right targeting question, and flags a consumer-welfare problem: the most committed buyers may be quietly overpaying.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands segmenting by political leaning may be missing the more commercially relevant axis — identity intensity. Worth testing whether high-conviction customer cohorts show measurably lower price elasticity in your own data, while recognizing that exploiting that insensitivity is exactly the practice regulators and critics would seize on.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Strong political conviction appears to spill into shopping: people who hold intense ideological identities defend their brand choices as superior and swallow price increases others would walk away from. A useful prompt to check whether your loyalty to a brand is actually saving you money.

**Caveats:** The abstract does not specify sample sizes, populations or whether studies used real purchases; effects on price sensitivity appear to be measured in experimental settings rather than observed market behavior.

### 2. "High protein" doesn't make shoppers think a food is healthier — it makes them think it's more processed

**Journal:** *Food Quality and Preference* | **Published:** 2026-09-05
**Work ID:** W7208834717 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.foodqual.2026.106095

**The study:** In an online experiment (N=218), participants evaluated five food products shown either with or without a "high-protein" front-of-pack label and rated perceived calories, healthiness, sugar, additives, processing level and purchase intention. The label produced no general health halo: it did not shift perceived healthiness, calories, sugar or purchase intent. Instead, labeled products were judged to contain more additives and to be more industrially processed — a "horn" effect — with calorie and sugar inferences shifting mainly for high-calorie products, and existing attitudes toward protein products moderating responses throughout.

**Why it matters:** Protein claims have proliferated across categories on the assumption they signal virtue; this suggests the claim can instead cue "engineered food" for a meaningful share of shoppers.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Before stamping "high protein" across a portfolio, test the claim against a no-claim control on processing and additive perceptions, not just on appeal — and segment by how enthusiastic your buyers already are about protein, since prior attitude did more work than the label itself.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Shoppers may be getting more skeptical than food marketers assume: a protein flag on the pack can read as a sign the product has been reformulated in a factory rather than as a badge of health.

**Caveats:** Single online experiment with 218 participants; hypothetical product evaluations and stated purchase intention rather than real purchases; five products only; country and panel source not specified in the abstract.

### 3. Serve spicy food on an angular plate: shape-taste matching lifts how diners rate a dish

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-09-14
**Work ID:** W7212607085 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105104

**The study:** Four online experiments with 754 valid Chinese respondents spanning all seven major geographic regions tested whether plateware shape should match a dish's pungency. Pungent foods drew more favorable evaluations and higher ordering intention when shown on angular rather than round plates, while mild foods did better on round plates. Processing fluency — how easily the pairing is mentally processed — mediated the effect.

**Why it matters:** Plateware is one of the cheapest levers a restaurant or food brand controls, and this suggests matching plate geometry to a dish's sensory profile can shift appeal at essentially no cost.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** A low-risk A/B test for restaurant groups, meal-kit photography and delivery-app menu imagery: pair angular plates with spicy items and round plates with mild ones, and check whether ordering rates move. Note these were image-based online studies, so in-venue replication is the real test.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Your senses cross-talk more than you think — the outline of the plate under a curry can nudge how appetizing it looks before you have tasted anything.

**Caveats:** Online experiments using depicted rather than eaten food; ordering intention rather than actual orders; all participants Chinese, and shape-taste associations may not transfer across cuisines and cultures.

### 4. New Year shoppers want coupons; year-end shoppers want discounts

**Journal:** *European Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-18
**Work ID:** W7213550700 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ejm-07-2025-0600

**The study:** Three preregistered experiments tested how "temporal landmarks" — the start versus the end of a calendar period, manipulated via dates, weekdays and time of day — shape preference for two economically similar promotions: a coupon versus a straight discount. Consumers at start landmarks preferred coupon framing; those at end landmarks preferred discount framing, with mindset mediating the effect. Product type reversed the pattern: for hedonic goods the coupon-at-start, discount-at-end rule held, while for utilitarian goods it flipped.

**Why it matters:** Retailers routinely treat coupons and percentage-off discounts as interchangeable mechanics; this suggests the calendar moment and product category together determine which one lands better.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Test promotion mechanics against the calendar, not just against depth of offer — try coupon-led campaigns at January, Monday and month-start moments for indulgence categories, discount-led at period-end for the same, and the reverse for everyday staples.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Fresh-start moments change what feels like a good deal. The same money off can appeal or fall flat depending on whether you feel you are opening a new chapter or closing one out.

**Caveats:** Experimental studies with hypothetical offers and stated preferences rather than observed redemption; sample sizes and participant sources not specified in the abstract; mix of real and fictional brands.

### 5. Beauty filters widen the gap between how you look and how you want to look — and raise interest in cosmetic surgery

**Journal:** *European Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-07-31
**Work ID:** W7171940513 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ejm-01-2025-0033

**The study:** Drawing on self-discrepancy theory, this research tested how augmented reality face filters of the kind found on Snapchat and Instagram affect users' views of their actual versus ideal selves. Filter use enlarged the ideal-actual attractiveness gap, which produced psychological discomfort, which in turn predicted greater propensity toward cosmetic surgery and lower self-acceptance; body image moderated the first link. Analysis used the Hayes PROCESS macro on a hypothesized mediation-moderation model.

**Why it matters:** AR filters are a standard engagement and try-on feature for beauty and fashion brands, and this is among the first empirical evidence linking them to measurable self-concept harm and surgical intent.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands and platforms deploying AR beauty tools should anticipate scrutiny of the wellbeing case, not just the conversion case: the authors suggest warning or educational messaging, and there is an untested commercial question of whether "unfiltered" try-on modes build more trust than beautifying ones.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The filtered version of your face may not be harmless fun — it can quietly reset what you think you should look like, and the discomfort that follows is what pushes some people toward considering surgery.

**Caveats:** Sample size, country and recruitment source are not reported in the abstract; the design appears cross-sectional and survey-based, so causal direction and long-term effects are unestablished — the authors themselves call for longitudinal work.

### 6. Electric delivery vans may be quietly pushing customers toward cheeseburgers

**Journal:** *European Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-04
**Work ID:** W7172408085 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ejm-09-2025-0765

**The study:** Across six experiments, including two field studies, researchers tested how advertising a food delivery order as sustainable — delivered by electric vehicle — changed what people ordered. When delivery was framed as green, consumers felt less guilt about the meal itself and shifted toward more indulgent, less healthy options. The effect was stronger when shoppers had to pay extra for the sustainable delivery, suggesting that paying for the green option buys a bigger moral license.

**Why it matters:** A sustainability feature attached to the service layer of a transaction spilled over into the product itself, nudging up unhealthy consumption — an unintended consequence for delivery platforms, restaurants and public health bodies promoting green logistics.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Delivery platforms and restaurant brands promoting electric fleets should test whether the green badge is changing basket composition, not just brand perception — and consider whether pairing sustainability messaging with health cues, or decoupling the green fee from checkout, dampens the licensing effect. The field studies make this more than a lab curiosity, but effect sizes at commercial scale remain unproven.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A useful self-check for anyone who orders in: the feeling of having done something virtuous — recycling, walking to the store, paying extra for the eco option — can quietly loosen restraint on the next decision. The van being electric says nothing about what's inside the bag.

**Caveats:** Mix of online experiments and two field studies; the paper does not report sample sizes or countries in the abstract, and food delivery is a single category. Guilt is a self-reported mechanism.

### 7. People on GLP-1 drugs show a gut-level preference for healthy food — and don't know it

**Journal:** *Food Quality and Preference* | **Published:** 2026-09-15
**Work ID:** W7213351764 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.foodqual.2026.106111

**The study:** Researchers compared implicit and explicit food evaluations of GLP-1 medication users and non-users, using the Implicit Association Test with two different sets of evaluative terms: one about enjoyment and taste, the other about desirability. Medication users showed significantly stronger implicit preferences for healthy foods when the terms related to enjoyment and taste, an effect driven by semaglutide users rather than tirzepatide users. Explicit ratings of healthiness, tastiness and irresistibility did not differ between groups, implying users are not consciously aware of the shift.

**Why it matters:** It suggests GLP-1 drugs may not just suppress appetite but change what food feels enjoyable at an automatic level — which would reshape how food brands should talk to a fast-growing segment of customers.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Food and beverage brands building GLP-1 strategies typically assume smaller portions and higher protein; this hints the deeper opportunity is taste and pleasure positioning around healthy options, since users may find them genuinely more appealing without saying so on a survey. Worth testing directly with sensory and purchase data rather than stated preference research, and worth watching whether the effect differs by drug.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If you're on one of these medications and find yourself reaching for the salad, it may not be willpower — your automatic taste associations may have moved without you noticing, which is why self-reported cravings are a poor guide to what you'll actually eat.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional comparison of users and non-users, so it cannot establish that the medication caused the shift (people who choose these drugs may differ to begin with); sample size and country not stated in the abstract; implicit association measures are noisy predictors of real-world eating; the semaglutide versus tirzepatide contrast rests on subgroup comparisons.

### 8. Consumers file "cell-based sausage" by how it was made, not by what it is

**Journal:** *Food Quality and Preference* | **Published:** 2026-09-07
**Work ID:** W7211919306 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.foodqual.2026.106110

**The study:** A total of 154 German-speaking omnivores in Switzerland freely sorted 24 written product concepts — animal-based items such as sausage, plus explicitly labeled plant-based and cell-based versions — into groups of their own choosing, with no images or products involved. Multidimensional scaling and cluster analysis showed that people organized the concepts primarily by production method and biological source rather than by food type, and the pattern was similar for men and women.

**Why it matters:** Prior research suggests alternatives are adopted more readily when consumers mentally file them in the same category as the conventional product; these results indicate explicit "cell-based" labeling does the opposite, carving out a separate mental category.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For companies commercializing cultivated meat and fish, the wording on the pack may determine whether the product competes inside the meat category or sits in a novelty ghetto of its own — a case for testing label formats, adjacency on shelf and visual presentation before regulation locks terminology in place.
- **For general readers and consumers:** How a food is described shapes where it sits in your head before you ever taste it: told something was grown from cells, shoppers stopped thinking "sausage" and started thinking "lab."

**Caveats:** Single country (German-speaking Switzerland), modest sample of 154, omnivores only; participants sorted written concepts with no images, tasting or actual purchases, so it measures mental categorization rather than buying behavior.

### 9. Free vegetable-rich school lunches changed nothing — because the kids didn't eat them

**Journal:** *Appetite* | **Published:** 2026-08-11
**Work ID:** W7202165920 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.appet.2026.108739

**The study:** Researchers ran a controlled quasi-experiment across six Dutch primary schools: three schools served vegetable-rich lunches three days a week to children aged 7–13, three acted as controls. Before and after, they measured cognition, wellbeing, food neophobia, food literacy and skin carotenoid levels (a biomarker of vegetable intake, via the Veggie Meter). There were no significant differences between groups on any measure; if anything, food neophobia fell in the control group but not the intervention group. The authors attribute the null result to low meal acceptance, social dynamics at the table, and the cultural unfamiliarity of a warm lunch at school in the Netherlands.

**Why it matters:** A well-intentioned, well-funded healthy-food program can produce zero measurable benefit if the product itself isn't accepted — the distribution problem is solved long before the consumption problem is.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** A textbook case of trial without adoption: supply and availability are not the same as uptake. For anyone launching a health-positive product or reformulation, this argues for testing acceptance and gradual familiarization first — and for measuring actual consumption, not distribution, as the success metric.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Handing children vegetables doesn't make them eat vegetables. Habit, peer pressure and what food 'should' look like at lunchtime appear to matter more than what's on the plate — which is why abrupt dietary switches at home often fail too.

**Caveats:** Quasi-experimental, not randomized; only six schools in one country; short intervention period; null findings may reflect insufficient dose and implementation rather than an ineffective concept; the positive associations reported (carotenoid status with wellbeing, meal liking) are exploratory and correlational.

### 10. Ads run during wartime were judged more trustworthy — and lifted brands more than peacetime ads

**Journal:** *International Journal of Advertising* | **Published:** 2026-09-12
**Work ID:** W7212377582 | **DOI:** 10.1080/02650487.2026.2730727

**The study:** Researchers compared television advertising during the first month of a war with a matched routine period, combining advertising expenditure data, consumer attitude measures and a content analysis of the ads themselves. Total ad spending fell sharply, with health and financial services holding relatively steady while food, fashion and leisure dropped significantly. Wartime ads were rated as more reliable and were linked to stronger brand evaluations; routine-period ads were rated as more entertaining but also more irritating. Content analysis showed a wartime shift toward informational and socially oriented messaging.

**Why it matters:** It offers rare evidence on what happens to advertising effectiveness when brands pull back during a national crisis — the survivors in the break faced less clutter and were received more credibly.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** The instinct in a crisis is to go dark. This data suggests the opposite trade-off is worth modeling: as competitors withdraw and tone shifts to informational and community-oriented messaging, the ads that remain may be perceived as more reliable and lift brand evaluations. Treat it as a hypothesis to test against your own crisis-period tracking, not a universal rule.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Viewers don't judge ads by a fixed standard. The same jokey, high-gloss commercial that reads as entertaining in normal times reads as irritating in a national emergency — and a plain informational ad that would normally be forgettable suddenly reads as honest.

**Caveats:** Single country and a single crisis period observed over one month; attitude measures are self-reported evaluations rather than sales; the comparison period is matched but not randomized, so wider contextual changes cannot be ruled out; findings may not generalize to other types of crisis or other media.

### 11. When an AI 'doctor' gives health advice, numbers beat stories — up to a point

**Journal:** *Asia Pacific Journal of Marketing and Logistics* | **Published:** 2026-09-11
**Work ID:** W7212302631 | **DOI:** 10.1108/apjml-04-2026-0860

**The study:** Across three experiments in online health communication settings, participants received health information from digital human doctors using either statistical evidence or anecdotal evidence. Statistical evidence increased willingness to adopt the information, and the effect ran through lower perceived deceptiveness — the numbers made the AI presenter feel less like it was trying to manipulate them. The advantage disappeared when the information came from a 'mirrored' digital human (a digital replica of a real person) rather than a purely virtual one.

**Why it matters:** Health brands, pharmacies and insurers increasingly deploy AI avatars to deliver advice, and this suggests the evidence style and the avatar's provenance interact: what persuades from a synthetic presenter is not what persuades from a digital twin of a real clinician.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you are piloting AI presenters in health or wellness content, treat evidence format as a variable to test alongside avatar design. The signal here is that synthetic-looking presenters may need statistics to buy credibility, while replicas of real humans can carry anecdote — worth validating in live content before rolling it out.
- **For general readers and consumers:** People appear to apply a suspicion discount to AI-delivered health claims, and hard numbers are what lowers it. A vivid patient story from a machine reads as a sales pitch; a percentage reads as a fact.

**Caveats:** Lab-style online experiments with hypothetical health messages rather than real medical decisions or behavior; sample sizes, recruitment source and country are not specified in the abstract; measures stated adoption intentions rather than observed health actions.

### 12. 116 studies later: why alcohol awareness campaigns change minds but not drinking

**Journal:** *Journal of Advertising Research* | **Published:** 2026-09-02
**Work ID:** W7206215832 | **DOI:** 10.1080/00218499.2026.2693332

**The study:** Researchers synthesized evidence from 116 studies of alcohol harm-reduction campaigns to work out why such campaigns reliably move awareness and attitudes yet rarely produce lasting behavior change. They propose a "Limits and Levers" framework built on four mechanisms: alignment (message-audience fit), psychological activation (emotion, efficacy, resistance), social amplification (norms and peer diffusion), and structural integration (media and environmental conditions). The central finding is that standalone campaigns underperform, while communication embedded in wider systems of influence, such as pricing, availability and peer norms, does better.

**Why it matters:** Public-health and CSR-funded advertising budgets are frequently spent on awareness campaigns evaluated on recall and attitude shifts, metrics this synthesis suggests are poor predictors of actual drinking behavior.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For anyone commissioning or buying behavior-change advertising, the practical question shifts from "is the creative good?" to "what else is in place to convert intent into action?" Worth testing campaigns that are deliberately paired with environmental or retail-level changes, and worth reconsidering KPIs that stop at awareness.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It's a useful lens on why the anti-drinking ad you remember vividly never changed what you ordered at the bar: messages compete with price, availability and the drinking norms of the people around you.

**Caveats:** This is an evidence synthesis and conceptual framework rather than new primary data; the underlying studies vary in design, country and campaign type, and the framework itself has not been tested prospectively.

### 13. Nostalgic packaging nudges people to waste less food, Chinese experiments suggest

**Journal:** *Asia Pacific Journal of Marketing and Logistics* | **Published:** 2026-08-29
**Work ID:** W7204661572 | **DOI:** 10.1108/apjml-04-2026-0892

**The study:** Two online experiments run on the Credamo platform in early 2026 with 456 participants across Chinese provinces (N=196 and N=260) tested whether retro-styled food packaging affects intentions to reduce food waste. Retro designs raised waste-reduction intentions, working through two parallel routes: a sense of "groundedness" and perceptions of the effort behind the food. A follow-up showed that consumers' perceived subjective social status moderated both pathways, so the effect was not uniform across the sample.

**Why it matters:** It points to a near-zero-cost design lever, packaging aesthetics, that may influence how carefully consumers treat food after purchase, an area brands usually treat as outside their control.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Packaging teams could run in-market tests of nostalgic or heritage-styled designs against current art, measuring not just purchase but post-purchase outcomes like portion finishing or leftover use. The status moderation also suggests the effect may need segment-level testing rather than a blanket rollout.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The look of a package appears to change how much effort you think went into the food inside, and that in turn changes how willing you are to throw it away.

**Caveats:** Online panel participants in a single country; stated intentions rather than measured waste; hypothetical packaging stimuli rather than real purchases; short time horizon.

### 14. The most effective men's health program may be the one that never mentions health

**Journal:** *Health Promotion International* | **Published:** 2026-09-01
**Work ID:** W7213513131 | **DOI:** 10.1093/heapro/daag140

**The study:** Researchers conducted semi-structured and pitch-side interviews with 33 men across two sites of the Football Cooperative, an Irish social enterprise using recreational football to reach men who avoid conventional health services, and analyzed them using reflexive thematic analysis and Schein's organizational culture framework. They found that football functions as a taken-for-granted part of masculine identity and requires no health framing at all to draw men in. Health and social benefits followed when the program's design, reliability and inclusive norms aligned with what the men already valued.

**Why it matters:** For health communicators and social marketers, it suggests the barrier is often the health frame itself, which carries social risk, rather than a lack of information or motivation.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Anyone trying to reach hard-to-engage audiences might test "host the thing they already love" over "advertise the benefit": embed the offer in an existing cultural ritual and let outcomes follow, rather than leading with health messaging that signals vulnerability.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Men who would never book a check-up will show up every week for a kickabout, and the friendships and fitness arrive as a by-product rather than the pitch.

**Caveats:** Qualitative interviews with 33 men at two sites of a single Irish initiative; no health outcomes measured; self-selected participants who were already attending.

### 15. Kids know sugar is bad for them. They just really like it

**Journal:** *Appetite* | **Published:** 2026-09-15
**Work ID:** W7213326632 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.appet.2026.108786

**The study:** Researchers ran six focus groups with 44 fourth-grade primary school children across two schools, asking about their motivations for eating sugar, where sugary foods are most available, and the roles of family and school. The children could readily identify sugar sources and name health consequences, but described consumption as driven by sensory pleasure and emotional attachment to sweets. Findings were mapped onto the COM-B behavior framework, showing capability (knowledge), opportunity (family and school contexts) and motivation (sensory appeal) each act as both barrier and facilitator.

**Why it matters:** It undercuts the assumption behind much child-focused nutrition communication, that more information will change behavior, and points instead at availability and hedonic appeal as the levers that matter.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For food and beverage brands and public-health campaigners alike, the takeaway is that education-only messaging to children is likely to fail; the actionable variables are where sugary products appear in a child's day and whether reduced-sugar alternatives can compete on taste and fun.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Children as young as nine can explain why sugar is bad for them and eat it anyway, which is a fairly good description of most adults' relationship with food too.

**Caveats:** Small qualitative study, 44 children in two schools, single country; focus groups capture stated views, not observed eating; group dynamics may shape what children say.

### 16. Can a VR headset make people want to volunteer abroad?

**Journal:** *Journal of Business Research* | **Published:** 2026-07-30
**Work ID:** W7171788770 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jbusres.2026.116432

**The study:** A four-study program combining netnography, an experiment, focus groups and structural modeling examined whether virtual reality promotional content increases willingness to engage in voluntourism. VR raised willingness indirectly, through perceived authenticity and curiosity, but the authenticity route weakened among people high in cultural adventurousness and "aspiring indulger" orientation. Self-congruence predicted willingness positively, while a sense of moral obligation predicted it negatively, and willingness itself was positively related to psychological wellbeing.

**Why it matters:** It suggests immersive promotional content works on prosocial offers by sparking curiosity and a sense of realness, and that guilt-based moral appeals may actively backfire.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Travel, charity and cause marketers experimenting with immersive content could test curiosity-led and identity-led framings against obligation-led ones, and watch for the finding that the most worldly, adventure-seeking segments respond least to authenticity cues.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The finding that feeling obligated makes people less willing to sign up is a reminder of how easily guilt-based appeals from charities can push people away rather than draw them in.

**Caveats:** Willingness and stated intentions rather than actual volunteering or bookings; mixed-method design with limited detail on sample sizes and sourcing; wellbeing link is correlational.

### 17. When a disease is brand new, telling people how likely they are to catch it beats describing the symptoms

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-31
**Work ID:** W7204799462 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jcm-11-2024-7364

**The study:** Across three experiments, researchers tested how two kinds of information — the odds of contracting a disease and the severity of its symptoms — drive information seeking and preventive intentions such as vaccination. For "truly novel" diseases like COVID-19 in its early days, where infection risk is knowable but consequences are not, only probability information moved behavior; descriptions of symptoms and consequences did not. Emphasizing probability in the message raised preventive intentions for novel diseases, but had no such effect for familiar, well-understood illnesses.

**Why it matters:** Public health campaigns and health brands routinely lead with vivid depictions of what a disease does to you; this evidence suggests that for an unfamiliar threat, the more persuasive lever is simply how likely you are to get it.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Health communicators and pharma marketers could test probability-led messaging against consequence-led messaging in early-stage outbreak or new-vaccine campaigns — and note that the same framing appears to add nothing for established diseases, where the playbook may need to flip. These are controlled experiments, so the effect size in a real campaign is an open question.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It's a window into how we process unfamiliar risk: when we can't picture the outcome, we anchor on the odds instead — which helps explain why early pandemic messaging about case rates landed harder than descriptions of symptoms.

**Caveats:** Experimental studies with hypothetical scenarios and stated behavioral intentions rather than observed vaccination or prevention behavior; sample composition and country not specified in the abstract.

### 18. AI companions make users feel better and more exposed at the same time

**Journal:** *Psychology and Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-07-23
**Work ID:** W7170143709 | **DOI:** 10.1002/mar.70230

**The study:** Researchers combined a netnography of 1,131 online posts with a survey of 610 users of AI companion apps to map how emotional dependence on these products affects wellbeing. The warmth and constant availability of an AI companion produced a "positive" dependence that reduced felt vulnerability and improved wellbeing. But the AI's emotional capabilities — combined with the fact that a third-party company controls the relationship — increased users' sense of a power imbalance, raising vulnerability and lowering wellbeing.

**Why it matters:** Companion AI is becoming a consumer category, and this suggests the very features that make it engaging (emotional responsiveness, always-on availability) are also what make users feel exposed to the company behind the app.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For anyone building or partnering with emotionally responsive AI products, the finding points to a design and governance question worth testing: can protective features — transparency about company control, coping and self-worth supports, user input into the product — retain the warmth benefit while defusing the power-imbalance harm? Expect regulators and journalists to press on who owns the relationship.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For users, the discomfort many feel about an AI friend isn't irrational: the study puts a name to it — a company, not the companion, holds the controls, and the more emotionally capable the AI, the sharper that asymmetry feels.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey plus social-media posts, so causal direction cannot be established; self-selected users of companion apps; platform and country coverage not specified in the abstract.

### 19. In rural areas, opaque bank algorithms push people out of banking — unless the community pushes back

**Journal:** *International Journal of Bank Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-07-23
**Work ID:** W7170190396 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ijbm-10-2025-0850

**The study:** A two-part study examined how algorithmic opacity in financial services affects remote communities in North America and Europe: 32 in-depth interviews across eight peripheral regions (Nunavut, Yukon, Appalachia, Northern Maine, the Scottish Highlands and Islands, Lapland, Galicia, the Eastern Carpathians), followed by a survey of 575 residents modeled with structural equation modeling. Opaque algorithms and weak AI governance were linked to perceptions of "digital redlining" and financial anxiety, eroding trust and driving withdrawal from banking. Community empowerment buffered the effect: in digitally literate, well-connected communities, people read algorithmic failures as a systemic problem rather than a personal one and stayed engaged.

**Why it matters:** Banks automating credit and service decisions may be quietly losing whole geographies of customers, and the mitigating factor is not just better algorithms but whether local communities can interpret rejections collectively.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Financial services marketers should look at withdrawal and disengagement rates by geography, not just by segment, and test whether local explanation efforts — plain-language decision reasons, community partnerships, digital literacy programs — measurably retain customers where automated decisioning is dense.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A quietly reassuring finding for consumers: people who talk to their neighbors about being turned down by an algorithm are less likely to blame themselves — and less likely to give up on banking altogether.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey for the quantitative portion, so causation cannot be established; self-reported perceptions and intentions rather than observed account closures; eight specific rural regions that may not generalize to urban or Global South contexts.

### 20. Financial anxiety hurts men and women's wellbeing in exactly the same way

**Journal:** *International Journal of Bank Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-18
**Work ID:** W7203676845 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ijbm-11-2025-0873

**The study:** Using the 2021 US National Financial Capability Study, researchers modeled financial anxiety as the link between financial capability and financial wellbeing among American adults. Higher capability predicted lower anxiety and higher wellbeing, with anxiety carrying a significant share of the effect. A multi-group analysis found the mechanism was gender-invariant: despite women typically reporting higher anxiety levels, the pathway from capability to wellbeing via anxiety worked identically for men and women.

**Why it matters:** It challenges a common assumption in financial services marketing and financial education that women need structurally different programs — the difference appears to be in levels of anxiety, not in how anxiety operates.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Banks and fintechs segmenting financial wellness content by gender may be solving the wrong problem: the evidence suggests targeting emotional barriers directly, for everyone, rather than building parallel gendered products. Worth testing whether anxiety-reducing messaging outperforms knowledge-only content in live campaigns.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Financial know-how doesn't just help you make better decisions — it helps mostly by making you less anxious, and that route to feeling better about money works the same regardless of gender.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional secondary survey data, so causation cannot be established; single country (United States); self-reported measures; a single survey wave (2021), during pandemic-era financial conditions.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** A cluster of studies in this batch lands on the same uncomfortable conclusion: telling people what is healthy does almost nothing, because the obstacle is pleasure, availability and social context, not ignorance. "Free vegetable-rich school lunches changed nothing — because the kids didn't eat them" (W7202165920) and "Kids know sugar is bad for them. They just really like it" (W7213326632) show knowledge and supply both failing on their own; "116 studies later: why alcohol awareness campaigns change minds but not drinking" (W7206215832) generalizes it across an entire communications category; and "The most effective men's health program may be the one that never mentions health" (W7213513131) shows what works instead — embedding the behavior in something people already want to do. Two further studies suggest health framing can actively misfire: a "high protein" flag makes shoppers think "processed," not "healthy" (W7208834717), and a green delivery badge loosens restraint on what people order (W7172408085).

**Why pitch this now:** Governments across the UK and Europe are expanding free school meals and tightening junk-food advertising rules on the assumption that supply and messaging shift diets, while public-health and CSR communications budgets are being defended on recall and awareness metrics this evidence says are poor predictors of behavior.

**Angle:** A reported feature on the collapse of the information-deficit model in health marketing — why the best-funded awareness work keeps failing its own evaluation, and what the alternative (design for pleasure, availability and existing rituals) looks like in practice — aimed at marketers, agency strategists and public-health commissioners, with enough human material to carry a general-interest read.

**Potential headlines:**
- Awareness campaigns move attitudes, not behavior — and the research now says why
- The healthiest lunch in the room is the one nobody eats
- Your health messaging budget is buying recall you can't cash

**Potential outlets:**
- **Marketing Week** — its effectiveness coverage would take on the argument that awareness and attitude KPIs are the wrong measure for behavior-change advertising.
- **The Grocer** — directly relevant to reformulation, school-meal policy and the finding that a "high protein" claim can read as "industrially processed" to shoppers.
- **MIT Sloan Management Review** — fits its appetite for evidence-led pieces on program design and why well-funded interventions fail at the adoption stage.
- **The Guardian (food/consumer desk)** — runs school-food and public-health-policy stories with a strong consumer-behavior angle and would use the null school-lunch result as a news hook.
