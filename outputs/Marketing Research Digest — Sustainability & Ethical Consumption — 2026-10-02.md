### 1. Thinner packaging saves material — and ends up in the trash instead of the recycling bin

**Journal:** *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* | **Published:** 2026-09-23
**Work ID:** W7214074641 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11747-026-01208-7

**The study:** Marketing researchers examined "lightweighting," the common sustainability-and-cost tactic of using less raw material per package, and traced what happens after the package reaches the consumer. Across a set of studies on disposal decisions, they found that lower packaging density acts as a cue: flimsier packs are judged lower in quality and value, trigger less waste aversion, and are more likely to be improperly thrown in the trash rather than recycled. Prompting people to reconsider the value of the low-density package reversed much of the effect.

**Why it matters:** A packaging change that cuts a firm's material footprint on paper can increase the volume of recyclable material that never gets recycled, meaning sustainability wins measured at the factory may be partly cancelled at the curb.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands that have lightweighted packaging should test whether their recyclable packs are actually being recycled — and whether on-pack value or recyclability cues close the gap. It's a reminder to measure sustainability claims across the full life cycle, including consumer disposal, rather than only at the manufacturing stage.
- **For general readers and consumers:** We quietly judge a package's worth by how substantial it feels in the hand — and flimsy packaging gets treated as garbage even when it's perfectly recyclable. Worth a second look at what you toss.

**Caveats:** The abstract does not report sample sizes, participant pools, or whether disposal was observed in the field versus simulated in experiments; effects on actual recycling rates at scale remain untested.

### 2. Shoppers want recycled packaging — but will pay extra only for the plastic seal that proves it's not a fake

**Journal:** *Asia Pacific Journal of Marketing and Logistics* | **Published:** 2026-08-05
**Work ID:** W7172534656 | **DOI:** 10.1108/apjml-09-2025-1965

**The study:** Researchers ran a choice-based conjoint study with 133 young Thai consumers, varying shop type (official store versus other sellers), inner packaging, secondary packaging and price across e-commerce product profiles, then estimated willingness to pay and simulated market shares. Participants preferred 100% recycled plastic inner packaging but would not pay a premium for it; they would pay extra for plastic-sealed secondary packaging, which signals authenticity and safety. When buying from an official shop, they were more willing to skip the extra packaging entirely, because the retailer's reputation already addressed counterfeit fears.

**Why it matters:** In markets where counterfeiting is a live concern, plastic-heavy packaging is doing trust work, not just protection work — and sustainability campaigns that ignore that will lose to safety anxiety.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For e-commerce brands in emerging markets, the fastest route to less packaging may be building verified-seller trust signals (official storefronts, authentication codes, tamper-evident labels) rather than persuading shoppers to value recycled material — a hypothesis worth testing with real listings and real sales.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The gap between what people say they want and what they'll pay for shows up clearly here: green packaging earns approval but not money, while the shrink-wrap that promises "this isn't a counterfeit" earns a premium.

**Caveats:** Hypothetical choice scenarios rather than real purchases; stated preferences and willingness to pay rather than observed behavior; small sample (N=133) of young consumers in a single country (Thailand); findings may not generalize to older shoppers or mature markets.

### 3. When big investors spread themselves thin, companies' social responsibility slips

**Journal:** *Journal of Business Research* | **Published:** 2026-09-24
**Work ID:** W7214208660 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jbusres.2026.116565

**The study:** Using 22,823 firm-year observations covering 2,877 U.S. listed firms from 1997 to 2017, this study tested whether institutional investors holding large stakes in many companies at once monitor corporate social responsibility as effectively as they monitor financial performance. They don't: as blockholders' portfolios spread across more firms — especially across more industries, and during periods of exogenous "distraction" — the firms they own score lower on CSR. The effect concentrated in financially immaterial CSR dimensions and among active, short-term investors, and held up under two quasi-experimental tests based on financial-institution mergers and Russell index reconstitutions.

**Why it matters:** ESG performance may depend less on investor intentions than on investor attention capacity, which has implications for anyone relying on large shareholders to police corporate sustainability.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Communications and sustainability leaders should recognize that CSR is harder for investors to verify than earnings, so material, measurable sustainability disclosure is likelier to survive investor scrutiny than diffuse social initiatives — and shareholder composition may predict how much slack a company gets.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The assumption that giant institutional investors are quietly keeping companies honest on climate and social issues has limits: when a fund is watching hundreds of firms, the softer commitments are the first to go unwatched.

**Caveats:** U.S. listed firms only, data ending in 2017 (before the recent ESG-reporting wave); CSR measured through third-party ratings, which are themselves contested; observational data with quasi-experimental identification rather than a true experiment.

### 4. Why green influencers get called out — and why followers keep watching anyway

**Journal:** *International Journal of Consumer Studies* | **Published:** 2026-09-29
**Work ID:** W7214725050 | **DOI:** 10.1111/ijcs.70262

**The study:** Researchers ran a netnographic analysis of 1,453 TikTok comments on 21 videos from 16 green influencers, mostly in Vietnam, coding how followers talk about authenticity and greenwashing in public. Three distinct strands of skepticism emerged: doubts about the influencer's personal motives, doubts about whether the promoted action actually helps, and doubts about whether the recommended "green" alternative is genuinely better. Crucially, skepticism did not always kill engagement — when followers judged the content as theatrical or staged, commentary turned hostile, but when they separated the messenger from the cause, admiration and stated pro-environmental intentions coexisted with open doubt.

**Why it matters:** Green influencer marketing is often evaluated as if trust were binary, but this suggests audiences routinely hold doubt and support at the same time — and that the trigger for backlash is perceived performance, not imperfection.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands briefing sustainability creators might test the paper's practical prescription: keep the camera subordinate to the activity, report verified outcomes rather than gestures, and state the conditions under which a green claim actually holds. These are observational patterns from one platform, so treat them as hypotheses to A/B test in comment sentiment, not a proven formula.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For anyone scrolling eco-content, the study is a portrait of how ordinary viewers do their own greenwashing audits in real time — and how many of them consciously decide to take the cause seriously even while writing off the person selling it.

**Caveats:** Qualitative, observational and non-causal; a single platform (TikTok) and predominantly a single country (Vietnam); comments capture expressed intentions and public posturing, not actual purchases or behavior.

### 5. Visible sustainability can make luxury look less fashionable — unless the brand frames it as a design choice

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Behaviour* | **Published:** 2026-10-01
**Work ID:** W7215083585 | **DOI:** 10.1002/cb.70244

**The study:** Two experiments in the luxury fashion context tested how consumers interpret visible signs of circular production — recycled or reclaimed materials showing in the design itself. Rather than reading as a straightforward virtue, these cues triggered inferences about whether the look was an aesthetic compromise or a deliberate creative decision, running through perceived aesthetic burden, perceived designer authorship, and perceived symbolic novelty. The effect on perceived fashionability was not uniform: it hinged on those interpretations and on how the brand was positioned.

**Why it matters:** In symbolic categories where the product is partly a status signal, making sustainability visible can backfire if it reads as constraint rather than creativity — so the framing around a circular product may matter as much as the material itself.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you are launching recycled or upcycled lines in a premium category, consider testing whether your creative communicates designer intent — authorship, deliberate novelty — versus looking like a material compromise. These are controlled experiments, so they show a mechanism that can operate, not a guaranteed market outcome.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A quiet insight into status shopping: people appear to judge a visibly sustainable luxury item partly on whether they believe the designer chose the look or was stuck with it.

**Caveats:** Experimental studies with evaluation measures rather than real purchases; luxury fashion only; sample details and sizes are not specified in the abstract; perceived fashionability is a judgment, not behavior.

### 6. Why people hang onto things they no longer use — and what that means for the circular economy

**Journal:** *Australasian Marketing Journal (AMJ)* | **Published:** 2026-09-24
**Work ID:** W7214159818 | **DOI:** 10.1177/14413582261483313

**The study:** Using the means-end chain method, researchers conducted 64 laddering interviews to map the hierarchy of attributes, consequences and personal values behind consumers' decisions to keep, pass on or throw away household goods. Security and self-direction emerged as the dominant values driving disposal decisions, and five distinct perceptual patterns appeared: performance, well-being, social recognition, trade-off and altruism. The analysis focuses on why products get discarded prematurely rather than retained in use.

**Why it matters:** Circular-economy policy and resale business models usually target the moment of purchase; this points at the disposal decision as the real choke point, and shows it is driven by deeply personal values rather than environmental attitudes alone.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For brands building take-back, repair or resale programs, the finding suggests messaging built on security (will this still work, is it safe, do I have a backup) and autonomy may outperform generic eco-appeals. Qualitative and exploratory, so it is a source of testable message hypotheses rather than a validated segmentation.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A mirror for the drawer full of old chargers: the study suggests people's keep-or-toss instincts trace back to a handful of core values — feeling secure, staying in control, being seen a certain way — more than to any calculation about waste.

**Caveats:** Small qualitative sample (64 interviews), no causal test, self-reported reasoning about past decisions rather than observed disposal behavior; single-study, geographically limited.

### 7. Secondhand clothing subscription boxes: most success factors are hurdles you must clear, not levers you can pull

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-09-21
**Work ID:** W7213931215 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105119

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 215 German consumers about intention to buy a secondhand fashion subscription box, testing eleven candidate determinants from the resale literature. They paired standard regression (which identifies what drives intention on average) with Necessary Condition Analysis (which identifies conditions that must be present above a threshold for intention to exist at all). Most determinants turned out to be necessity-based bottlenecks — absent them, intention collapses regardless of other strengths — and only some were also meaningful drivers, with relevance varying across subgroups.

**Why it matters:** It reframes how to prioritize investment in a circular business model: clearing minimum thresholds on hygiene factors may matter more than maximizing any single appealing feature.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you are piloting resale subscription or rental formats, this suggests auditing for bottlenecks — the conditions that, if unmet, cap adoption outright — before optimizing the pitch. The authors themselves warn the determinants are context-dependent and do not transfer across circular categories, so run the diagnostic on your own category.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Explains why so many secondhand-by-subscription services flame out: shoppers appear to have non-negotiables (hygiene, fit, value, trust) that must all be satisfied at once, and a great story on one dimension does not buy forgiveness on another.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey measuring purchase intention, not actual subscriptions; single country (Germany); N=215 total, so subgroup analyses rest on small cells; authors note findings do not generalize to other circular-economy contexts.

### 8. "Experts recommend it" beat "you said you cared" — in both Ecuador and Norway

**Journal:** *Food Quality and Preference* | **Published:** 2026-09-23
**Work ID:** W7214115221 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.foodqual.2026.106122

**The study:** In a preregistered behavioral field experiment, 346 participants in Ecuador and Norway were randomly assigned to a control condition or to one of two persuasion messages built on classic influence principles — authority (an expert endorsement) or commitment-consistency (reminding people of their own stated values) — before making a real choice between a more and a less sustainable option. Both message conditions together lifted the share choosing the sustainable option over control, but the effect was driven almost entirely by the authority message. Contrary to the researchers' own hypothesis, culture did not moderate the result: the authority appeal worked about equally well in both countries, and it worked regardless of how much participants said they trusted authorities or how much they valued personal consistency.

**Why it matters:** Sustainability messaging is often built on self-consistency and identity appeals, yet this test found a simple, contextually fitting expert endorsement outperformed them — and traveled across two very different cultural settings without adaptation.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Worth A/B testing an expert- or institution-backed claim against values-and-identity copy in your own sustainability communications, and resisting the assumption that each market needs a culturally bespoke appeal — this one experiment suggests the authority route may generalize further than marketers expect, but it needs replication at your scale and category.
- **For general readers and consumers:** People like to think their green choices come from their own values, but in this experiment what actually moved the needle was being told that experts recommend an option — and it worked even on people who said they didn't especially trust authorities.

**Caveats:** Single choice task in two countries with a modest total sample (N=346 across three conditions); the authority effect emerged from the combined test rather than a planned contrast, and the country-moderation hypothesis was disconfirmed; one product domain.

### 9. Hungry donors respond better to thank-you messages, four experiments suggest

**Journal:** *Asia Pacific Journal of Marketing and Logistics* | **Published:** 2026-10-01
**Work ID:** W7215072413 | **DOI:** 10.1108/apjml-03-2026-0741

**The study:** Four experiments with 883 participants recruited through the Chinese panel platform Credamo manipulated or measured momentary hunger, then showed participants donation feedback (the confirmation messages charity platforms send after a gift) and measured willingness to donate again. Relatively hungry donors reported higher intentions to give again than satiated ones, an effect the authors trace to a heightened sense of achievement from the feedback. Framing mattered: feedback emphasizing benefits to others amplified the hunger effect, while self-benefit framing weakened it.

**Why it matters:** Platforms increasingly personalize charitable prompts by user data; this suggests a donor's momentary physiological state — and the time of day it implies — could be another lever on repeat giving, and that other-benefit framing is the safer default.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For apps and platforms running embedded giving, the testable question is whether timing donation confirmations and re-engagement nudges around natural pre-meal windows lifts repeat donation rates — and whether "here's what your gift did for them" consistently outperforms "here's what you achieved." These are stated intentions in a panel, so treat it as a hypothesis for a live holdout test, not a scheduling rule.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A reminder that decisions we experience as moral are nudged by mundane bodily states: the same thank-you message can land differently depending on whether you've eaten lunch.

**Caveats:** Online panel participants in a single country (China) reporting willingness to donate rather than making real donations; hypothetical feedback scenarios; short time horizon; effects on stated intentions may not survive in live platform data.

### 10. How sponsoring Olympians buys a mining magnate glowing press

**Journal:** *Public Relations Review* | **Published:** 2026-09-23
**Work ID:** W7214088398 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.pubrev.2026.102774

**The study:** This critical public relations study analyzed Australian newspaper coverage generated by mining billionaire Gina Rinehart's sponsorship of elite Olympic athletes, using qualitative content analysis to trace how earned media framed the sponsor. Coverage overwhelmingly cast her as a benevolent benefactor of sport, linking her to likeable athletes and to patriotism and national glory rather than to her core business. The authors build a model of "sportswashing" as a reputational outcome of sports philanthropy — genuinely beneficial to the athletes funded, and simultaneously a vehicle for image bolstering.

**Why it matters:** It documents the mechanism by which sports sponsorship converts money into favorable framing in national media, which is exactly what makes athlete philanthropy attractive to brands and individuals whose core business is under scrutiny.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For sponsorship and communications leads, this is a map of the halo transfer athlete deals produce in earned media — and a warning that as journalists and watchdogs adopt the sportswashing frame, the same deals carry rising reputational risk for brands in contested sectors.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Next time a feel-good story about an athlete's benefactor appears, it's worth asking what business the benefactor is in — this analysis shows how consistently that detail drops out of the coverage.

**Caveats:** Single-case qualitative media analysis in one country, interpretive rather than causal, with no measurement of what audiences actually believed; no consumer data.

### 11. Consumers read a diverse workforce as a sign a company is also greener

**Journal:** *European Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-04
**Work ID:** W7172382856 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ejm-09-2024-0728

**The study:** Across three large-scale experiments run in the US and Europe, researchers varied information about the share of employees from underrepresented communities across all levels of a company and measured how consumers judged that firm's environmental commitment. Firms with more diverse workforces were rated as more committed to environmental stewardship, and the effect ran through perceived social responsibility — consumers appear to treat diversity as a general signal of a company's values. The halo was stronger for smaller firms than for large ones.

**Why it matters:** It suggests DEI disclosures and sustainability communications are not separate silos in consumers' minds: one can spill over into the other, which is both an opportunity and a reputational risk if the environmental substance is not there.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Smaller brands may get more credit per unit of diversity disclosure than large ones; test whether combining workforce-composition reporting with environmental claims lifts perceived credibility in your category — and consider the flip side, that a diversity halo without real environmental performance invites greenwashing accusations.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A useful reminder for shoppers: we infer a lot about a company from one visible trait. Knowing a firm employs a diverse workforce says nothing about its carbon footprint, yet our brains quietly fill in the blank.

**Caveats:** Experiments with consumer participants reacting to firm descriptions rather than observed purchases; perception measures rather than behavior; US and European samples only.

### 12. Students will eat the climate-friendly lunch — just don't call it a vegetarian day

**Journal:** *Appetite* | **Published:** 2026-09-23
**Work ID:** W7214091952 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.appet.2026.108793

**The study:** A mixed-methods study surveyed 1,268 students across six Swedish schools and ran group discussions about climate-friendly school meals. Reformulating recipes to cut carbon (for example, lowering the meat content or swapping ingredients) was clearly more acceptable than all-vegetarian days, even though students accurately understood that red meat has a far bigger footprint than plant-based food. Acceptance hinged on taste, satiety and familiarity; meat was treated as the normative core of a proper meal.

**Why it matters:** For anyone selling or serving lower-carbon food, quietly reformulating beats labeling a day as meat-free — knowledge of climate impact does not translate into acceptance when the dish feels unfamiliar or insufficiently filling.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Food brands and foodservice operators can test stealth reformulation against explicit meat-free positioning: the same carbon reduction may land very differently depending on whether it is framed as a restriction or simply as a dish. Taste and satiety claims likely do more work than sustainability messaging.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Young people already know red meat is the climate problem — and still resist vegetarian days. It is a vivid case of knowing one thing and wanting another, and of how deeply meat is wired into what counts as a 'real meal'.

**Caveats:** Single country (Sweden) and school context; attitudes and perceptions rather than measured plate waste or intake; girls and frequent vegetarian-meal choosers drive much of the positive attitude, limiting generalizability.

### 13. Inside five agencies rewiring creative work around generative AI

**Journal:** *Journal of Advertising* | **Published:** 2026-09-30
**Work ID:** W7214980150 | **DOI:** 10.1080/00913367.2026.2733981

**The study:** Researchers conducted an inductive multi-case study of five advertising organizations, including an established agency and an AI-native startup, examining how they restructure creative production around generative AI. They identify four collaboration modes — Augmentation, Automation, Agentic and Alliance — mapped along AI autonomy and how deeply AI is embedded in the process, and two evolutionary routes firms take (Process Integration versus Native Innovation). Teams that performed best switched fluidly between modes within a single job rather than committing to one.

**Why it matters:** It gives creative leaders a concrete vocabulary for the choice they are actually making — how much autonomy to hand the model, and at which stage — instead of a blanket 'adopt AI' mandate.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit where AI sits in each stage of your creative process and whether your teams can move between modes mid-project; the paper's core tension is that generative models produce novelty cheaply but drift from brand strategy and execution requirements, so the governance question is where human strategic discipline re-enters.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The ads you see are increasingly produced by hybrid human-machine workflows, and the differences between agencies are less about whether they use AI than about how much latitude they give it.

**Caveats:** Qualitative case study of five organizations; no outcome data on creative effectiveness, cost or client results; framework is descriptive and not tested against performance.

### 14. Young shoppers don't blame themselves for unsustainable shopping — they blame the system

**Journal:** *International Journal of Retail & Distribution Management* | **Published:** 2026-09-17
**Work ID:** W7213447462 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ijrdm-01-2026-0044

**The study:** Researchers collected and inductively coded 343 written narratives from US university students about how they see sustainable retail today, where they think it is heading, and where they would like it to go. Students consistently described sustainability not as a personal purchase decision but as a coordination problem spanning retailers, suppliers, landlords and government — and reported that price, availability and store operations block them from acting on generally favorable attitudes. "Policy" emerged from the narratives as an additional strategic dimension alongside the conventional marketing mix, producing an "8Ps" organizing framework.

**Why it matters:** The familiar green attitude–behavior gap is usually treated as a motivation problem to be solved with persuasion; this sample locates it instead in price, assortment and infrastructure, which are retailer and policy levers rather than messaging levers.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If young consumers read sustainability failures as system failures, sustainability communications that put the onus on shopper choices may land badly. The testable question for retailers is whether visible structural moves — price parity on sustainable lines, guaranteed availability, take-back logistics, public policy advocacy — shift perceptions more than campaign messaging does; this exploratory study flags the hypothesis rather than proving the effect.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For shoppers, it is validation of a common frustration: the reason the sustainable option often loses is that it costs more and is frequently out of stock, not that people lack good intentions.

**Caveats:** Interpretive, exploratory qualitative study with no causal claims; single-country sample of young, university-educated US students writing narratives rather than making real purchases; the authors themselves note the "coordination" reading needs confirmatory interview-based and experimental follow-up.

### 15. The invisible labor of shopping for healthcare across a border

**Journal:** *Journal of Service Research* | **Published:** 2026-08-26
**Work ID:** W7204439549 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10946705261474264

**The study:** Through multimethod ethnography — in-depth interviews, participant observation and online diaries — researchers followed Hispanic women in the US–Mexico borderlands as they coordinated their households' healthcare across two national systems. The women built and deliberately deployed navigational, social, familial and linguistic resources ("cultural health capital") at distinct phases: before seeking care, while choosing and using providers, and afterward. The strategies worked, but arose from constraint and never fully removed exposure to risk.

**Why it matters:** It documents the substantial unpaid expertise and effort consumers in marginalized communities supply to make fragmented services usable — a reminder that "customer journeys" in services like health, insurance and finance are often completed by customers' own labor.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For service designers, payers and providers: the study maps exactly where households are absorbing coordination work that the service system is offloading, phase by phase. A practical angle is auditing which journey stages in your own category depend on customers' language skills, family networks and improvised navigation — and whether that dependency is quietly rationing access.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A window into how cross-border families actually manage medical care — comparison-shopping across two countries, interpreting for relatives, stitching together records — and why cost savings in that system come bundled with real risk.

**Caveats:** Qualitative ethnography in one specific geographic and cultural context (US–Mexico borderlands, Hispanic women); findings are interpretive and not generalizable or quantified; no measure of health or financial outcomes.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- The post-purchase blind spot in "less is more" sustainability: "Thinner packaging saves material — and ends up in the trash instead of the recycling bin" (W7214074641) supplies a consumer-side mechanism for the credit gap documented last time in "Consumers reward brands for adding green features — and barely notice when they take bad stuff away" (W7212134127). Subtraction not only goes unrewarded; here it actively misleads disposal behavior.
- Green attributes earn approval, not money. "Shoppers want recycled packaging — but will pay extra only for the plastic seal that proves it's not a fake" (W7172534656) repeats the pattern priced out in "Consumers will pay more for 'sustainable' beer — but Fair Trade labels actually cost you money" (W7204076930) — and matches that study's reuse-beats-recyclability finding, since in both cases the attribute doing commercial work is functional trust, not environmental substance.
- Sustainability is rarely the live motive in resale. "Secondhand clothing subscription boxes: most success factors are hurdles you must clear, not levers you can pull" (W7213931215) lines up with "Religious young shoppers buy secondhand clothes for the thrill and the status, not out of moral duty" (W7213254319): adoption hinges on hygiene-factor thresholds and self-interested payoffs, not moral framing.
- Knowledge does not buy acceptance in food. "Students will eat the climate-friendly lunch — just don't call it a vegetarian day" (W7214091952) extends "Italy's food future: algae and plant protein get a hearing, insects and lab-grown meat don't" (W7211939433) — taste, familiarity and disgust/satiety dominate, while accurate climate knowledge does not move acceptance.

**Advances**
- From CSR-as-signal to CSR-as-monitored. "When big investors spread themselves thin, companies' social responsibility slips" (W7214208660) moves the governance thread beyond "Tying executive bonuses to ESG goals appears to lower a company's reputational risk" (W7213035748) and "When CSR looks like compliance, it can damage a firm's marketing muscle" (W7212064195): the new work locates the weakness in investor attention capacity, and finds that financially immaterial CSR dimensions slip first.
- Luxury sustainability gets a mechanism. "Visible sustainability can make luxury look less fashionable — unless the brand frames it as a design choice" (W7215083585) advances "Wanting to stand out doesn't make luxury shoppers buy green — unless they already see themselves as green" (W7204951358) by shifting from who converts (green self-identity) to how the product is read (designer authorship versus aesthetic compromise).
- Greenwashing audits, now observed in the wild. "Why green influencers get called out — and why followers keep watching anyway" (W7214725050) takes the self-scrutiny described in "Inside zero-waste living: the exhausting self-audit behind 'responsible' consumption" (W7211935268) and shows it performed publicly on other people — with the practical prescription (verified outcomes, stated conditions) echoing the single-frame discipline of "Say what you did or what you achieved — but don't do both in the same sustainability post" (W7170105320).

**Counterpoints**
- Identity appeals lose to authority. "'Experts recommend it' beat 'you said you cared' — in both Ecuador and Norway" (W7214115221) cuts against the identity-and-values route emphasized by "Wanting to stand out doesn't make luxury shoppers buy green" (W7204951358) and the value-alignment pathway in "Does corporate social responsibility make people consume better — or just consume more guilt-free?" (W7212036936). Its null culture moderation also sits awkwardly with that meta-analysis's collectivism moderator and its "glocalized" CSR recommendation — though it chimes with the cross-country stability of sustainability motives in "From Nairobi to Norway, two food motives show up everywhere" (W7208710969).
- Consumers over-credit as readily as they under-credit. "Consumers read a diverse workforce as a sign a company is also greener" (W7172382856) qualifies the credit-gap story: invisible subtractions earn nothing, yet an entirely unrelated visible trait earns environmental credit the firm may not have earned.
- Who is to blame for the attitude–behavior gap. "Young shoppers don't blame themselves for unsustainable shopping — they blame the system" (W7213447462) is the mirror image of the Danish zero-wasters in W7211935268, who internalize responsibility to the point of exhaustion. Same gap, opposite attribution — and a direct challenge to persuasion-led remedies.

**New themes**
- Disposal and retention as the research object, not purchase: "Why people hang onto things they no longer use — and what that means for the circular economy" (W7214159818), paired with W7214074641, has no counterpart in the previous digest.

- AI moves from consumer-facing novelty to production infrastructure: "Inside five agencies rewiring creative work around generative AI" (W7214980150) replaces the previous run's consumer-reaction framing in "AI clones of famous people leave fans cold" (W7213257365) and the adoption framing in "Small firms that trust their partners most are the slowest to adopt AI" (W7213367223).

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Four studies in this batch relocate the sustainability problem from the moment of purchase to everything that happens after it. "Thinner packaging saves material — and ends up in the trash instead of the recycling bin" (W7214074641) shows that flimsier packs read as low-value and get binned rather than recycled; "Why people hang onto things they no longer use" (W7214159818) finds keep-or-toss decisions driven by security and autonomy rather than environmental attitudes; "Secondhand clothing subscription boxes" (W7213931215) finds resale adoption capped by non-negotiable thresholds; and "Shoppers want recycled packaging — but will pay extra only for the plastic seal that proves it's not a fake" (W7172534656) shows recycled content earning approval but no premium while trust-signaling plastic earns one. Taken together: companies are optimizing material inputs while the decisive behavior sits downstream, where consumers are reasoning about value, safety and control.

**Why pitch this now:** Extended producer responsibility and packaging rules now arriving in US states and in Europe put recyclability targets and on-pack labeling obligations onto brands at the same moment material-cost pressure is pushing them to lightweight — the exact combination this evidence says can backfire at the curb.

**Angle:** A reported feature on the last mile of the circular economy: why the recycling bin, the junk drawer and the resale cart are where corporate sustainability math quietly breaks, and what packaging, retail and resale teams can actually test there. Written for packaging and retail decision-makers, with a consumer-facing thread on how we judge what is worth keeping.

**Potential headlines:**
- Lighter packaging is more likely to end up in the trash
- Companies spent a decade using less material. Consumers started throwing more of it away.
- The circular economy keeps failing in your kitchen, not in the factory

**Potential outlets:**
- **Packaging Dive** — lightweighting versus recyclability is its core beat, and this is new evidence that the two goals can work against each other at disposal.
- **Retail Dive** — covers resale and take-back program economics; the bottleneck-versus-driver finding is a direct operational argument about where to spend.
- **Grist** — runs consumer-facing recycling and waste reporting and would take the "your bin is the weak link, and that's by design" framing for a general audience.
- **Marketing Brew** — the on-pack cue angle (how a package's feel shapes whether it gets recycled) is a tight, behavioral read for brand-side readers.

## Story Ideas by Study

- **Thinner packaging saves material — and ends up in the trash instead of the recycling bin (Work ID W7214074641):** Material savings on the factory floor can be cancelled at the curb, because flimsy packs read as worthless — a packaging trade explainer, with a consumer version on how we judge a package by its heft.
- **Shoppers want recycled packaging — but will pay extra only for the plastic seal that proves it's not a fake (Work ID W7172534656):** Anti-counterfeit anxiety is what plastic-heavy e-commerce packaging is really selling, so the route to less of it runs through verified-seller trust — for an e-commerce or packaging trade piece.
- **When big investors spread themselves thin, companies' social responsibility slips (Work ID W7214208660):** ESG oversight is a function of investor attention bandwidth, and immaterial commitments go unwatched first — for a business or governance section, or an investor-relations/corporate-affairs trade.
- **Why green influencers get called out — and why followers keep watching anyway (Work ID W7214725050):** Audiences run their own greenwashing audits in the comments, and what triggers backlash is theatricality rather than imperfection — for a creator-economy or influencer-marketing trade feature.
- **Visible sustainability can make luxury look less fashionable — unless the brand frames it as a design choice (Work ID W7215083585):** Upcycled luxury is judged on whether the designer chose the look or was stuck with it — for a fashion-business trade piece or a luxury-marketing column.
- **Why people hang onto things they no longer use (Work ID W7214159818):** Security and autonomy, not eco-guilt, govern the keep-or-toss decision — a message brief for take-back and repair programs, and a consumer-psychology explainer on the junk drawer for a general-interest outlet.

- **"Experts recommend it" beat "you said you cared" — in both Ecuador and Norway (Work ID W7214115221):** A preregistered field test where expert endorsement outperformed values-and-identity copy, and worked identically in two very different markets — for a marketing-trade news brief or an effectiveness column.
