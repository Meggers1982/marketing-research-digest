### Study 3: Infinite scroll sells — and then gets returned
**Work ID:** W7214126722 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Intention reported as behavior** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Shoppers browsing infinite-scroll layouts were more likely to return what they bought."
- **Abstract says:** "an infinite-scrolling (versus paginated) layout increases consumers' tendency to return purchased products... thereby increasing product return intentions"
- **Problem:** The body sentence reads as observed return behavior, whereas the abstract's stated dependent measure is return intentions; the digest's own caveat acknowledges this, so the body text is slightly out of step with it.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say "were more likely to say they would return what they bought" or "showed higher return intentions.""

**Issue B. Paraphrase of an intervention** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "or making return costs salient reduced the effect"
- **Abstract says:** "and accounting for the cost of product returns"
- **Problem:** "Making return costs salient" is one plausible reading of "accounting for the cost of product returns," but the abstract does not specify that the intervention was salience of costs to shoppers.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use the abstract's wording, e.g. "and accounting for return costs," or mark the interpretation as inferred."

### Study 4: More photos, fewer bookings: why extra visuals hurt experience sales
**Work ID:** W7213493969 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline implies booking behavior** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "More photos, fewer bookings: why extra visuals hurt experience sales"
- **Abstract says:** "Too much of a good thing: Promotional visuals and their effect on buying intentions of experiences... consumers are less inclined to buy an experiential product when it is promoted with many visuals rather than a few"
- **Problem:** The paper's outcome is buying intentions (plus newsletter follow-up and a choice task), not bookings or sales revenue.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as "More photos, less buying intent" or note that outcomes were intentions and choice, not booked sales."

**Issue B. Category list changed** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "an experiential product — vacations, dining, attractions —"
- **Abstract says:** "experiential products (such as vacations, dining out, and sports equipment)"
- **Problem:** "Attractions" replaces the abstract's "sports equipment" in the list of example categories.
- **Suggested fix:** "Match the abstract's examples: vacations, dining out and sports equipment."

**Issue C. Mechanism inference stated as finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "suggesting redundancy rather than volume is the problem"
- **Abstract says:** "Study 2 identifies a boundary condition—heterogeneous extensive sets eliminate the effect."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports only that heterogeneous large sets eliminate the effect; it does not test or claim redundancy as the underlying driver.
- **Suggested fix:** "Present the redundancy explanation explicitly as the digest's interpretation, e.g. "which hints that similarity, not sheer volume, may be what hurts.""

### Study 5: Virtual influencers get more traction when they're pictured hanging out with real people
**Work ID:** W4318015176 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Attenuation described as elimination** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "and disappeared when the companion was merely present, was another virtual entity, or when the poster was a real human influencer"
- **Abstract says:** "but are attenuated when the human other is merely present, when the companion is another virtual entity, and when the poster is a human influencer"
- **Problem:** The abstract says the effects are attenuated (weakened), not that they vanish entirely; the story angle then builds on this by asserting "no lift" for human influencers.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "were weaker when..." and soften the story angle to "offers less lift for human influencers.""

### Study 6: Tearjerker or revenge flick? Culture predicts which mixed emotions sell

### Study 7: Consumers will pay more for low-carbon milk — but a big chunk of them dislike 'organic'
**Work ID:** W7213546298 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Unsupported segment size** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "but a big chunk of them dislike 'organic'"
- **Abstract says:** "a lower-socioeconomic-status, less environmentally engaged segment, which actually exhibits disutility from organic production"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports no segment shares, so "a big chunk" implies a magnitude the abstract does not provide.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say "one consumer segment shows disutility from the organic label" without quantifying its size."

**Issue B. Hedge dropped on education finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Education was the main driver of the organic premium; environmental attitudes drove the premium for emissions reductions."
- **Abstract says:** "A secondary analysis suggests that education is the main determinant of increased WTP for organic production, while environmental attitudes likely drive WTP associated with GHG reductions."
- **Problem:** The abstract presents these as suggestive results from a secondary analysis ("suggests," "likely"); the digest states them as established.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add the hedge: "A secondary analysis suggests education was the main determinant...""

**Issue C. Extrapolation to market outcomes** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "which helps explain why sustainable products stall outside affluent zip codes"
- **Abstract says:** "We find evidence of heterogeneity in preferences and conduct several market segment analyses."
- **Problem:** The study measures stated preferences in a hypothetical choice experiment; it says nothing about actual sales performance by geography.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as a hypothesis: "which may be one reason sustainability claims land differently across income groups.""

### Study 8: Sticky seats beat beer lists: what actually drives the choice of pub
**Work ID:** W7172190947 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Unsupported ranking of attributes** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Cleanliness and physical environment factors surfaced as decisive alongside drink range"
- **Abstract says:** "This study investigates how consumers trade-off between various features when making pub choices... examining analysis of product, physical and service elements"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports that trade-offs among product, physical and service elements were examined and that two segments emerged, but it does not report cleanliness or physical environment as decisive; the only cue is the title quotation.
- **Suggested fix:** "State only what the abstract supports — trade-offs across product, physical and service features and two psychographic segments — and note the cleanliness emphasis is inferred from the title quote."

**Issue B. "Why it matters" overreaches** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "this suggests the physical environment and inclusivity of the space are doing more segmentation work than the product lineup"
- **Abstract says:** "offering a novel, structured segmentation that moves beyond outdated stereotypes, notably highlighting the growing influence of women and families in pubs as well as non-beer drinkers"
- **Problem:** No comparative weighting of physical environment versus product range appears in the abstract; Q-methodology also yields subjective viewpoint patterns rather than importance rankings generalizable to a market.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rewrite as "this suggests physical environment and inclusivity deserve attention alongside drinks range in how operators segment customers.""

**Issue C. Country not stated in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "single country (UK) and single category"
- **Abstract says:** "(no country is named in the abstract)"
- **Problem:** The UK setting is a reasonable inference from the pub context but is not stated in the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "Mark as "Unverifiable from abstract only" or write "the abstract does not name the country studied.""

**Issue B. Institution described as governmental** — Severity: Minor

- **Problem:** The abstract says "official" rather than "government"; in the Chinese context the inference is plausible but not stated.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "official agricultural research institute" as in the abstract."

### Study 13: Put a face in the first five seconds and fewer people watch your ad
**Work ID:** W7212428236 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal headline on observational data** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Put a face in the first five seconds and fewer people watch your ad"
- **Abstract says:** "Across product categories, we find that a lesser percentage of facial presence before the skip option is associated with greater video view counts."

- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as association, e.g. "Ads with faces before the skip button draw fewer completed views"."

**Issue B. "Five seconds" not stated in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "run A/B variants of the same spot with and without a human face in the first five seconds"
- **Abstract says:** "a lesser percentage of facial presence before the skip option"
- **Problem:** The abstract refers only to the portion "before the skip option" and never specifies a five-second window. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say "before the skip button appears" rather than "in the first five seconds"."

### Study 16: Your AI rollout will live or die with the middle manager
**Work ID:** W7171543795 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Overstated framing of where the bottleneck lies** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "It locates the main bottleneck in frontline AI investment not in the technology or in staff resistance but in the readiness of the managers translating strategy into practice."
- **Abstract says:** "Frontline managers are critical conduits of top-down AI initiatives ... little research explores their role as catalysts or bottlenecks in such implementation initiatives."
- **Problem:** The abstract positions managers as potential catalysts or bottlenecks; it makes no comparison against technology or staff resistance, so declaring managers "the main bottleneck" is a step beyond the qualitative findings.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say the study points to manager AI readiness as a key, under-examined determinant of implementation outcomes."

**Issue B. "Middle manager" versus "frontline manager"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Your AI rollout will live or die with the middle manager"
- **Abstract says:** "we tap into the mental models of 51 frontline managers and top management executives tasked with implementing AI systems in the frontlines"
- **Problem:** The informants are frontline managers and top executives, not "middle managers"; the headline also asserts a make-or-break outcome not tested quantitatively.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "frontline manager" and soften to something like "Frontline managers shape how AI actually gets implemented"."

### Study 20: Make people suffer a little: painful charity events boost perceived authenticity of cause marketing
**Work ID:** W7204997257 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Dropped nuance: motive effect held across all pain levels** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "A public-serving motive paired with an effortful event (a marathon) produced the highest perceived authenticity and support compared with self-serving motives or low-effort events"
- **Abstract says:** "The effect of public-serving motive on CRM authenticity was significant across all perceived pain and calling conditions but was strongest in the marathon/high-calling condition."
- **Problem:** Experiment 2 found the public-serving motive raised authenticity regardless of how much effort the event demanded; effort only amplified it. Presenting effort as the condition for authenticity, as the headline and "Why it matters" do, could lead marketers to add friction when the motive framing is doing most of the work.
- **Suggested fix:** "Note that public-serving framing lifted authenticity in every condition, with the effortful marathon/high-calling condition producing the largest effect."

**Issue B. Prescriptive headline from two experiments** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Make people suffer a little: painful charity events boost perceived authenticity of cause marketing"
- **Abstract says:** "Experiment 1 demonstrated that the public-serving CRM motive, paired with a CRM event that requires pain (i.e., a marathon), resulted in greater perceived CRM authenticity and supporting behaviors than other conditions."
- **Problem:** The imperative headline turns two scenario experiments on a single cause into a directive playbook, and "boost" implies an established real-world lift when outcomes were stated support measures.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as a finding, e.g. "Effortful charity events were seen as more authentic — when the motive was public-serving"."
