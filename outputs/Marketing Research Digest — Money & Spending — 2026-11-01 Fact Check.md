### Study 1: "It's customary to leave a tip" beats appeals to reciprocity — and doesn't scare customers away
**Work ID:** W7224889302 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Mechanism not tested in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Tipping prompts work on us largely because they signal what other people do"
- **Abstract says:** "Overall, the norms message increased tipping the most, and did so without reducing returning demand"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports which appeal performed best, not why; attributing the effect to social-signalling is an inference about mechanism that the abstract does not support.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "Norm-based wording outperformed reciprocity appeals here — a reminder that 'customary' is a powerful word" without asserting the psychological mechanism."

**Issue B. Unverifiable caveat detail** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Single platform (a global freelance marketplace) and a single service category"
- **Abstract says:** "field experiments ... in a global freelance marketplace"
- **Problem:** The abstract does not say the experiments were confined to one service category; a freelance marketplace typically spans many. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Drop "and a single service category" or mark it as not specified in the abstract."

### Study 2: Charging a penny for the second item beats giving it away free
**Work ID:** W7223666241 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Complementary" substituted for "nonidentical"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "note the finding that the bonus item being identical versus complementary flips the long-run effect"
- **Abstract says:** "when the two items are identical, consumers who encounter a token-price (vs freebie) promotion express stronger value discounting for the focal item. This effect reverses when the items are nonidentical. ... Future research should examine whether the bundle promotional format effect differs with ... the complementarity of the focal and supplemental items"
- **Problem:** The tested contrast was identical vs nonidentical items; complementarity is explicitly flagged as an open question for future research, so calling the second condition "complementary" misstates what was manipulated.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change "complementary" to "a different (nonidentical) item"."

**Issue B. Lab result generalized to shoppers** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Shoppers often respond more to a token price"
- **Abstract says:** "Two imaginary-scenario and two incentive-compatible experiments test the hypotheses."
- **Problem:** Findings come from scenario and lab-style experiments, not retail behavior; "shoppers often respond" implies a general field pattern.
- **Suggested fix:** "Write "In these experiments, participants rated token-price offers as more attractive than freebies.""

### Study 3: Advice from friends pushes people into crypto; advice from professionals pulls them out
**Work ID:** W7221884200 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal phrasing for cross-sectional associations** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Advice from friends pushes people into crypto; advice from professionals pulls them out"
- **Abstract says:** "personal advice from family and friends is positively associated with cryptocurrency investment likelihood, whereas professional advice is negatively associated with it"
- **Problem:** Headline and "Why it matters" ("maps who actually moves retail investors", "is what makes clients tune out") assert causation from pooled cross-sectional survey data, contradicting the digest's own caveat.
- **Suggested fix:** "Soften to "is linked to"/"goes with" in the headline and "Why it matters", keeping the body's associational wording."

### Study 4: Malls are becoming schedules, not maps — and the order of activities changes what shoppers buy
**Work ID:** W7221500893 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Order effect and purchase outcome overstated** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "the order of activities changes what shoppers buy"
- **Abstract says:** "consumers may prefer to undertake experiential activities in particular, asymmetric orders. ... An experiment in which participants schedule a visit to their city center illustrates and supports the role of experiential activities in determining consumers' store choices."
- **Problem:** Asymmetric ordering is presented in the abstract as a theoretical proposition, and the empirical support concerns which stores are chosen in a hypothetical schedule — not what consumers buy.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as "experiential activities shape which stores get included in a planned visit" and present the ordering claim as theory rather than a demonstrated purchase effect."

**Issue B. "Anchors" for "activities"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "follow-up simulations tested how store choices would shift if specific shopping or entertainment anchors were added"
- **Abstract says:** "Follow up simulations explore how store choices would change when specific shopping or entertainment activities are added to the study area."
- **Problem:** The abstract refers to adding activities to the study area, not anchor tenants; "anchors" implies a specific retail-property construct not tested.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "specific shopping or entertainment activities"."

### Study 6: When the app recommends you: what algorithmic visibility actually does for a local restaurant
**Work ID:** W7223381933 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Only" overstates "primarily"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Exposure helped geographically disadvantaged restaurants most—but only at the discovery stage"
- **Abstract says:** "the estimated exposure effect is larger for geographically disadvantaged restaurants, but primarily at the discovery stage (clicks)"
- **Problem:** "Only" implies no benefit beyond clicks, whereas the abstract says the advantage was primarily — not exclusively — at discovery.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change "only at the discovery stage" to "mainly at the discovery stage"."

**Issue B. Country inferred** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "largely a single country (Korea, with the revenue evidence limited to a restricted Seoul credit-card subsample)"
- **Abstract says:** "Auxiliary credit-card sales data from a restricted Seoul subsample"
- **Problem:** The abstract names only the Seoul subsample; the country of the full 16,720-restaurant sample is not stated. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Note that only the Seoul subsample is identified by location in the abstract."

### Study 8: The lifetime price tag of smoking, and what quitting at 25 versus 65 is worth
**Work ID:** W7223008621 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Multiplier wrong for women** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "the steep age gradient suggests the persuasion budget belongs with the youngest smokers, where the economic return per convert is roughly ten to fifteen times higher"
- **Abstract says:** "for males, these ranged from THB 0.04 million (quitting at age 65 years) to THB 0.58 million (quitting at age 25 years). Among females, individuals who quit smoking at the same ages could save THB 0.19 million and THB 0.67 million, respectively."
- **Problem:** The 14.5x ratio applies to men only; for women the ratio of savings at 25 versus 65 is about 3.5x, so the stated range misrepresents the gradient for half the modelled population.
- **Suggested fix:** "State the gradient separately: roughly 14x for men and about 3.5x for women."

### Study 9: People are falling for AI companions — but they aren't letting them do the shopping
**Work ID:** W7223447670 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Absent functionality recast as user refusal** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "they still draw a line at taking its shopping advice"
- **Abstract says:** "no app positions shopping as a core feature ... design-weighted coding estimates 9.1% of corpus posts contain commercial discourse, but only 0.3% involve the companion itself in consumer or purchasing decisions"
- **Problem:** The low 0.3% figure reflects that shopping features are not built into these apps, not evidence that users reject them; the headline and consumer angle imply a consumer choice the data cannot show (the marketer angle notes this, so the two sections conflict).
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as "companion apps haven't built shopping in yet" rather than "users won't let them shop"."

**Issue B. Google Trends result smoothed** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Attention to both relational AI and AI commerce rose sharply and moved together"
- **Abstract says:** "relational-AI attention rises significantly in five countries, AI-commerce attention rises in all six, with substantial but non-invariant temporal co-movement in attention"
- **Problem:** Drops that relational-AI attention rose significantly in only five of six markets and that co-movement was non-invariant across markets.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add "in five of six markets" and note that co-movement varied by market."

**Issue C. "Millions" of paying users** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "millions of people are paying subscriptions and in-app fees to talk to an artificial partner"
- **Abstract says:** "the category shows substantial downloads and spending ... in-app purchasing is universal"
- **Problem:** The abstract quantifies neither users nor payers; "millions ... paying" is an inference. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say "the category shows substantial downloads and spending, with in-app purchasing available across every app examined"."
