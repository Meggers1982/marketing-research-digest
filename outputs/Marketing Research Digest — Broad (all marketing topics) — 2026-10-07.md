### 1. The thank-you note that costs you money — and the one that makes you money

**Journal:** *Marketing Science* | **Published:** 2026-09-25
**Work ID:** W7214276420 | **DOI:** 10.1287/mksc.2025.0394

**The study:** Researchers ran a field experiment with 35,077 prior donors to a major Indian charity, randomly assigning thank-you letters that either included a direct call to action (an explicit ask to give again) or were otherwise identical without one. Overall response was low (0.69% gave), but the effect of the call to action depended entirely on where the donor was in their giving cycle: among those who had not yet given in the current cycle, the ask significantly raised donation amounts, while among those who had already given, estimates pointed to lower giving. Aggregate results masked these two opposing effects.

**Why it matters:** A single, already-available data field — whether the donor has given yet this cycle — determines whether an ask in a gratitude message adds revenue or destroys it, and averaging across all donors hides that entirely.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Fundraisers and CRM teams should test splitting thank-you messaging by recency rather than running one template: this real-world experiment suggests the same creative can lift one segment and depress another, which means A/B tests reported only in aggregate may be systematically misleading. The broader lesson travels beyond charity to any repeat-purchase or renewal communication.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It gives a name to a familiar irritation: being asked for money again moments after you just gave. The data suggests donors have an internal sense of appropriate cadence, and organizations that violate it pay for the intrusion.

**Caveats:** A single charity in a single country (India) using direct mail; overall response rates were very low, and the drop among already-given donors was a point estimate within a significant interaction rather than a standalone effect.

### 2. Swap a vague word for a precise one and the product starts to feel one of a kind

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-10-04
**Work ID:** W7219889147 | **DOI:** 10.1002/jcpy.70041

**The study:** Across four experiments, researchers tested what happens when product descriptions use more precise words (terms sitting deeper in their semantic category and covering a narrower set of referents) instead of broader, more general ones. More precise wording consistently raised perceptions that the product was unique, and the authors trace the effect to categorical depth rather than to the words simply sounding more impressive. Perceived uniqueness in turn fed into attractiveness and purchase intention, most strongly among consumers with a high need for unique products.

**Why it matters:** It identifies a nearly costless copywriting lever — word choice at the lexical level — that shifts how differentiated a product seems, which matters most in crowded categories where every listing reads the same.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Worth testing in live product pages, ad copy and packaging: replace generic category words with narrower, more specific ones and watch uniqueness-related metrics, especially for products targeted at customers who want to stand out. Because these are controlled experiments, treat it as a hypothesis for live testing rather than a guaranteed conversion lift.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A quiet reminder that the words on a label do work on you. 'Loafer' rather than 'shoe,' 'sauvignon blanc' rather than 'wine' — the specificity itself signals rarity, whether or not the product is actually rarer.

**Caveats:** Four controlled experiments rather than field data; outcomes are perceptions and behavioral intentions rather than observed purchases; sample details and country not specified in the abstract.

### 3. Why luxury logos feature panthers and eagles rather than rabbits

**Journal:** *European Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-24
**Work ID:** W7214201194 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ejm-07-2025-0615

**The study:** Using seven studies — an exploratory study, an eye-tracking study and five controlled experiments — researchers tested how the type of animal in a luxury brand's logo shapes consumer response. Animals whose visual design conveys competence (think predators and powerful creatures) were preferred over those conveying warmth, and the effect ran through a heightened sense of perceived dominance in the consumer. The advantage disappeared when the brand's message used a cooperative rather than competitive appeal.

**Why it matters:** Logo imagery and ad copy interact: a competence-signaling animal mark only pays off when the surrounding message is framed competitively, which means visual identity and campaign tone need to be decided together rather than in separate briefs.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For luxury and premium brands, this is a prompt to audit whether brand iconography and current campaign messaging are pulling in the same direction — the research suggests a mismatch neutralizes the logo's effect. As lab and eye-tracking work, it indicates a mechanism to test in market, not a rule to redesign around.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Luxury buying is partly about feeling powerful, and brands know it. The fierce animal on the bag isn't decoration — it's doing psychological work on the person carrying it.

**Caveats:** Experimental and eye-tracking studies rather than sales data; responses are likely stated preferences and intentions rather than real luxury purchases; sample composition and country not reported in the abstract.

### 4. Why paying your friend back beats splitting the bill at the register

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Research* | **Published:** 2026-08-19
**Work ID:** W7203919913 | **DOI:** 10.1093/jcr/ucag030

**The study:** Across eight preregistered experiments, researchers compared two ways of settling a shared expense — each person paying the vendor directly versus one person covering the total and the others reimbursing them. Indirect payment through another consumer consistently produced higher satisfaction with the purchase, whether the co-payer was a close friend, an acquaintance or someone the participant disliked, and whether or not the shares were equal. The mechanism was relief from clearing a social debt: the effect vanished when the financial obligation was made explicit, or when the focal person had fronted the money themselves.

**Why it matters:** Satisfaction with the same meal, ride or trip changes based purely on the plumbing of the payment — a lever restaurants, travel operators and payment apps can pull at essentially zero cost.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Operators of group-consumption categories (restaurants, ride-hailing, travel, ticketing) could test whether nudging one person to pay the whole tab — with Venmo-style reimbursement afterward — lifts post-purchase satisfaction and repeat rates, instead of defaulting to split-the-check functionality. These are controlled experiments, so the next step is a live A/B test in a real checkout flow.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The dinner-table etiquette angle: paying your friend back later feels better than tapping your own card at the terminal, because the good feeling comes from discharging a social obligation, not from the meal. Worth knowing if you budget by what feels painless — the cheerful route can also be the one that obscures what you actually spent.

**Caveats:** Experimental studies, likely online panels, with scenario-based or lab-style shared expenses rather than observed real-world group spending; sample sizes and nationality not specified in the abstract.

### 5. "Up to 40% better" or "30–50% better"? It depends on whether shoppers smell a sales pitch

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing Research* | **Published:** 2026-09-16
**Work ID:** W7213274554 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00222437261490921

**The study:** Six preregistered studies, including one field study, tested whether consumers prefer point estimates (a single performance number) or range estimates when comparing products. By default, shoppers preferred point estimates because a single figure conveys more certainty about what they will get. But once persuasion knowledge was activated — when people were primed to think about how a claim was being sold to them — the preference flipped: ranges signaled transparency about real-world variability, raised perceived trustworthiness, and won out. The effects faded when a product was evaluated alone rather than side by side.

**Why it matters:** Performance claims are everywhere — battery life, fuel economy, weight loss, skincare results — and this suggests the optimal format is not fixed but depends on how skeptical and comparison-minded the audience is at that moment.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands could test claim format by context: crisp point numbers on comparison pages and spec sheets where shoppers are in evaluation mode and not yet guarded, honest ranges in environments where skepticism runs high (reviews-heavy categories, previously over-promised products, regulated claims). The inclusion of a field study makes this more actionable than the usual lab-only format research, but it is still one set of categories.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A consumer-literacy point: a single precise-sounding number feels more reassuring than a range, even though the range is usually the more honest description of what a product will actually do for you. Our instinct rewards false precision.

**Caveats:** Mostly online experiments with comparative purchase scenarios; one field study, scope and partner unspecified; sample sizes and countries not reported in the abstract.

### 6. Doing both brand and customer management well pays off worse than picking one

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-09
**Work ID:** W7202044997 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00222429261478881

**The study:** Researchers ran automated text analysis on more than 95,000 earnings conference call transcripts from over 1,800 firms across 22 years, supplemented by manual coding and secondary financial data, to measure how heavily firms emphasize brand management versus customer management. The two foci turned out to be statistically uncorrelated — firms are not systematically trading one off against the other, nor pairing them. Each had distinct effects on revenue- and cost-side performance, and strikingly, a dual emphasis on both was less financially attractive than concentrating on one.

**Why it matters:** It puts hard longitudinal evidence into a long-running strategy argument and suggests that "we do both brand and CRM equally well" may be a costlier position than committing to one as the organizing logic.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** CMOs can use this to pressure-test budget splits and board narratives: where is the firm genuinely emphasizing brand, where customer management, and is the straddle diluting returns? The moderating environmental factors matter too — the right focus appears to be contingent on market conditions, not universal.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A window into how companies really talk about marketing: what executives say on quarterly earnings calls is a measurable record of strategy, and that record shows wildly different philosophies behind brands consumers encounter every day.

**Caveats:** Observational data from earnings-call language, which proxies stated emphasis rather than actual internal spending or capability; causal interpretation is limited; US-listed public firms skew the sample.

### 7. Heavy users of your free tier may be the hardest to convert — and overly habitual subscribers churn

**Journal:** *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* | **Published:** 2026-09-21
**Work ID:** W7213768857 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11747-026-01205-w

**The study:** Four field studies within a live freemium subscription service examined how expected premium benefits, benefits experienced during trials, and habit formation drive conversion and retention. Habits built on the free version proved double-edged: they strengthened expectations of what premium would deliver, boosting conversion, but also locked users into free usage through sheer inertia. Habits formed during premium use generally supported conversion and long-term retention — except in discounted trials, where the relationship was inverse-U-shaped and the most intensely habitual users retained worse than moderate ones.

**Why it matters:** Freemium operators often treat free-tier engagement as an unambiguous conversion signal; this evidence says engagement can entrench people in free, and that discounted-trial power users are a churn risk rather than a sure thing.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Subscription teams should segment by habit intensity, not just usage volume: test interventions that disrupt free-tier routine (feature gating, paywalled moments) for high-habit free users, and watch discount-trial heavy users as a distinct retention-risk cohort when the promotional price expires.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For consumers, it is a reminder that free tiers are engineered around routine — the more automatic your use becomes, the more the economics of the product are working on you, in both directions.

**Caveats:** Field studies within a single freemium service, so category and platform specificity limits generalization; the firm partner is not named in the abstract; observational field data means some relationships are correlational.

### 8. Can a Model Tell You Which Slogan People Will Actually Remember?

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing Research* | **Published:** 2026-08-21
**Work ID:** W7203945013 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00222437261481110

**The study:** Researchers assembled the proposed drivers of slogan memorability from a systematic literature review, then measured them with natural language processing, human ratings, and features extracted from text embeddings. They built predictive models of two outcomes for real, existing slogans: whether people recognize the line, and whether it cues the correct brand. The models were then used to score candidate slogans for new brands, and to test how much of memorability the published literature still fails to capture — text embeddings picked up textual characteristics the academic factors miss.

**Why it matters:** Slogan selection is usually settled by creative judgment and small focus groups; this offers a cheap, scalable pre-test that scores dozens of candidate lines before any money is spent on media.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Agencies and brand teams could pilot an automated memorability score as a first-pass filter on slogan shortlists, then validate the top candidates with conventional recall testing — worth checking whether the model's rankings line up with your own category's tracking data before trusting it.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The lines that lodge in your head are not random: measurable textual properties predict both whether you remember a slogan and whether you can name the brand behind it — and those are two different things. Plenty of famous slogans are remembered while the advertiser is forgotten.

**Caveats:** Predictive modeling on recognition and recall judgments rather than real-world ad exposure or sales; language and market coverage of the slogan set is not specified in the abstract; model performance on genuinely novel slogans is demonstrated but not validated against later market outcomes.

### 9. The Shoppers Who Can't Live With a Scratch — and Why They Keep Buying New

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Research* | **Published:** 2026-08-22
**Work ID:** W7201850894 | **DOI:** 10.1093/jcr/ucag034

**The study:** Across four qualitative studies, scale-development work, and eight experiments, researchers defined and measured a consumer trait they call the desire for perfect products (DPP), captured in an eight-item scale. High-DPP consumers hold richer and more demanding ideals, revise those ideals upward when they see something better, and react strongly to even minor flaws. Behaviorally, they apply more and stricter search filters, prefer newer products even when objective quality is unchanged, and are less willing to keep, repair, or donate flawed but still usable items.

**Why it matters:** It identifies a measurable customer segment that drives both premium upgrade demand and avoidable product disposal — and offers a specific messaging fix: reframing a flaw as a source of uniqueness softened high-DPP consumers' negative reactions.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands in refurbished, resale, repair, and open-box channels could test "uniqueness" framing on cosmetic imperfections rather than apologizing for them or discounting; retailers might also look at whether their most filter-heavy searchers are also their highest returners.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If you find yourself replacing things that still work because of a small blemish, this research names the trait — and shows the upgrade treadmill is partly self-inflicted, because seeing something better quietly raises the standard you hold everything else to.

**Caveats:** Eight experiments, mostly stimulus-based and likely online panel samples; the acquisition and disposal outcomes are experimental rather than observed in real households over time; intervention effects are short-horizon.

### 10. Bundle a Mug With a Folding Chair: The Odd-Couple Pairing That Makes Stuff Feel Like an Experience

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Research* | **Published:** 2026-08-23
**Work ID:** W7204125043 | **DOI:** 10.1093/jcr/ucag029

**The study:** Thirteen preregistered experiments tested how usage complementarity — how likely two products are to be used together — changes the way bundles are perceived. Bundling goods with moderate complementarity (a coffee mug and a folding chair) made consumers see the products as more experiential than bundling items with low or high complementarity, or than the same items sold separately. The mechanism was contextualization: a moderately related pair pushes people to imagine the setting where the items would be used rather than the items themselves, which raised purchase likelihood when shoppers were seeking an experience.

**Why it matters:** Experiences are consistently valued more highly than material goods, and this suggests retailers can borrow that halo for physical products simply by changing which items they pair, at essentially no cost.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Merchandisers and e-commerce teams could A/B test "moderately related" bundles against the conventional tightly matched pairing (mug plus coffee beans) — especially in gifting, outdoor, and lifestyle categories where buyers are shopping for an occasion rather than a function.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A mismatched pair of objects makes your brain fill in the scene — the campsite, the morning on the porch — and you end up buying the daydream along with the merchandise.

**Caveats:** All findings come from preregistered experiments rather than retail field data; purchase likelihood is a stated measure, not actual sales; samples are likely online panels; the effect is category- and occasion-dependent.

### 11. When Workers Unionize, Suppliers Quietly Tighten the Payment Terms

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing* | **Published:** 2026-09-01
**Work ID:** W7212292008 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretai.2026.09.001

**The study:** Researchers analyzed 1,378 National Labor Relations Board union elections at 909 U.S. public firms between 1977 and 2021, using a regression discontinuity design around narrowly won and narrowly lost votes. Unionization cut the firm's access to trade credit from suppliers by 4.2 percentage points — about $220.4 million for the average firm in the sample — and fewer suppliers subsequently named the unionized firm as a major customer. Three pre-election signals (product-market scope, product-market fluidity, and prior sales growth) amplified or dampened the supplier response.

**Why it matters:** It documents a real, large, and largely invisible cost of labor events: the supply chain repricing a customer's credit risk, which hits working capital without ever appearing in a labor negotiation.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Retail and consumer-goods executives facing organizing campaigns should treat supplier communication as part of the response plan — the findings suggest suppliers read pre-existing signals about growth and market position when deciding how much credit risk to absorb.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Union votes are usually covered as a story about wages and workers, but the financial ripple runs through vendors who deliver goods now and get paid later — and who adjust long before anything shows up on a shelf.

**Caveats:** U.S. public firms only, observational data with a regression discontinuity identification strategy; covers a long historical window in which credit markets and union dynamics changed substantially; says nothing about whether the credit tightening is justified by actual default risk.

### 12. Who You Publish With Beats Where You Publish

**Journal:** *International Journal of Research in Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-01
**Work ID:** W7211972867 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.ijresmar.2026.09.004

**The study:** Researchers built a longitudinal database of 134,558 marketing scholars spanning 1960 to 2020 and compared two routes to influence: publishing in top-tier journals ("universalist authority") versus co-authoring with people who sit at the center of the discipline's collaboration network ("social authority"). Citations were more strongly associated with social authority than with top-tier placement. But social authority came with a "ghetto effect": in subfields where scholars mostly collaborate among themselves, higher social authority was linked to fewer citations for that subfield's collective output.

**Why it matters:** It quantifies something academics suspect but rarely measure — that network position outperforms journal prestige as a predictor of impact — with implications for how universities and journals evaluate researchers whose work informs marketing practice.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Anyone who relies on academic marketing research — consultancies, insight teams, thought-leadership programs — should note that citation counts partly reflect who an author knows, and treat citation volume as a weaker quality signal than it looks.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The finding is a useful reminder that in almost any expert field, reputation is a social network effect as much as a merit ranking, and tight-knit communities can end up talking mainly to themselves.

**Caveats:** Correlational and observational — associations, not causal effects; single discipline (marketing) and a citation-based measure of impact that may not track real-world usefulness; network centrality and productivity are hard to disentangle.

### 13. "2 hours and 59 minutes" feels longer than "3 hours" — the hidden cost of adding a smaller time unit

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing Research* | **Published:** 2026-09-06
**Work ID:** W7210281977 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00222437261488297

**The study:** Across nine preregistered experiments plus observational data from an online course aggregator platform, researchers tested how time descriptions are phrased. Stating a duration or deadline with a larger unit plus a smaller one ("2 hours and 59 minutes", "by 8 PM on June 30") consistently made the time feel longer than the larger unit alone ("3 hours", "by the end of day on June 30"), because the extra unit pushes people to mentally break time into many small pieces. The effect held across date+hour, hour+minute and minute+second combinations and spilled over into course interest, judgments of how well a product works, goal setting, and trade-offs between time and money.

**Why it matters:** Delivery promises, service wait times, course lengths and treatment durations are written by someone making an arbitrary formatting choice — and that choice measurably shifts how long customers think they'll be waiting or working.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** An easy, cheap A/B test for any brand that quotes time: strip or add the second unit depending on whether you want the duration to feel short (a wait, a course commitment, a shipping window) or impressively long (how many hours a battery lasts, how long a treatment keeps working). The platform data suggest it can move real interest, but each category deserves its own test before rewriting every time field.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Your sense of how long something takes is partly a formatting artifact. The same wait feels longer written as "1 hour and 45 minutes" than as "under 2 hours" — useful to know when a quoted delivery or service window makes you balk.

**Caveats:** Most evidence comes from online experiments with hypothetical choices; the field component is a single course-aggregator platform in one category.

### 14. Livestream hosts sell more when they talk about touch, smell and taste — not what viewers can already see

**Journal:** *Asia Pacific Journal of Marketing and Logistics* | **Published:** 2026-09-23
**Work ID:** W7214088309 | **DOI:** 10.1108/apjml-05-2026-1355

**The study:** Researchers combined minute-level transaction data matched to livestream transcripts with two controlled experiments to test which sensory words move product. "Near-sensory" language (touch, smell, taste — senses a livestream cannot transmit) significantly raised minute-by-minute sales, while "far-sensory" language about sight and sound had no significant effect. The experiments traced the effect to viewers feeling the missing sensory gap was compensated and forming more vivid mental imagery, and found the advantage was strongest when buyers expected to use the product soon.

**Why it matters:** It gives livestream and short-video sellers a concrete scripting rule: describe what the camera can't deliver, since narrating the visible adds little.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit host scripts and creator briefs for the ratio of texture/scent/taste words to visual ones, and test near-sensory phrasing in categories where touch and smell matter most. The sales evidence comes from one platform's streams, so treat it as a hypothesis to test in your own feed rather than a universal script.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A reason impulse buys happen during livestreams: hearing a host describe how something feels or smells makes your brain simulate the experience, which can feel a lot like having tried it.

**Caveats:** Single platform and likely a single national market; the field portion is correlational minute-level data, and the experiments measure purchase intention rather than purchases.

### 15. Firms with cash to spare are the least responsive to their own environmental scorecards

**Journal:** *Journal of Business Research* | **Published:** 2026-09-23
**Work ID:** W7214077911 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jbusres.2026.116561

**The study:** Using panel data on Chinese publicly listed firms, researchers examined how companies adjust green innovation after their environmental performance lands above or below their own aspiration levels. Green innovation followed a U-shape when performance fell short of aspirations and an inverted U-shape when it exceeded them — meaning both mild shortfalls and extreme success dampened effort. Organizational slack flattened both patterns, with resource-rich firms reacting least to either good or bad environmental feedback.

**Why it matters:** It suggests sustainability targets motivate action mainly at the extremes, and that well-cushioned firms can absorb poor environmental results without changing anything — a warning for anyone relying on internal scorecards to drive green progress.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For sustainability and brand leaders: check whether your environmental KPIs are actually triggering investment, or whether comfortable financial buffers are letting the organization shrug off mediocre results. Consider pairing targets with explicit resource commitments rather than assuming feedback alone changes behavior.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A useful lens for reading corporate green claims: a company's environmental effort may track its slack resources and how far it is from its own goals more than any stated commitment to the planet.

**Caveats:** Observational panel data from publicly listed firms in a single country (China); associations cannot establish causation, and results may not generalize to private firms or other regulatory regimes.

### 16. On Etsy, Talking Too Much About Handcraft Hurts Sales

**Journal:** *Marketing Letters* | **Published:** 2026-09-24
**Work ID:** W7214240842 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11002-026-09840-7

**The study:** Researchers analyzed 17,480 seller narratives — the "about" and shop-story text sellers write — on Etsy, coding how heavily each emphasized craft cues (tradition, handmade care, attention to detail) versus industrial cues (efficiency, scale, production capability), and linked that language to market appeal. Both types of cues helped, but only up to a point: the relationship was an inverted U, so the most craft-saturated and the most industrial-sounding shops both underperformed sellers who struck a balance. Drawing on Language Expectancy Theory, the authors argue sellers must satisfy two audiences at once — buyers who expect artisanal authenticity and a platform environment that rewards signals of reliability and scalability.

**Why it matters:** It gives small sellers and craft brands a concrete, testable copy rule: authenticity language has diminishing and then negative returns, and refusing to sound "commercial" can cost you as much as sounding too commercial.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit your brand storytelling for cue saturation rather than cue presence. If your product pages lean entirely on heritage-and-handmade language, this analysis of real marketplace listings suggests A/B testing a version that adds credible operational signals — consistency, fulfillment capability, production standards — and vice versa for sellers whose copy reads like a factory spec sheet.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The next time a shop's description piles on words like "lovingly handcrafted in small batches," notice your own reaction — shoppers apparently discount narratives that protest their artisanship too much, and quietly reward sellers who also sound like they can actually ship the order.

**Caveats:** Observational data from a single platform (Etsy) and the craft category, so the inverted-U pattern is correlational rather than causal — successful sellers may write differently for reasons other than their language. "Market appeal" is a platform-derived measure, not verified revenue, and the findings may not generalize to other marketplaces or product types.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** A striking share of this batch finds that the biggest available gains sit not in the product, the price or the media buy, but in the wording and plumbing around the transaction — swapping a general word for a precise one lifts perceived uniqueness ("Swap a vague word for a precise one and the product starts to feel one of a kind," W7219889147), adding a smaller time unit makes the identical wait feel longer ("'2 hours and 59 minutes' feels longer than '3 hours'," W7210281977), choosing a point estimate over a range flips with shopper skepticism (W7213274554), routing a shared bill through a friend rather than the register raises satisfaction with the same meal (W7203919913), and livestream hosts move more product by narrating touch and smell rather than what the camera already shows (W7214088309). Two further studies show the same lever has a ceiling: craft language on Etsy and craft-versus-industrial cues follow an inverted U (W7214240842), as does habit intensity in freemium conversion (W7213768857) — presentation effects are dose-dependent, not monotonic.

**Why pitch this now:** Marketing budgets are under hard scrutiny while generative tools have made it nearly free to produce and test hundreds of copy and format variants, so the cheapest lever in the stack is suddenly the most scalable — and regulators and platforms are simultaneously tightening up on how performance and sustainability claims are phrased.

**Angle:** A feature on the rise of the "presentation layer" as a growth channel: what the newest experimental evidence says brands can move by rewriting rather than rebuilding, where the effects reverse if overdosed, and the uncomfortable line between clarity and manipulation now that AI can generate the variants at industrial scale. Aimed at CMOs, e-commerce and CRM leads, with a consumer-literacy strand for general readers.
