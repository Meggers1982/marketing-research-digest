### Study 2: When Aldi and Lidl start stocking big brands, supermarket prices fall — but most of those cuts don't pay off
**Work ID:** W7221809005 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Dropped counterpoint on average sales effect of price cuts** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "On average, conventional retailers cut national-brand prices after a discounter listing — and the listings themselves actually increased conventional retailers' sales of those brands."
- **Abstract says:** "HD listings increase CRs' NB sales on average, with price reductions further improving these sales outcomes."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports that price reductions on average further improved the sales outcomes from HD listings; the digest reports only the later finding that most individual reductions did not improve short-run sales, which makes the case against cutting look more one-sided than the abstract does.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add a clause noting that on average price reductions did further improve sales from the listing, even though most individual reductions did not raise short-run brand sales."

### Study 3: Letting fans do what they like with your logo may quietly cost your brand its status
**Work ID:** W7221673714 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Recruitment source not stated in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "All five studies used online panel participants in hypothetical scenarios rather than real purchases"
- **Abstract says:** "Five between-subjects online experiments (n = 958) tested the hypotheses."
- **Problem:** The abstract says the experiments were conducted online but does not identify a panel as the recruitment source; "online panel participants" is unverifiable from the abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say "online experiments" and note that the recruitment source is not reported in the abstract."

**Issue B. "Unconsciously" not supported** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "shoppers unconsciously downgrade who they think the brand is for"
- **Abstract says:** "This relative status disadvantage arises because open branding weakens associations with higher-status reference groups"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports a measured mediation through reference group association; it makes no claim about the process being unconscious.
- **Suggested fix:** "Drop "unconsciously" or replace with "tend to downgrade"."

### Study 6: Letting customers into your sustainability program builds trust — if you're a big brand
**Work ID:** W7222271096 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Consumer angle generalizes beyond the scenario experiments and drops the moderator** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Being invited to take part in a company's environmental effort makes shoppers more willing to pay extra"
- **Abstract says:** "Study 2 shows that the positive indirect effect of perceived brand involvement of consumers on WTPP via brand trust is significant for large brands but not for small brands, indicating the moderating role of brand size."
- **Problem:** Stated willingness to pay measured in two scenario experiments is presented as what "shoppers" do, and the brand-size boundary condition named in the abstract is not carried into the consumer-facing line.
- **Suggested fix:** "Qualify as "participants said they were more willing to pay extra" and note the effect held for large brands in these experiments."

**Issue B. Speculation about small brands** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "while smaller brands may already get trust credit on other grounds"
- **Abstract says:** "indicating the moderating role of brand size ... reducing consumer bias toward large brands"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports only that the indirect effect was non-significant for small brands; it offers no explanation involving pre-existing trust for small brands.
- **Suggested fix:** "State that the pathway simply did not show up for small brands in Study 2, without offering a mechanism."

### Study 7: Loving a brand isn't what makes people overspend — being hooked on one is
**Work ID:** W7221758843 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal phrasing in headline for cross-sectional survey** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Loving a brand isn't what makes people overspend — being hooked on one is"
- **Abstract says:** "Brand addiction is positively associated with compulsive buying, impulsive buying, social comparison and narcissism. On the contrary, brand love is not significantly associated with compulsive or impulsive buying."
- **Problem:** "What makes people overspend" asserts causation from a single-wave questionnaire study with associational findings; the body text uses correct "linked to" language, so the headline is out of step.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to something associational, e.g. "Brand love isn't linked to overspending — brand addiction is"."

### Study 8: How long does a happy customer take to pay off? About 10 months, and the neighbors benefit too
**Work ID:** W7223096881 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal verb for a predictive association** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "a store's own NPS starts moving sales about eight months after purchase and peaks at 10 months"
- **Abstract says:** "We find that the NPS, operationalized by surveying established customers six months after purchase, predicts future sales. Moreover, NPS sales effects unfold over many months along an "inverted U" time path: for own-store effects, from eight months after purchase, peaking at 10 months"
- **Problem:** "Starts moving sales" implies the NPS causes the sales change, while the abstract and the digest's own caveat describe a predictive relationship without causal identification.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "a store's own NPS starts predicting sales about eight months after purchase"."

### Study 9: Authenticity only pays when customers see themselves in the brand
**Work ID:** W7222626184 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Overstates exclusivity of the identification pathway** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "It suggests that authenticity claims are commercially inert unless they convert into customers defining themselves partly through the brand"
- **Abstract says:** "the observed pattern links authenticity with commercial performance through identification, without establishing that this is its only pathway or that a direct premium is absent"
- **Problem:** The authors explicitly decline to claim that identification is the only route or that no direct authenticity premium exists; "commercially inert unless" asserts what they say the data cannot establish.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "the commercial payoff in this dataset ran through identification, though the authors note other pathways are not ruled out"."

### Study 11: Taking a side on a political policy hurts brand trust — unless your customers voted for it
**Work ID:** W7223133219 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Country context not stated in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "US policy context; sample sizes not reported in the abstract."
- **Abstract says:** "In two online experiments, this research tests whether organizational responses of supporting, stalling or a mixed approach to a policy crisis, as well as actor political affiliation, impact individual brand perceptions and behavioral intentions... individuals who share a political affiliation with the policy source (e.g. executive branch)."
- **Problem:** The abstract never specifies the country; "executive branch" is suggestive but not confirmation of a US setting. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say "policy source described as an executive branch; country not specified in the abstract" or drop the US claim."

**Issue B. Headline paraphrase of political affiliation** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Taking a side on a political policy hurts brand trust — unless your customers voted for it"
- **Abstract says:** "this pattern is reversed for individuals who share a political affiliation with the policy source (e.g. executive branch)"
- **Problem:** Shared political affiliation with the policy source is not the same as having voted for a specific policy; the headline implies a vote on the policy itself.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "...unless your customers share the policymaker's politics.""

**Issue C. Generalizing experiment to consumers at large** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Consumers do not evaluate corporate political moves on principle — they evaluate them on whether the brand ended up on their side"
- **Abstract says:** "In two online experiments... Supportive actions, compared to mixed and stalling actions, negatively impact individual perceptions of a service brand's authenticity, brand trust and behavioral intentions."
- **Problem:** States a universal behavioral law about all consumers on the basis of two online scenario experiments measuring stated intentions.
- **Suggested fix:** "Hedge: "In these experiments, participants' reactions tracked their own political alignment rather than the policy's merits.""

### Study 14: When customers turn your packaging into decor, they buy again
**Work ID:** W7222986365 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Intention presented as behavior in headline** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "When customers turn your packaging into decor, they buy again"
- **Abstract says:** "upcycling enhances repurchase intentions by elevating perceptions of symbolic value"
- **Problem:** The outcome is stated repurchase intention in three experiments, not observed repeat purchasing; the headline asserts behavior (the caveat corrects this, but the headline still overstates).
- **Suggested fix:** ""...they say they'll buy again" or "...repurchase intentions rise.""

**Issue B. Moderator strength overstated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "since the experiments suggest the repurchase lift largely depends on that social amplification"
- **Abstract says:** "The effects are moderated by social media engagement: brand and user interactions amplify the impact, whereas their absence attenuates it."
- **Problem:** "Attenuates" means weakened, not eliminated; "largely depends on" implies the effect is mostly absent without social engagement.
- **Suggested fix:** ""...suggest the repurchase lift is amplified by social engagement and weaker without it.""

### Study 16: Why quiet color palettes make luxury feel timeless — and brains light up for them
**Work ID:** W7222224863 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Mechanism stated more firmly than abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Study 2 identified "chromatic timelessness" as the perceptual mechanism"
- **Abstract says:** "Study 2 provides evidence consistent with the proposed role of chromatic timelessness: lower color differentiation increases perceived chromatic timelessness, which is associated with greater liking..."
- **Problem:** The abstract offers evidence "consistent with" a proposed role; "identified as the mechanism" claims more than the authors do.
- **Suggested fix:** ""Study 2 found evidence consistent with 'chromatic timelessness' as the mechanism.""

**Issue B. Causal framing of correlational findings** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "this suggests a cheap, controllable design lever — palette restraint — shifts how long buyers expect to keep and enjoy a product"
- **Abstract says:** "exposure to lower-differentiation color composition is associated with greater activation in reward-related brain regions... and with higher liking, choice, willingness to pay, and perceived length of product use"
- **Problem:** The abstract consistently uses associational wording; "shifts" asserts a causal effect on expectations.
- **Suggested fix:** ""...palette restraint is associated with how long buyers expect to keep and enjoy a product.""

### Study 17: Disclosing AI in creative work backfires most when the content is emotional
**Work ID:** W7221671230 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Not perceived ability" overstated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Disclosure reduced acceptance mainly by lowering perceived legitimacy — not perceived ability"
- **Abstract says:** "Existing explanations primarily emphasize ability and reliability judgments, offering only a partial account... Collectively, the studies shift attention from AI ability to the perceived legitimacy of disclosed creative arrangements."
- **Problem:** The abstract says the research shifts theoretical attention from ability to legitimacy; it does not report testing and rejecting an ability pathway.
- **Suggested fix:** ""...by lowering perceived legitimacy, rather than the ability judgments prior work emphasized.""

**Issue B. Researcher nationality attributed** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Chinese researchers combined a scenario experiment (N=300)..."
- **Abstract says:** "Because experimental evidence comes primarily from younger Chinese participants and archival evidence from one Chinese social media platform..."
- **Problem:** The abstract identifies the participants and platform as Chinese, not the research team. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** ""Researchers combined a scenario experiment (N=300) with Chinese participants...""

### Study 18: "That's for old people": how an age stereotype blocks young buyers from a whole category
**Work ID:** W7223121676 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Efficacy contrast not supported** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The mechanism was identity threat — using the category felt like signaling you were old — rather than doubts about efficacy."
- **Abstract says:** "this stereotype significantly reduces TCM usage intention by triggering identity threat, and peer recommendation weakens this negative effect"

- **Suggested fix:** "Drop "rather than doubts about efficacy" or mark it as not tested in the abstract."
