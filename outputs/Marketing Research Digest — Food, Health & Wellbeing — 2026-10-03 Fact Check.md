### Study 2: Trust, not realism, is what makes AI influencers work — and hyper-realistic avatars backfire
**Work ID:** W7207770543 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Outcome conflated with marketing effectiveness** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Trust, not realism, is what makes AI influencers work"
- **Abstract says:** "establishing trust in AI influencers as the central driver of consumer well-being … Trust emerged as the strongest predictor of well-being, explaining nearly half of the variance across contexts."
- **Problem:** The dependent variable is consumer well-being, not whether an influencer "works" commercially (persuasion, sales, engagement). The body text clarifies this, but the headline and the "builds or erodes the relationship" framing imply a brand-effectiveness outcome that was not measured.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to something like "Trust, not realism, drives how AI influencers affect consumer well-being" and keep the "Why it matters" tied to well-being outcomes."

**Issue B. Uncanny valley overstated as backfire** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "hyper-realistic avatars backfire"
- **Abstract says:** "whereas persona realism shows an uncanny valley pattern"
- **Problem:** An uncanny valley pattern means a non-linear dip at near-human realism; the abstract does not state that the most realistic personas perform worst. "Backfire" asserts a stronger and more specific claim than reported.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say realism showed a non-linear (uncanny valley) relationship rather than that hyper-realistic avatars backfire."

**Issue C. Overlapping-paper claim not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The same author team appears to have a closely related paper in the same window, so findings overlap."
- **Abstract says:** "(no mention of related or overlapping papers)"
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Drop or source this claim explicitly."

### Study 7: Anger at other people's food waste predicts who actually eats their leftovers
**Work ID:** W7208792358 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Intention reported as behavior in headline** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Anger at other people's food waste predicts who actually eats their leftovers"
- **Abstract says:** "The anger felt in response to others' food waste does significantly strengthen the intention to consume their leftovers"
- **Problem:** The outcome is stated intention in an online survey, not observed leftover consumption. The word "actually eats" tells readers behavior was measured, which contradicts the digest's own caveat.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rewrite as "…predicts who says they will eat their leftovers" or "…predicts intention to eat leftovers"."

### Study 9: Australia's smokers have quietly deserted the supermarket — and it's reshaping tobacco policy
**Work ID:** W7214949285 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Causal attribution to tax and plain packaging** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "A decade of tax rises and plain packaging has pushed a mass-market category out of mainstream retail and into specialist and gray channels"
- **Abstract says:** "We analysed four waves of national cross-sectional survey data … Multinomial logistic regression examined factors associated with source relative to supermarkets. … Understanding these patterns may inform policies to reduce tobacco supply and availability."
- **Problem:** The study describes trends and correlates of purchase source; it tests no policy exposure and attributes no cause. The body asserts a causal driver that the caveats explicitly say the design cannot establish.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as "over a period of rising taxes and plain packaging, purchasing shifted away from supermarkets" without claiming the policies caused it."

**Issue B. Association strength overstated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Both tobacconist and informal channels were strongly linked to using illicitly traded tobacco."
- **Abstract says:** "Informal sourcing (aRRR=1.44, 95% CI 1.20 to 1.74) and purchasing from tobacconists (aRRR=1.31, 95% CI 1.16 to 1.49) were associated with using illicitly traded tobacco."
- **Problem:** Adjusted relative risk ratios of 1.31 and 1.44 are modest associations; "strongly linked" inflates them.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say both channels were associated with illicit tobacco use (roughly 30–45% higher relative risk)."

### Study 11: Twenty days of diaries from naval cadets: making work playful cut daily exhaustion
**Work ID:** W7214901172 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal headline for diary data** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Twenty days of diaries from naval cadets: making work playful cut daily exhaustion"
- **Abstract says:** "On days when cadets designed their work playfully, they reported more activated positive affect and less exhaustion... small discretionary choices in how employees approach work may produce immediate well-being gains"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports a same-day association in self-reported diary data and hedges with "may produce"; "cut daily exhaustion" asserts a causal effect. The digest's own caveats acknowledge reverse causality, so the headline contradicts them.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to something like "making work playful tracked with lower daily exhaustion"."

**Issue B. Separate effects of fun and competition** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "On days when cadets deliberately added fun to routine tasks — or turned them into a self-imposed competition — they reported more activated positive affect and less exhaustion that same day."
- **Abstract says:** "On days when cadets designed their work playfully, they reported more activated positive affect and less exhaustion. Combining designing fun with designing competition amplified these benefits."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports the effect for playful work design overall plus an amplification when the two are combined; it does not state that each component separately produced the main effect.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say that on days cadets designed their work playfully (adding fun and/or competition) they reported the benefits, with the combination amplifying them."

### Study 12: Brand tracking for federal agencies: ICE is the most recognized and least liked
**Work ID:** W7214439490 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Unsupported superlative in headline** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "ICE is the most recognized and least liked"
- **Abstract says:** "the FBI maintains the strongest attitudinal profile, ICE demonstrates high awareness but low attitudinal evaluations, and the U.S. Marshals Service suffers from low public recognition"
- **Problem:** The abstract never says ICE scored highest on recognition or lowest on evaluations relative to the other four agencies; it only reports a high-awareness/low-evaluation combination. Readers could take a specific ranking as established.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rephrase to "ICE scores high on awareness but low on favourability" or similar, matching the abstract's wording."

### Study 14: Short brand names feel warmer — and that warmth shows up in sales pitches and crowdfunding totals
**Work ID:** W7214622498 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Sales pitches" not in the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "that warmth shows up in sales pitches and crowdfunding totals"
- **Abstract says:** "shorter (vs. longer) brand names convey stronger impressions of warmth, which lead to higher brand equity, reflected in more favorable brand evaluations and stronger purchase intentions. Shorter names are also associated with more funds raised through crowdfunding."
