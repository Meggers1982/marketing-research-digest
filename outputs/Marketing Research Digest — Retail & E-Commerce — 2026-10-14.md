### 1. Tell shoppers they're building a collection, not just buying a thing

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-09
**Work ID:** W7221306245 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00222429261498190

**The study:** Across seven preregistered experiments plus one supplemental study (N=3,306), researchers tested what happens when a purchase is framed as adding to the consumer's own collection rather than as a one-off buy. Framing acquisition as "collecting" made the shopping process itself more enjoyable and raised how desirable the sought product seemed, because attention shifted from securing a standalone item to advancing a collection. The effect was strongest when consumers actively searched, when the find genuinely advanced the collection, and when the collection tied to an identity they valued — and it weakened or reversed otherwise.

**Why it matters:** Retailers have spent a decade stripping friction out of search; this suggests the opposite move — making search feel like a curatorial quest — can increase willingness to buy and make the process itself a source of value.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** A cheap copy and merchandising test: reframe category pages, loyalty tiers, or recommendation modules around "your collection" rather than "items you may like," and watch whether desirability and conversion move. But note the boundary conditions — frictionless one-click flows and generic product sets may neutralize or invert the effect, so test before rolling out.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It explains why sneaker drops, trading cards, makeup shades and vinyl crates hook people so effectively: the pleasure is in the hunt and the sense of a set being completed, not in the object. Knowing the trick is being deployed on you is a useful defense at checkout.

**Caveats:** All studies are experiments, largely with online participants; product desirability and acquisition experience are measured responses rather than observed long-run purchases, and the effect depends on identity-relevant collections.

### 2. Launching a green product line lifted the parent brand — but only for firms with clean reputations

**Journal:** *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* | **Published:** 2026-10-10
**Work ID:** W7221350449 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11747-026-01209-6

**The study:** Researchers ran a staggered event study around a major laundry detergent brand's first sustainable product line launch, then extended the analysis to subsequent sustainable new product introductions across 15 leading brands sold in 12 retail chains. Sustainable launches measurably increased parent-brand equity, acting as a credible product-based signal of environmental responsibility. The lift was conditional: it was stronger when the parent firm already had an ethical reputation and when the retail chain carried a higher share of sustainable products.

**Why it matters:** It puts a brand-equity number on sustainability launches beyond the sales of the green item itself — and shows the halo is borrowed against existing credibility rather than created from nothing.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Treat a sustainable line extension as a brand investment, not just an SKU decision, and model the parent-brand return — but audit your own ethical track record first, since the evidence suggests firms with weak reputations get little signaling benefit. Retail placement matters too: the same product appears to signal more in chains already known for sustainable assortments.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Shoppers may be reading more into a green product than its packaging claims: a single eco line can shift how the whole brand is perceived, which is exactly why greenwashing accusations sting so much.

**Caveats:** Observational market data rather than a randomized test; anchored in one category (laundry detergent) for the main event study and a limited set of brands and chains; results reflect one retail market and may not generalize across categories.

### 3. In live shopping, the host's mood moves sales — and the sidekick may be costing you money

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-10-05
**Work ID:** W7220454801 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105141

**The study:** Analyzing minute-by-minute data from 40 Taobao live streaming sessions (4,671 minutes total), researchers used sentiment analysis to quantify streamers' and viewers' emotional expression, then modeled the dynamics with a panel vector autoregression. Emotion flowed one way: streamer emotion was closely linked to viewer interaction and purchases, while viewer-to-viewer emotional contagion was not statistically supported. Viewer emotion was tied to new follower growth, and more frequent intervention by the on-screen assistant raised interaction frequency but was negatively associated with product sales.

**Why it matters:** Live commerce teams optimize for engagement metrics; this suggests chat activity and sales can pull in opposite directions, and that the host's emotional performance is the main lever on revenue.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit whether your live-stream assistant's interjections are buying chatter at the expense of conversion, and consider coaching and scheduling hosts around sustained emotional energy rather than chasing comment volume. The data are observational and from a single platform, so treat it as a hypothesis to A/B test in your own streams.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The enthusiasm in a live shopping stream is not just ambiance — the host's excitement is statistically connected to what viewers buy in the following minutes, a useful thing to notice before you tap "add to cart."

**Caveats:** Observational panel data from 40 sessions on one platform (Taobao) in one market; associations and impulse responses, not randomized causal evidence; sentiment is inferred algorithmically from text and speech.

### 4. When the shock hit, paid search carried e-commerce revenue — and Facebook ads brought visitors who didn't buy

**Journal:** *Journal of Advertising Research* | **Published:** 2026-10-08
**Work ID:** W7220946924 | **DOI:** 10.1080/00218499.2026.2719856

**The study:** Researchers analyzed a 71-week Google Analytics panel covering 24 online retailers, comparing how site entry channels, visitor status and transaction value related to revenue before and during the COVID-19 lockdown disruption. Direct traffic and repeat visitors were consistently the strongest revenue correlates in both periods, while paid search became a notably stronger revenue pathway during the disruption. Facebook ads were associated with first-time visitor volume but little immediate revenue, and lagged models showed paid search spending today predicted later organic search and direct visits.

**Why it matters:** It gives marketers real platform-level evidence that paid search has a delayed halo on "free" channels, and that social-driven first-time traffic should not be judged on same-session revenue.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Revisit attribution windows: if paid search reliably precedes later organic and direct arrivals, last-click models will systematically understate it — and cutting search spend in a downturn may quietly erode the direct traffic you rely on. Also reconsider the KPI you set for social ads, which here delivered audience rather than immediate sales.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Your own shopping path explains the finding: people often discover a retailer via an ad or search, then come back later by typing the name directly — which is why the channel that got credit for your purchase is rarely the one that actually found you.

**Caveats:** Observational panel of 24 retailers only, with associations rather than causal estimates; the disruption period is a unique exogenous shock that may not generalize to normal conditions or to other markets and platforms.

### 5. A little blue check moved eyeballs — and choices — in a risky online marketplace

**Journal:** *International Journal of Bank Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-12
**Work ID:** W7221510551 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ijbm-01-2026-0011

**The study:** Researchers ran a controlled eye-tracking experiment with 60 participants who each browsed 10 NFT listings, with five listings randomly assigned a platform-issued verification badge, then picked three for a hypothetical purchase. Badged item cards drew a fixation in 83.3% of observations versus 35.7% for unbadged cards, were looked at roughly 1.47 seconds sooner, and were chosen 42.3% of the time versus 17.7% for unbadged listings. The effect was concentrated among participants who said afterward they had clearly noticed the badge, suggesting the signal only works once it captures attention.

**Why it matters:** It gives process-level evidence that trust badges on marketplace listings work less as a reasoned credibility argument and more as an attention magnet that gets a listing into the consideration set at all.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For platform and marketplace teams: this is a case for A/B testing badge placement, size and contrast — not just whether to have a verification program, but whether shoppers actually see it. The study suggests the conversion lift is gated on noticing, so a badge buried in a card layout may be doing nothing. Treat this as a controlled-lab signal of what can happen, not a guaranteed lift on live traffic.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Shoppers may want to ask what a verification badge actually certifies. In this study a randomly assigned badge — carrying no real quality information — more than doubled selection rates, a reminder that a visual trust cue can steer choice before you've consciously evaluated the item.

**Caveats:** Small sample (60 participants); hypothetical selections rather than real purchases; a single, unusual category (NFT listings) in a controlled interface; gaze was measured for the whole item card, not the badge icon itself; badge 'noticing' was self-reported after the task, not manipulated.

### 6. Loyalty programs don't fail on rewards alone — trust and commitment have to show up too

**Journal:** *European Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-25
**Work ID:** W7204202782 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ejm-08-2024-0633

**The study:** Two surveys of Australian supermarket shoppers (N=250 to validate the engagement measure, N=820 to test the model) examined what makes customers actually engage with loyalty programs, using both PLS-SEM and fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis. Customers' own inclination and capacity to engage — including their habits of searching for deals over time and across stores — predicted loyalty program engagement, which in turn predicted engagement with the retailer and both attitudinal and behavioral loyalty. The configurational analysis found that perceived benefits, trust and commitment together form the key recipe: their joint presence drives engagement, and their joint absence blocks it.

**Why it matters:** It suggests loyalty program performance is not simply a function of how generous the rewards are, but of whether a particular customer is disposed to engage at all — pointing toward segmenting programs by engagement propensity rather than treating all members alike.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For retail CRM and loyalty leads: the practical question is whether your program is over-indexed on benefit richness while underinvesting in trust signals (data use, reward transparency, reliability of redemption). Worth testing whether segmenting members by engagement disposition — deal-seekers versus passive enrollees — changes program ROI more than another points tweak.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A useful consumer-side read: the people who get the most out of supermarket loyalty programs are those already inclined to hunt for deals across stores and over time. If you're a passive member, the program is likely delivering far less value to you than to the engaged shopper next to you in line.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional surveys, so causal direction cannot be established; self-reported loyalty and engagement rather than observed purchase data; single country (Australia) and single sector (supermarkets).

### 7. AI review summaries didn't silence shoppers — they wrote more reviews, and better ones

**Journal:** *Information Systems Research* | **Published:** 2026-10-05
**Work ID:** W7220470690 | **DOI:** 10.1287/isre.2024.1282

**The study:** Researchers studied an e-commerce platform's rollout of AI-generated summaries (AIGS) of customer reviews, comparing review behavior before and after the feature appeared, and added a controlled lab experiment that measured the psychological mechanism. Contrary to the intuition that AI-written content crowds out user-generated content, review volume rose after AIGS was introduced, with the lab study showing the effect was mediated by a heightened sense of "collective efficacy" — the feeling that one's individual review feeds a shared body of product knowledge. The lift was strongest for products that were already well-reviewed and for polarizing products. Content analysis showed post-AIGS reviews covered fewer attributes the summary already mentioned, raised more attributes it had missed, and elaborated rather than restated.

**Why it matters:** Retailers worried that generative-AI summaries would cannibalize the user reviews they depend on now have evidence of the opposite — summaries can act as a recruitment device for more, and more informative, contributions.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you're piloting AI review summaries, instrument the downstream effect on review volume and content quality, not just on conversion or time-on-page; the mechanism here suggests the framing of the summary (as a collective product of shoppers' contributions) may be what drives the lift, which is a testable design variable.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The AI summary at the top of a product page may be quietly changing what shoppers write: people stop repeating the obvious and start filling the gaps, which means reviews posted after a summary exists may be more useful to read than the ones above them.

**Caveats:** Observational data from a single e-commerce platform with a before/after design; the mediation evidence comes from a separate controlled lab experiment; effects on well-reviewed and polarizing products may not generalize to thin catalogs; short post-introduction horizon.

### 8. On B2B platforms, a strong online reputation and a strong trade-show presence cancel each other out

**Journal:** *Journal of Business Research* | **Published:** 2026-10-07
**Work ID:** W7220830415 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jbusres.2026.116586

**The study:** Using 30 months of operational data from sellers on a major Chinese B2B platform, researchers tested whether online reputation scores and participation in offline events reinforce each other. Both were independently associated with better seller performance, and online reputation mattered more on average — but their interaction was negative: the payoff from each weakened as the other grew stronger. The authors interpret both as credibility cues that substitute for one another once a buyer already has a strong signal.

**Why it matters:** It challenges the default omnichannel assumption that online and offline investments compound, and suggests B2B sellers with already-excellent ratings may be over-spending on event budgets.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit whether your offline event spend is concentrated on sellers or business units that already have top-tier online ratings — this analysis implies the marginal return there is lowest, and the budget may do more work behind weakly rated or newly listed offerings.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Buyers appear to stop collecting evidence once one credibility signal is convincing enough — a useful reminder of how quickly people settle on "good enough" proof before committing.

**Caveats:** Observational platform data from a single Chinese B2B marketplace; the paper carefully describes associations rather than causal effects; selection into offline events is not randomized.

### 9. Fun-seeking shoppers forgive influencers who stray off-topic; practical shoppers don't

**Journal:** *Journal of Business Research* | **Published:** 2026-10-11
**Work ID:** W7221454638 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jbusres.2026.116589

**The study:** Across five experiments, researchers treated social media influencers as "person brands" and tested how consumers react when an influencer recommends something outside their usual domain. Purchase motivation moderated the effect: hedonic, fun-driven shoppers were relatively tolerant of low-relevance recommendations, while functionally motivated shoppers punished them — and for the functional group, the damage ran through perceived influencer authenticity. When the product was far outside the influencer's lane, hedonic consumers responded better to warm influencers than to competent-seeming ones.

**Why it matters:** It gives brands a concrete rule for matching influencer partnerships to category and shopping mindset, rather than assuming reach and follower fit are enough.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Test whether your category is bought hedonically or functionally before buying off-domain influencer reach: for utilitarian products the evidence points to tight domain fit and authenticity signals, while for indulgence categories warmth may beat expertise even on a stretch recommendation.
- **For general readers and consumers:** That nagging sense that a favorite creator has "sold out" when they post about something unrelated appears to depend on why you're watching — people shopping for a solution judge harshly, people shopping for fun largely shrug.

**Caveats:** Five experiments, likely with online panel participants and hypothetical products; measures are purchase intentions rather than observed purchases; sample details and country not specified in the abstract.

### 10. Three in four Vietnamese grocery shoppers will pay for home delivery — but they're exquisitely sensitive to the fee

**Journal:** *Journal of Asia Business Studies* | **Published:** 2026-09-01
**Work ID:** W7204970234 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jabs-01-2026-0046

**The study:** Researchers ran a discrete choice experiment with 438 grocery shoppers in Vietnam's Mekong Delta, estimating willingness to pay for last-mile delivery attributes using a generalized multinomial logit model in willingness-to-pay space that allows for both taste and scale heterogeneity. About 75% of consumers showed positive willingness to pay for home delivery, but demand was highly sensitive to shipping fees, and willingness to pay for each attribute varied widely across individuals, driven by both demographics and order value as well as unobserved taste differences.

**Why it matters:** It's rare WTP evidence for e-grocery delivery in a developing market, and it argues against flat, one-size-fits-all shipping fees in favor of segmented or order-value-conditioned delivery options.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you operate e-grocery in an emerging market, treat the delivery fee as a segmentation lever rather than a fixed cost pass-through — the heterogeneity here suggests menu-based options (speed, window, price tiers) tied to order value could capture more demand than a single flat fee.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Most shoppers do value having groceries brought to the door — but the price at which that value evaporates is lower and more variable than retailers tend to assume, which is why the delivery fee is so often the thing that kills a cart.

**Caveats:** Hypothetical choice scenarios, not observed purchases; a single region of one country; modest sample (N=438) for the number of segments explored; stated preferences may overstate real willingness to pay.

### 11. The myth of the "bad returner": eight years of Nordic fashion data says returns don't drive future spending — or kill profit

**Journal:** *The International Review of Retail Distribution and Consumer Research* | **Published:** 2026-10-08
**Work ID:** W7221059811 | **DOI:** 10.1080/09593969.2026.2743811

**The study:** Researchers analyzed archival data from a Nordic fashion e-tailer covering 2015–2022: 260,698 orders from 110,772 customers. Using Bernoulli modeling, difference-in-differences with matched control customers, and a profitability threshold analysis, they found that nearly every long-tenured customer eventually becomes a "returner" simply through exposure, and that making a return had no detectable causal effect on how much a customer bought afterward (DiD = −0.0031, p = 0.62). Declines in spending showed up equally among matched non-returners, pointing to normal customer lifecycle decay rather than anything caused by the return itself. Returners reached profit parity with non-returners once reverse-logistics costs fell below a calculable recovery threshold.

**Why it matters:** Retailers routinely segment, flag, and even ban "serial returners," but this evidence suggests returner status is mostly a function of how long someone has shopped with you — and the real profit lever is the cost of processing returns, not the customer's behavior.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit whether your returns policy and customer-scoring models are punishing a customer type that may not exist. The actionable question shifts from "who returns too much?" to "what does a return cost us to process, and where is our break-even threshold?" — a single-retailer finding worth replicating against your own order history before rewriting policy.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If you've worried that sending items back marks you as a problem customer, the data from one large retailer suggests returning is close to universal among regular shoppers — and returners spent no less afterward than anyone else.

**Caveats:** Single retailer, single category (fashion) and a single region (Nordic market); archival and observational, so matched-control DiD mitigates but does not eliminate selection effects; the profitability threshold depends on that firm's specific reverse-logistics cost structure.

### 12. Simple beats smart: basic rule-based chatbots outperformed LLM bots on retail service logs

**Journal:** *The International Review of Retail Distribution and Consumer Research* | **Published:** 2026-09-29
**Work ID:** W7214950393 | **DOI:** 10.1080/09593969.2026.2740584

**The study:** A two-part mixed-methods study used real chatbot logs rather than lab tests. Study 1 covered 108,566 sessions across nine rule-based chatbots in four industries in Portugal and Brazil during 2023, finding that efficiency — how little effort it took to resolve a query — was strongly associated with customers returning to use the bot (β = .92, p = .004), while effectiveness showed no independent association. Study 2 combined six interviews with Portuguese retail managers and descriptive log data from three rule-based and two LLM retail chatbots (11,320 sessions, 50,321 interactions in 2024), and found the rule-based deployments recorded higher efficiency and effectiveness rates than the LLM systems.

**Why it matters:** As retailers rush to replace scripted bots with generative AI, operational logs from live deployments suggest the newer technology did not automatically deliver better service outcomes — and that minimizing customer effort mattered more for repeat use than getting the answer perfectly right.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Before swapping a working rule-based bot for an LLM, instrument both and compare on effort-per-resolution, not on demo impressiveness. The result also hints at a design principle worth A/B testing: optimizing for fewer turns and faster resolution may buy more repeat usage than optimizing for conversational richness.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It helps explain a common frustration: a chatty AI assistant that takes five exchanges to do what a menu of buttons did in one can feel worse, even when it's technically more capable.

**Caveats:** Observational and correlational — no causal claims possible; very small samples at the chatbot level (nine bots in Study 1, five in Study 2) and Study 2 did not compare revisit rates between architectures at all; limited to Portugal and Brazil; LLM deployments were early-generation and may improve.

### 13. Why India's lowest-income shoppers stay loyal to one store — and it isn't trust

**Journal:** *Journal of Global Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-29
**Work ID:** W7214970397 | **DOI:** 10.1080/08911762.2026.2726348

**The study:** Researchers studied retail and brand choice among base-of-the-pyramid consumers in semi-urban West Bengal, India, combining 48 grounded-theory interviews to build measures with a survey of 342 low-income consumers analyzed via PLS-SEM. Bounded rationality (limited information and literacy) and lock-in — credit ties, proximity, dependence on a single shopkeeper — significantly drove constrained store selection, which in turn constrained which brands people could buy. Perceived opportunism, meaning the suspicion that retailers exploit them, was present but did not meaningfully shape either constraint.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that for subsistence consumers, brand choice is effectively decided at the store level before a brand ever competes: win the shopkeeper and the credit relationship, and you win the basket.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For companies targeting emerging-market mass segments, this points to distribution and retailer relationships — not advertising — as the binding constraint on brand access, and raises an uncomfortable test: is your growth in these markets coming from preference or from shoppers having nowhere else to go?
- **For general readers and consumers:** A reminder that "consumer choice" is partly a privilege of mobility and information; when a neighborhood shop extends you credit, your shopping list is shaped by that relationship long before any brand message reaches you.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey, so no causal inference; self-reported attitudes rather than observed purchases; confined to semi-urban West Bengal and may not generalize to other Indian regions or emerging markets; retailer perspectives not captured; constructs are newly developed and not yet externally validated.

### 14. Giving your shopping assistant a face: when human-looking AI avatars actually lift engagement

**Journal:** *Journal of Research in Interactive Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-01
**Work ID:** W7215066966 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jrim-01-2026-0017

**The study:** Two studies tested whether making AI service avatars look more human changes how shoppers respond. Study 1 was a between-subjects experiment manipulating bundled visual anthropomorphism (more versus less human-looking avatar); Study 2 was a three-wave survey of online shoppers recalling real AI-avatar service encounters, analyzed with PROCESS mediation and moderation using 5,000 bootstrap resamples. More human-looking avatars raised engagement intention, and emotional connection explained part — but only part — of the effect. Perceived authenticity strengthened the link from anthropomorphism to emotional connection, while technological familiarity strengthened the link from emotional connection to engagement.

**Why it matters:** Retailers are rapidly deploying avatar-fronted AI assistants on the assumption that a friendlier face builds rapport; this work suggests the lift is real but modest, and depends on whether users read the avatar as authentic and are already comfortable with the technology.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Treat avatar design as a testable variable, not a default: run holdout tests comparing a human-looking avatar against a plain text or icon-based assistant, and segment results by how tech-fluent your customer base is. The paper's own finding that emotional connection carries only part of the effect is a warning against over-investing in warmth cues at the expense of competence, speed and accuracy.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A shopping bot with eyes and a name is designed to trigger the same social instincts you use with people — and this research suggests it partly works, especially if the persona feels authentic rather than cloying.

**Caveats:** Study 2 is a self-reported survey of recalled encounters and cannot establish causation; Study 1 measures engagement intentions rather than purchases; sample size and country for both studies are not specified in the abstract; effects on emotional connection were described as comparatively modest.

### 15. Scams are now a marketing problem: researchers reframe AI fraud as 'malicious persuasion'

**Journal:** *Journal of Interactive Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-07
**Work ID:** W7211892755 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10949968261487442

**The study:** This conceptual article in the Journal of Interactive Marketing treats online scams as AI-driven malicious persuasion attempts, arguing that generative AI changes fraud on three dimensions at once: simplicity of creation, sophistication of execution and scale of distribution. Drawing on persuasion knowledge theory and a dynamic view of consumer vulnerability, the authors argue vulnerability is both a cause of being scammed and a consequence of it — victimization deepens susceptibility. They propose consumer empowerment (recognizing and resisting attacks, reporting victimization, and recovering over time) as a necessary complement to regulation and platform self-regulation.

**Why it matters:** Fraud erodes trust in the same digital channels legitimate retailers depend on, and this framing makes scam resilience a shared commercial responsibility rather than only a policing issue.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit how closely your own persuasion tactics — urgency countdowns, scarcity claims, lookalike checkout flows — resemble the patterns consumers are being trained to distrust, and consider whether clear authentication and anti-impersonation messaging is becoming a brand asset rather than a compliance cost.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The research reframes being scammed as something that happens to people in a weakened moment rather than to gullible people, and argues that victims spiral: being defrauded once makes the next attempt more likely to land.

**Caveats:** This is a conceptual and agenda-setting paper, not new empirical evidence; the interventions it proposes have not been tested here.

### 16. The original purpose-driven brand: what Gandhi's homespun cloth campaign got right — and where it hit a wall

**Journal:** *Journal of Historical Research in Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-30
**Work ID:** W7214900383 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jhrm-08-2025-0054

**The study:** A qualitative historical case study reanalyzes khadi, the indigenous hand-spun cloth of India's Swadeshi and Non-Cooperation movements, as an early campaign of ethnocentric countermarketing. The author synthesizes archival visual material, period print advertisements and historical economic data through the lenses of social identity theory and consumer ethnocentrism. The conclusion: khadi won not by being a better product but by rewriting the evaluation criteria so that symbolic identity value outweighed clear functional shortcomings — yet price gaps, class divides and technological dependence kept it from displacing colonial supply chains.

**Why it matters:** It is a historical stress test of purpose-driven branding, showing both that meaning can override product inferiority and that symbolic positioning collapses without scalable cost and supply infrastructure behind it.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If your brand is asking customers to accept a functional trade-off for a values-based reason, treat the supply chain as the binding constraint: the case suggests identity-led demand can be created faster than the operations needed to serve it affordably at scale.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A century before boycotts and buycotts went viral online, shoppers were already using what they bought to signal who they were — and the same economics applied: the ethical choice cost more, which limited who could make it.

**Caveats:** Interpretive historical case study, not a controlled or quantitative design; the author notes reliance on compiled historical literature and published archival media rather than raw trade ledgers; single country and single movement, so generalization to modern brands is inferential.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** A striking share of this batch tests standard retail playbook moves against real operational data — and the playbook loses. Eight years of Nordic order history found the "bad returner" is mostly an artifact of tenure, not behavior ("The myth of the 'bad returner'", W7221059811); 108,566 live chatbot sessions found rule-based bots outperforming LLM bots on efficiency and effectiveness (W7214950393); minute-level Taobao data found chat engagement and sales pulling in opposite directions (W7220454801); a platform rollout found AI review summaries increasing rather than cannibalizing user reviews (W7220470690); 30 months of B2B seller data found online reputation and trade-show presence substituting for each other rather than compounding (W7220830415); and a 71-week, 24-retailer traffic panel found paid search quietly feeding the "free" direct and organic traffic that last-click models credit instead (W7220946924).

**Why pitch this now:** Retailers are simultaneously tightening returns policies, replacing scripted chatbots with generative AI, and cutting performance-marketing budgets under margin pressure — three decisions this evidence suggests are being made on untested folk wisdom rather than on their own logs.

**Angle:** A reported feature on the widening gap between what retail operators assume and what their operational data shows, built around the specific assumptions now failing audit, written for senior retail and marketing decision-makers who are setting next year's budgets. The honest framing is methodological as much as managerial: these are mostly single-firm, observational findings, so the pitch is "go measure this yourself," not "the industry is wrong."

**Potential headlines:**
- The retail playbook moves that operational data doesn't back up
- The serial returner your policy is built around may not exist
- Your 2027 retail budget is funding problems the evidence says you don't have

**Potential outlets:**
- **Retail Dive** — returns crackdowns and chatbot replacement programs are its core operational beat, and this gives both a data-backed counterargument.
- **MIT Sloan Management Review** — fits its long-running interest in the gap between managerial intuition and the firm's own measurement, and tolerates the methodological caveats.
- **Modern Retail** — covers the practical mechanics of returns, live commerce and AI customer service at the retailer level, where these findings land directly.
- **Digiday** — the paid-search halo and the engagement-versus-sales split in live streams speak straight to its attribution and measurement audience.

## Story Ideas by Study

- **Tell shoppers they're building a collection, not just buying a thing (Work ID W7221306245):** After a decade of frictionless checkout, evidence that making the hunt harder can make shoppers want the item more — for a CMO-audience column or a consumer-psychology explainer in a general-interest outlet.
- **Launching a green product line lifted the parent brand — but only for firms with clean reputations (Work ID W7221350449):** Sustainability launches pay a brand-equity dividend that firms with weak ethical records can't collect — for a sustainability or brand-strategy feature in marketing trade press.
- **In live shopping, the host's mood moves sales — and the sidekick may be costing you money (Work ID W7220454801):** Live commerce teams are optimizing for comment volume while the assistant driving that chatter is associated with lower sales — for a marketing-trade news brief or a social-commerce explainer.
- **When the shock hit, paid search carried e-commerce revenue — and Facebook ads brought visitors who didn't buy (Work ID W7220946924):** Paid search spending today predicts tomorrow's "free" direct and organic traffic, which last-click attribution systematically hides — for a performance-marketing or media-measurement column.
- **A little blue check moved eyeballs — and choices — in a risky online marketplace (Work ID W7221510551):** A randomly assigned badge carrying zero quality information more than doubled selection rates, raising the question of what verification actually certifies — for a consumer-tech or marketplace-trust explainer in a general-interest outlet.
- **Loyalty programs don't fail on rewards alone — trust and commitment have to show up too (Work ID W7204202782):** The shopper most inclined to hunt deals extracts most of the program's value, which argues for segmenting by engagement propensity instead of adding points — for a retail CRM trade piece, with a personal-finance sidebar on whether your supermarket card is worth carrying.
- **AI review summaries didn't silence shoppers — they wrote more reviews, and better ones (Work ID W7220470690):** The feature retailers feared would kill user-generated content appears to recruit it, and shifts reviewers toward filling gaps the summary missed — for a marketing-trade news brief or a tech-and-commerce feature.
- **On B2B platforms, a strong online reputation and a strong trade-show presence cancel each other out (Work ID W7220830415):** Credibility signals substitute rather than compound, which puts top-rated sellers' event budgets under scrutiny — for a B2B marketing trade piece or an events-industry explainer.
- **Fun-seeking shoppers forgive influencers who stray off-topic; practical shoppers don't (Work ID W7221454638):** A usable matching rule for creator deals — warmth beats expertise on indulgence categories, tight domain fit is mandatory on utilitarian ones — for an influencer-marketing trade column.
- **Three in four Vietnamese grocery shoppers will pay for home delivery — but they're exquisitely sensitive to the fee (Work ID W7204970234):** Rare willingness-to-pay evidence from an emerging e-grocery market arguing flat shipping fees leave demand on the table — for a retail trade explainer or an emerging-markets business outlet.
- **The myth of the "bad returner" (Work ID W7221059811):** Returning becomes near-universal with tenure and had no detectable effect on later spending, so the profit lever is reverse-logistics cost, not customer punishment — for a retail trade feature and, separately, a consumer piece on whether returning items hurts your standing with a retailer.
- **Simple beats smart: basic rule-based chatbots outperformed LLM bots on retail service logs (Work ID W7214950393):** Live deployment logs where the scripted bot beat the generative one, and where customer effort predicted repeat use better than getting the answer right — for a customer-experience trade piece or an AI-skepticism column in business press.
- **Why India's lowest-income shoppers stay loyal to one store — and it isn't trust (Work ID W7214970397):** Credit ties and proximity settle brand choice before any brand competes, raising whether emerging-market growth reflects preference or constraint — for an emerging-markets business feature or a development-economics-adjacent general-interest piece.
- **Giving your shopping assistant a face (Work ID W7215066966):** The humanized avatar lift is real but modest and gated on perceived authenticity and tech familiarity — for a design-and-CX trade brief.
- **Scams are now a marketing problem (Work ID W7211892755):** The uncomfortable overlap between legitimate urgency and scarcity tactics and the patterns consumers are being trained to distrust — for a brand-strategy or trust-and-safety column, with a consumer angle on victimization as a cycle rather than a character flaw.
- **The original purpose-driven brand (Work ID W7214900383):** A century-old stress test showing identity-led demand outrunning the supply chain needed to serve it affordably — for a business-history essay or a purpose-branding feature.
