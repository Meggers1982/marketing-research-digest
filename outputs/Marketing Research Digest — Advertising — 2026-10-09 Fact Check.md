### Study 1: When shoppers spot an AI-made ad, they like it less — but still buy
**Work ID:** W7214625312 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Intention stated as behavior in headline** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "When shoppers spot an AI-made ad, they like it less — but still buy"
- **Abstract says:** "Consumers who realize an advertisement is synthetic experience diminished positive attitudes toward the advertisement and the brand, but it does not impact purchase intent or social media engagement."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports no effect on self-reported purchase intent, not on purchases. "Still buy" tells readers behavior was measured and unchanged, which the study does not show (the digest's own caveat acknowledges self-report).
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to something like "...they like it less — but say they'd still buy" or "...but purchase intent holds steady"."

**Issue B. Direction of moderation not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "high creativity softened the attitude penalty"
- **Abstract says:** "Lastly, creativity moderates the relationship between awareness of an advertisement's falsity and attitudes towards the advertisement and the brand."
- **Problem:** The abstract states only that creativity moderates the relationship; it does not say in which direction. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Write "creativity moderated the link between noticing the ad was fake and attitudes" without specifying the direction, or verify the direction in the full text."

### Study 3: Let the AI do the guilt-tripping and keep the humans for the pep talk
**Work ID:** W7216305737 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Dropped moderator (attribution focus)** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "AI-generated guilt appeals beat human-generated ones at driving green purchase intention, apparently because an AI scold triggers less "green moral pressure." Human-generated encouragement beat AI-generated encouragement, working through greater green self-efficacy."
- **Abstract says:** "Furthermore, attribution focus moderates these interaction effects: behavioral attribution strengthens the advantage of AI-generated guilt appeal, whereas personal attribution strengthens the advantage of human-generated encouragement appeal."
- **Problem:** The digest presents the creator-type × appeal effects as unconditional and omits the named boundary condition, so readers may assume the pairing rule holds regardless of how the message frames attribution.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add a sentence noting that the advantages were strengthened by behavioral attribution (for AI guilt) and personal attribution (for human encouragement)."

### Study 8: How much authority an agency gives its AI depends on where the client is from
**Work ID:** W7213885424 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Overstated causal/exclusive claim in Why it matters** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "this shows the real constraint on AI authority is client origin and perceived regulatory culture, not the technology's capability"
- **Abstract says:** "this paper develops evidence that AI governance is not standardized but relational: it is shaped by client-origin governance expectations as attributed by agency-side practitioners... introduce client-origin governance expectations as the moderating construct explaining this variation across clients, one that capability-based and institutional accounts alone cannot address"
- **Problem:** The abstract positions client-origin expectations as a moderating construct that capability-based accounts alone cannot explain, not as a replacement for capability; "shows the real constraint" is stronger than an interpretive three-agency ethnography supports.
- **Suggested fix:** "Soften to "suggests that client-origin expectations, not just the technology's capability, shape how much authority AI is given"."

### Study 9: When a lesson looks like an ad, parents clamp down on it
**Work ID:** W7116214838 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline asserts unreported direction** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "When a lesson looks like an ad, parents clamp down on it"
- **Abstract says:** "The serial mediation model proposed is statistically supported, offering a possible explanation for how parents are differentially psychologically processing the three forms of media... Findings highlight that edu-ads are a distinct category of media"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports no comparative direction showing that edu-ads increase restrictive mediation relative to the other formats. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe to the reported result, e.g. "Parents process 'edu-ads' differently from both ads and lessons — and that shapes how they mediate"."

**Issue B. Why it matters goes beyond reported findings** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "this suggests the format itself can trigger parental suspicion that blunts the educational payoff"
- **Abstract says:** "Mediators include perceived amusement value, perceived informational value, perceived manipulative intent, presumed positive impact and active mediation intent... The serial mediation model proposed is statistically supported"
- **Problem:** Perceived manipulative intent is listed as a mediator, but the abstract does not report that edu-ads raised it or that any educational payoff was reduced.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say the study maps how perceptions of manipulative intent and presumed impact feed into mediation decisions, without claiming edu-ads trigger suspicion or blunt learning."

**Issue C. Outcome variable described loosely** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "fed into parents' intentions to actively discuss or restrict the content"
- **Abstract says:** "this experiment (N = 147) tests a serial mediation model exploring the effects of advertisements, edu-ads and educator lessons on parents' restrictive mediation intent. Mediators include... active mediation intent."
- **Problem:** Active mediation intent is a mediator in the model; the dependent variable is restrictive mediation intent. The digest presents both as the outcome.
- **Suggested fix:** "Note that restrictive mediation intent was the outcome, with active mediation intent one of the mediators."

### Study 10: When "diverse" virtual influencers backfire: disability avatars cut donation intent
**Work ID:** W7202162669 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Substantially" overstates "significantly"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "found that a strong fit between the endorser and the cause substantially reduced that dissonance"
- **Abstract says:** "demonstrates that higher endorser–cause fit significantly reduces audiences' cognitive dissonance"
- **Problem:** "Significantly" here denotes statistical significance; "substantially" implies a large effect size the abstract does not report.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "significantly reduced" or "reduced"."

### Study 11: Watching someone else go green feels good enough — and that's the problem
**Work ID:** W7203716020 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Zero behavior change" overstates a qualitative finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "may be generating exactly the emotional response that lets audiences off the hook, with high moral approval masking zero behavior change."
- **Abstract says:** "In practical terms, we show how moral engagement with prosocial communication may not translate into behavioral participation."
- **Problem:** The study is interpretive and measured no behavior, so it cannot show that behavior change is "zero" — only that participants positioned themselves as behaviorally disengaged. The digest's own caveat says behavior was not measured, which this line sits awkwardly against.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "with high moral approval potentially coexisting with little or no behavioural participation, as participants described themselves.""

**Issue B. Targets of responsibility shifting not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "then shift responsibility for action onto the influencer or onto institutions"
- **Abstract says:** "We identify a three-stage process in which moral elevation, vicarious moral satisfaction, and responsibility shifting shape audience responses to prosocial influencer content."
- **Problem:** The abstract names "responsibility shifting" but does not specify that responsibility is shifted onto influencers or institutions. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Either drop the specific targets or mark them as detail drawn from the full paper."

### Study 12: Sports betting ads have made gambling feel like just another part of being young

### Study 13: The thing that keeps kids glued to a screen isn't the story — it's how much the picture moves
**Work ID:** W7214311385 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline rules out narrative, which was not tested** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The thing that keeps kids glued to a screen isn't the story — it's how much the picture moves"
- **Abstract says:** "higher visual motion significantly predicts video viewership after accounting for a broad set of visual, audio, and affective characteristics"
- **Problem:** The studies controlled for visual, audio and affective features and manipulated motion; they did not test narrative or story quality against motion, so the exclusive "isn't the story" framing goes beyond the evidence.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rephrase as "One measurable driver of children's viewing isn't content quality at all — it's how much the picture moves.""

**Issue B. Learning outcomes not measured** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "It also hints that educational videos with livelier backgrounds may hold attention without hurting learning."
- **Abstract says:** "motion increases attention to background regions without reducing attention to instructional content"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports attention to instructional content, not comprehension or learning outcomes; "without hurting learning" substitutes an untested outcome for a measured attention metric.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "may hold attention without pulling attention away from the instructional content itself (learning was not measured).""

### Study 20: Labeling an ad "AI-generated" mostly produces a shrug — except among AI-savvy consumers
**Work ID:** W7211870608 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Dispositional persuasion knowledge glossed as "skepticism"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "dispositional persuasion knowledge (their general skepticism of marketing)"
- **Abstract says:** "the interaction of dispositional persuasion knowledge, experience with AI tools, and trust in AI"
- **Problem:** The abstract does not define dispositional persuasion knowledge; it is generally understood as a standing understanding of persuasion tactics rather than skepticism per se. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Describe it as "their general awareness of how marketing tries to persuade" or drop the parenthetical."

**Issue B. Consumer angle drops the AI-text boundary condition** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "If you use AI tools yourself, you are likely to judge an AI-made ad more generously than someone who doesn't"
- **Abstract says:** "novel positive effects of dispositional persuasion knowledge, AI experience, and trust in AI with regard to specific advertising-focused dependent variables when the use of AI-generated text in ads is disclosed"
- **Problem:** The favourable response applied specifically when AI-generated text was disclosed, and only on certain outcomes; the consumer-facing line generalizes it to any AI-made ad.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add the qualifier: "...more generously when it's the ad copy that's labelled as AI-written.""

### Study 21: Eye-tracking finds moviegoers read poster colors differently than the industry assumes
**Work ID:** W7213276828 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Placement vs. color comparison not in abstract** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "should remember that composition and center placement can override color in driving attention"
- **Abstract says:** "Findings also confirm central fixation bias, underscoring the influence of spatial placement on visual attention."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports a central fixation bias but never tests or claims that spatial placement outweighs or 'overrides' colour in driving attention. The digest turns a descriptive finding into a relative ranking of two creative variables, which a creative team could act on incorrectly.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rewrite as: 'and should remember that where an element sits on the poster shapes attention in its own right — the study confirms a central fixation bias.'"

**Issue B. One-sided summary of colour-convention results** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Several associations departed from industry convention"
- **Abstract says:** "Results reveal both alignment and divergence from traditional colour-genre conventions, such as the unexpected association of green with sadness and orange with trust"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports alignment as well as divergence; the headline and study summary present only the divergence, slightly overstating how far audience perceptions depart from convention.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add that results showed both alignment and divergence, e.g. 'Some associations matched industry convention while several departed from it — green was linked to sadness and orange to trust.'"

**Issue C. Unsupported country caveat** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "single country context"
- **Abstract says:** "two experimental within-subjects studies were conducted with 41 participants aged 18 to 40"
- **Problem:** The abstract does not state the participants' country or whether the sample was drawn from one country. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to 'country and recruitment source not specified in the abstract'."

**Issue D. Overstated intensifier on fixation bias** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the study confirmed a strong central fixation bias, meaning placement in the poster's center heavily shaped where attention went"
- **Abstract says:** "Findings also confirm central fixation bias, underscoring the influence of spatial placement on visual attention."
- **Problem:** The abstract gives no effect size or magnitude; 'strong' and 'heavily' add emphasis the abstract does not support.
- **Suggested fix:** "Drop 'strong' and 'heavily': 'the study confirmed a central fixation bias, indicating that spatial placement influenced where attention went.'"

**Issue E. Consumer angle overstates mechanism** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The poster that made you want to see a film worked on you before you read a word of it — mostly through where your eye landed first."
- **Abstract says:** "Results reveal both alignment and divergence from traditional colour-genre conventions... Findings also confirm central fixation bias, underscoring the influence of spatial placement on visual attention."
- **Problem:** The study measured attention and colour–genre/emotion associations, not intention to see a film, and it does not establish that gaze placement is the dominant driver ('mostly').
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as: 'Where your eye lands on a poster, and the colours used, shape your first impression of a film before you read a word — though the study measured attention and associations, not ticket buying.'"
