### 1. When shoppers spot an AI-made ad, they like it less — but still buy

**Journal:** *International Journal of Advertising* | **Published:** 2026-09-28
**Work ID:** W7214625312 | **DOI:** 10.1080/02650487.2026.2737583

**The study:** Researchers tested "synthetic" out-of-home advertising — AI-altered images of billboards and street installations that brands post on social media as if they were real. They found that the more creative an ad looked, the more persuasive it was, but also the more likely viewers were to realize it wasn't real. Consumers who caught on reported lower attitudes toward both the ad and the brand, yet purchase intent and willingness to engage on social media were unaffected; high creativity softened the attitude penalty.

**Why it matters:** Fake-but-spectacular stunt ads have become a staple of brand social feeds, and this suggests the reputational cost of being found out is real but narrow — it dents sentiment without visibly denting commercial intent.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Worth testing whether your own synthetic stunt creative crosses the believability line: the study suggests creativity is a double-edged lever that raises both persuasion and detection, so brands could pilot disclosure versus non-disclosure and track brand attitude separately from conversion rather than assuming one proxies for the other.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A reminder for anyone scrolling past a jaw-dropping billboard: many of the most shareable outdoor ads never existed. Noticing the trick makes people think less of the brand — but apparently not enough to change what they buy.

**Caveats:** Experimental study of a single ad format (out-of-home) in a social media context; attitudes and purchase intent are self-reported rather than observed purchases; sample and country details are not specified in the abstract.

### 2. Slashing the ad budget makes your stock look riskier, 231,000 firm-years suggest

**Journal:** *Journal of Advertising Research* | **Published:** 2026-09-25
**Work ID:** W7214298879 | **DOI:** 10.1080/00218499.2026.2713784

**The study:** An analysis of 231,323 US firm-year observations examined not the level of advertising spending but the logarithmic percentage change in it, isolating only dramatic swings. Firms that made massive cuts to advertising expenses subsequently showed higher stock returns — the signature of investors pricing in higher risk — an effect described as both economically and statistically significant and holding across multiple robustness tests.

**Why it matters:** It puts a capital-markets price tag on the familiar recession reflex of gutting the ad budget: abrupt cuts appear to raise a firm's perceived risk and therefore its cost of equity.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you are arguing against a sudden marketing cut, this gives a finance-department argument rather than a marketing one: the paper's implication is that budgets should be adjusted gradually, because the market reads a cliff-edge drop as a risk signal. Worth stress-testing with your own CFO on how your investors have historically reacted to spend changes.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For investors and observers, a brand that suddenly goes quiet in advertising may be telling the market something — and the market appears to notice, demanding higher returns to hold the stock.

**Caveats:** Observational archival data, so associations rather than clean causal identification; US-listed firms only; accounting-reported advertising expense is a crude measure that omits much modern marketing spend.

### 3. Let the AI do the guilt-tripping and keep the humans for the pep talk

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-10-02
**Work ID:** W7216305737 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105134

**The study:** Three studies — two scenario-based experiments (one Chinese sample, one international English-speaking online sample) and a field experiment using actual purchase data from a live-streaming retailer — crossed the disclosed creator of a green ad (AI-generated versus human-generated) with its emotional appeal (guilt versus encouragement). AI-generated guilt appeals beat human-generated ones at driving green purchase intention, apparently because an AI scold triggers less "green moral pressure." Human-generated encouragement beat AI-generated encouragement, working through greater green self-efficacy.

**Why it matters:** It reframes the AI-versus-human creative debate as a matching problem: the same disclosure that hurts one kind of message can help another, with the negative appeals being exactly where machine authorship pays.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** A concrete A/B test for sustainability and cause marketing: pair guilt or responsibility framings with disclosed AI authorship, and keep human signatures on uplifting, you-can-do-this messaging. The field-experiment leg makes this stronger than most AI-disclosure research, but it is one retailer in one format.
- **For general readers and consumers:** People bristle at being made to feel guilty by another person — but take the same reproach from a machine with less resentment. It is a small window into why moral pressure lands differently depending on who is applying it.

**Caveats:** Two of three studies are scenario-based with stated intentions; the field component comes from a single live-streaming retailer and category; effects may be culture-specific despite the international sample.

### 4. Abstract nature imagery outperforms actual forests in service ads

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-10-05
**Work ID:** W7220455699 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105138

**The study:** Two experiments compared service advertising using direct biophilia (literal images of nature), indirect biophilia (abstract natural patterns, nature-like materials, earthy color schemes) and a no-nature control. Indirect, abstract nature cues produced the most favorable ad evaluations and behavioral intentions, working through perceived novelty and mystery rather than the restorative calm usually attributed to nature imagery. Benefits were largest when the advertised service was conceptually aligned with nature.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the standard stock-photo waterfall may be the weaker option, and that suggestive texture and palette can do more persuasive work than literal greenery.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** A cheap creative test for service brands — hotels, spas, wellness, financial or health services — swapping literal nature photography for abstracted natural forms and palettes, and measuring novelty and curiosity as the mechanism. Treat it as a hypothesis to validate in-market, not an established rule.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Our pull toward nature in advertising seems to be driven less by relaxation than by intrigue: a hint of the natural world grabs attention more than the full picture does.

**Caveats:** Two experiments only, with ad evaluations and behavioral intentions rather than real bookings or purchases; sample size, sample source and country are not specified in the abstract; limited to service categories.

### 5. Kids recorded their own screens for four days — and found an ad every 2.6 minutes of gaming

**Journal:** *Health Promotion International* | **Published:** 2026-09-01
**Work ID:** W7214801403 | **DOI:** 10.1093/heapro/daag119

**The study:** Researchers in Wellington, New Zealand recruited 66 children aged 11–13 from 16 schools and had them screen-share their real-time smartphone use over Zoom for four consecutive days. Among the 41 who played game apps, children averaged 39 minutes of game-app play across the period and were exposed to advertising for 9.7 minutes of every hour spent gaming — about 16% of game time, or 22.9 ads per hour. The single biggest ad category was other mobile games (38% of all ads), but much of the rest was adult-oriented: finance, real estate, and even alcohol. Unprompted, a third of participants said they wanted online advertising changed, and every comment about it was negative.

**Why it matters:** This is one of the first objective, child-captured records of in-game ad exposure rather than an estimate from parents or platforms, and it shows mobile games function as a high-frequency ad channel delivering adult categories to pre-teens.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands buying programmatic in-app inventory may have far less control over audience age than they assume — the finding is a prompt to audit where mobile placements actually land and to anticipate regulatory pressure on in-game advertising to minors.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For parents: a child's 'game time' is partly ad time, and the ads aren't just for other games — they include finance, property and alcohol. Kids themselves notice and dislike it.

**Caveats:** Small sample (66 children, 41 with game-app data) from a single region of one country; four-day observation window; children knew they were being recorded, which may have altered their behavior; descriptive rather than causal.

### 6. Should your social ad show 10,000 likes or 10? It depends on whether the brand is status or substance

**Journal:** *Journal of Research in Interactive Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-09
**Work ID:** W7220966006 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jrim-09-2025-0592

**The study:** Five experiments run on real Chinese social platforms (Weibo, Xiaohongshu, WeChat Moments) tested how the visible number of other people interacting with an in-feed ad — what the authors call 'ad-interactor size' — affects purchase intention, using both real and fictitious brands across functional and symbolic product types. For symbolic, status-signaling brands, ads showing a small interactor count produced higher purchase intention, driven by consumers' need for uniqueness. For functional brands, the reverse held: a large interactor count lifted purchase intention via need for belonging.

**Why it matters:** Social proof is treated as universally good in paid social, but these results suggest high visible engagement can actively undercut ads for aspirational or identity-based brands.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Worth A/B testing whether surfacing or suppressing engagement counts changes conversion by brand type — a cheap creative-ops lever most teams never touch, though these are lab-style experiments on one country's platforms, not live campaign results.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A quiet explanation for why a luxury or niche brand can feel less appealing once everyone is liking it — and why popularity signals reassure us on practical purchases but deflate us on expressive ones.

**Caveats:** All studies conducted in China on Chinese platforms; experimental settings with stated purchase intention rather than observed purchases; some brands fictitious; per-condition sample sizes not reported in the abstract.

### 7. Ad creatives are splitting into three camps on generative AI — and one is deliberately getting more human

**Journal:** *Journal of Business Research* | **Published:** 2026-08-28
**Work ID:** W7204571530 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jbusres.2026.116483

**The study:** Researchers combined three years of netnographic observation in online advertising communities with interviews with advertising creatives to map how practitioners interpret generative AI. They found competing frames around AI's creative agency, its job threat, and where value now sits in creative output. These frames drive three distinct adaptive strategies: AI skilling (learning the tools), reskilling (shifting roles), and 'deep skilling' — deliberately cultivating human capabilities that generative AI cannot easily reproduce.

**Why it matters:** It gives agency and in-house creative leaders a vocabulary for why AI adoption stalls or splinters inside the same team, and suggests resistance is often a coherent strategy rather than mere reluctance.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Instead of a single AI mandate, consider which frame dominates each team and whether 'deep skilling' talent should be protected and priced differently from tool-fluent talent.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A look inside how the people who make the ads you see are reacting to AI — some retraining, some leaning harder into the things machines can't fake.

**Caveats:** Qualitative and interpretive, no quantitative outcomes; sample of creatives in online advertising communities, composition and size not fully specified in the abstract; findings describe perceptions and strategies, not effects on creative performance.

### 8. How much authority an agency gives its AI depends on where the client is from

**Journal:** *Journal of Global Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-21
**Work ID:** W7213885424 | **DOI:** 10.1080/08911762.2026.2733411

**The study:** Multi-sited ethnographic fieldwork across three globally oriented advertising agencies in Asia — participant observation plus semi-structured interviews with 30 practitioners — examined how AI governance works in practice. The researchers found governance is not standardized but relational: agency staff calibrate who decides, how much the AI is trusted, and who is accountable based on what they believe clients from different regions expect. A European financial services client operating under GDPR and a performance-focused Southeast Asian brand effectively get different levels of algorithmic autonomy on the same agency floor.

**Why it matters:** As agencies automate millions of campaign decisions daily, this shows the real constraint on AI authority is client origin and perceived regulatory culture, not the technology's capability.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brand-side marketers should ask their agencies explicitly what level of algorithmic autonomy is being applied to their account — the answer may be an assumption about their market rather than a negotiated policy.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Limited direct consumer angle, though it reveals that whether a human or an algorithm chose the ad you just saw can depend on the advertiser's home country.

**Caveats:** Qualitative ethnography in three agencies in Asia; all claims about client expectations are agency-side practitioner attributions, explicitly not verified with clients; findings are not statistically generalizable.

### 9. When a lesson looks like an ad, parents clamp down on it

**Journal:** *Young Consumers Insight and Ideas for Responsible Marketers* | **Published:** 2026-10-07
**Work ID:** W7116214838 | **DOI:** 10.1108/yc-02-2026-2987

**The study:** An experiment with 147 parents compared reactions to three content types: a straight advertisement, an 'edu-ad' (an educational lesson packaged in persuasive ad format), and a conventional educator lesson. A serial mediation model traced how perceived amusement and informational value, perceived manipulative intent, and presumed positive impact on children fed into parents' intentions to actively discuss or restrict the content. Edu-ads occupied a distinct middle category — processed partly like entertainment, partly like commercial advertising — with consequences for how much parents would mediate children's exposure.

**Why it matters:** Brands and nonprofits increasingly wrap educational messaging in ad formats; this suggests the format itself can trigger parental suspicion that blunts the educational payoff.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you're building branded educational content for kids, test whether the ad-like packaging that wins attention also raises perceived manipulative intent among the gatekeepers — parents — who decide whether the content survives.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For parents: a useful reminder that 'educational' content aimed at children often carries a persuasive frame, and that your instinct about whether something is teaching or selling shapes how you respond.

**Caveats:** Single experiment with N=147 across three conditions (small per-condition cells); measures stated mediation intentions rather than observed parenting behavior; stimulus-based scenario study; single-country sample.

### 10. When "diverse" virtual influencers backfire: disability avatars cut donation intent

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Behaviour* | **Published:** 2026-08-11
**Work ID:** W7202162669 | **DOI:** 10.1002/cb.70223

**The study:** Two online experiments tested how diversity attributes built into computer-generated (virtual) influencers affect charitable endorsements. In Study 1 (N=256), a virtual influencer coded as an ethnic minority raised perceived attractiveness and donation intention, while one coded with a disability lowered both. Study 2 (N=623) replicated the negative effect for disability, showed it ran through cognitive dissonance in viewers, and found that a strong fit between the endorser and the cause substantially reduced that dissonance.

**Why it matters:** Brands and nonprofits increasingly use synthetic avatars as a low-cost, controllable way to signal inclusivity — but the research suggests that diversity attributes are not interchangeable, and that one common choice can depress the very giving the campaign is meant to drive.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you're commissioning virtual influencers, treat "diversity" as a set of distinct design decisions, each needing its own pretest rather than a blanket inclusivity win; the mitigating factor here was endorser–cause fit, which is a testable lever — try pairing an avatar's represented identity with a cause it plausibly connects to and measure donation outcomes, not just sentiment.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The result is an uncomfortable mirror: audiences in these studies reacted worse to a simulated person with a disability, apparently because the synthetic-plus-disabled combination felt dissonant. It's a reminder that representation delivered by an algorithmically generated character can read very differently from representation by a real human.

**Caveats:** Online panel experiments using hypothetical charitable scenarios and stated donation intentions rather than actual giving; avatars and causes were researcher-created, so effects may hinge on specific stimulus design; cultural context of the samples is not clear from the abstract.

### 11. Watching someone else go green feels good enough — and that's the problem

**Journal:** *International Journal of Advertising* | **Published:** 2026-08-18
**Work ID:** W7203716020 | **DOI:** 10.1080/02650487.2026.2718009

**The study:** Using focus groups, in-depth interviews and netnographic observation of social media, researchers examined how audiences actually interpret "green influencer" sustainability content. They identified a consistent three-stage pattern: viewers feel moral elevation and admiration, then experience what the authors call vicarious moral satisfaction, then shift responsibility for action onto the influencer or onto institutions. Audiences aligned themselves morally with the message while explicitly positioning themselves as behaviorally disengaged.

**Why it matters:** Sustainability campaigns measured on engagement — likes, admiring comments, positive sentiment — may be generating exactly the emotional response that lets audiences off the hook, with high moral approval masking zero behavior change.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Question whether warm engagement metrics on purpose-driven influencer content are a leading indicator of anything; the implied test is to build content that assigns the viewer a concrete, small action rather than showcasing the influencer's virtue, and to measure downstream behavior (clicks to a product, sign-ups, repeat purchase) against admiration-only creative.
- **For general readers and consumers:** There's a tidy name now for the feeling of scrolling past someone composting, feeling genuinely inspired, and then doing nothing: vicarious moral satisfaction. Admiring other people's good behavior can function as a substitute for your own.

**Caveats:** Qualitative and interpretive — no sample sizes, control conditions or quantified effects reported in the abstract; findings describe how people talk about their responses, not measured behavior; context and country of participants unspecified, limiting generalizability.

### 12. Sports betting ads have made gambling feel like just another part of being young

**Journal:** *Health Promotion International* | **Published:** 2026-09-01
**Work ID:** W7207692856 | **DOI:** 10.1093/heapro/daag114

**The study:** Researchers synthesized 22 qualitative studies published between 2015 and 2025 on how gambling becomes normalized for people up to age 35, using reflexive thematic analysis. Four themes emerged: early exposure through family and peers; pervasive gambling advertising, especially embedded in sport, that frames betting as entertaining and glamorous; smartphones and apps that push gambling into previously off-limits spaces and social situations; and young people's own calls for stricter advertising controls and counter-marketing.

**Why it matters:** The synthesis points to advertising — particularly sports sponsorship and broadcast integration — as a central mechanism of normalization, which is precisely the terrain regulators in several markets are moving to restrict.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For anyone advertising in or around sport, the regulatory risk here is live: the evidence base increasingly names in-game betting promotion as a driver of youth normalization, and young people themselves are advocating for restrictions. Brands sharing sponsorship inventory with betting operators should think about adjacency and reputational spillover now rather than after the rules change.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If betting odds read like part of the match commentary to you, that's the point — the review describes how gambling got woven into sport, friendship groups and phones until it stopped registering as a distinct activity at all.

**Caveats:** This is a narrative review rather than new primary data; the underlying studies are qualitative and self-reported, cannot establish causation, and skew toward particular sociocultural settings; findings describe perceptions and social meanings, not measured gambling behavior or ad exposure.

### 13. The thing that keeps kids glued to a screen isn't the story — it's how much the picture moves

**Journal:** *Information Systems Research* | **Published:** 2026-09-25
**Work ID:** W7214311385 | **DOI:** 10.1287/isre.2023.0718

**The study:** Researchers combined three methods to test whether raw visual motion drives children's video engagement. They analyzed more than 32,000 videos from leading children's YouTube channels and found higher optical-flow motion predicted viewership even after controlling for visual, audio and emotional features; they then produced educational videos with experimentally manipulated background motion and ran randomized YouTube ad campaigns, where higher-motion versions earned significantly higher view-rates and views; finally, a lab eye-tracking study with children showed motion works by shortening long attentional lapses — pulling kids back to the screen faster — rather than preventing look-aways, and without stealing attention from instructional content.

**Why it matters:** It identifies a concrete, measurable production variable — on-screen motion — that platforms, creators and advertisers can dial up or down, and that regulators could scrutinize as a design feature engineered to hold children's attention.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For brands making child-directed or family content, motion intensity is a testable creative lever distinct from narrative or characters — worth A/B testing in paid campaigns, but also worth weighing against reputational risk if "attention engineering" aimed at kids becomes a regulatory flashpoint.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Parents often blame content or characters for screen fixation; this suggests a more mechanical culprit — fast-moving visuals that snap a wandering child back to the screen. It also hints that educational videos with livelier backgrounds may hold attention without hurting learning.

**Caveats:** The field experiment was run through one platform's (YouTube) advertising infrastructure, so view-rate is an ad metric rather than sustained viewing; the eye-tracking study is a lab setting and sample size is not stated in the abstract; short observation horizon and no evidence on long-term learning or wellbeing effects.

### 14. Tobacco ads still show up on Japanese streaming — unless you tell the platform you're 18

**Journal:** *Tobacco Control* | **Published:** 2026-08-21
**Work ID:** W7203907887 | **DOI:** 10.1136/tc-2026-060050

**The study:** Researchers manually coded every commercial break in catch-up streaming programs from four major Japanese broadcasters on the ad-supported platform TVer over five days in March 2025, measuring total commercial duration across nine ad categories. When no age was declared, tobacco advertising accounted for 1.2% of total commercial time, concentrated in variety and comedy programming. When the researchers logged in with a declared age of 18 — below Japan's legal smoking age — no tobacco ads appeared at all.

**Why it matters:** It shows age-gating works when triggered, but that the default anonymous viewing experience on a mainstream streaming platform still serves tobacco ads to anyone, including minors — evidence that industry self-regulation leaves a gap in digital channels.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** A practical warning for any advertiser relying on platform-level age gating: controls that depend on voluntary self-declared login data can be functionally absent for a large share of the audience. Brands in age-restricted categories should audit what their ads look like to a logged-out viewer.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Streaming feels like a cleaner, more controlled ad environment than broadcast TV, but the targeting safeguards consumers assume are in place often only switch on if you volunteer personal data first.

**Caveats:** Single platform, single country, and a five-day observation window; manual coding of commercial duration rather than exposure data from actual youth viewers; shows ad availability, not measured youth exposure or behavioral effect.

### 15. Telling people an ad was made by AI made them trust it more, not less

**Journal:** *Journal of Current Issues & Research in Advertising* | **Published:** 2026-09-03
**Work ID:** W7207577485 | **DOI:** 10.1080/10641734.2026.2711967

**The study:** Two experiments tested how disclosing AI involvement affects responses to advertising. Study 1 found that disclosing an ad was AI-generated increased perceived ad trustworthiness and attractiveness, which in turn improved attitudes and raised purchase intention. Study 2 compared disclosure framings and found that "behavior" disclosures — explaining what the AI did with data — raised privacy concerns, while "benefit" framings, which emphasize what consumers gain, produced more favorable ad attitudes.

**Why it matters:** With AI-disclosure rules tightening in several markets, this suggests compliance need not be a cost — but the wording matters, and the instinct to explain the mechanics of data use may backfire relative to explaining the payoff.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Treat mandated AI labels as creative copy, not legal boilerplate: test benefit-framed disclosures against procedural ones, and watch whether the apparent transparency bonus holds as AI-generated ads become the norm rather than a novelty.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The label on an ad shapes how you judge it. A brand that admits using AI may read as more honest than one that stays quiet — at least for now, while disclosure is still unusual.

**Caveats:** Two experiments with sample sizes, recruitment source and country not specified in the abstract; outcomes are stated purchase intention and attitudes, not actual purchases; likely hypothetical ad stimuli, and the trust benefit may reflect the current novelty of disclosure rather than a durable effect.

### 16. In VR, travelers stop asking whether to trust the listing

**Journal:** *Journal Of Vacation Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-25
**Work ID:** W7204201453 | **DOI:** 10.1177/13567667261480389

**The study:** In a within-subject lab experiment, 306 participants viewed Airbnb listings in three formats — static images, 360-degree video, and full VR via a high-fidelity head-mounted display — and researchers modeled the paths from interactivity and vividness through mental imagery, arousal and pleasure to approach tendency. With static ads, intent was pleasure-driven and strongly conditioned on perceived trust in the transaction. In the immersive formats, responses shifted toward arousal-driven, more heuristic processing, and trust's moderating role weakened substantially.

**Why it matters:** It suggests immersive media doesn't just make listings more appealing — it changes how consumers appraise risk, functioning as a kind of ambient verification in peer-to-peer markets where trust is normally the bottleneck.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For platforms and travel brands, immersive formats may be most valuable precisely where trust is weakest: new hosts, unfamiliar destinations, unreviewed listings. But the same mechanism raises a governance question — if immersion suppresses trust scrutiny, platforms need stronger guarantees that what's shown is what's delivered.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Putting on a headset to tour a rental may make you feel you've verified it when you haven't. The sensation of "having been there" can quietly substitute for the checks — reviews, host history — you'd normally run.

**Caveats:** Controlled lab setting with a within-subject design (participants saw all three formats, which can inflate contrast effects); outcome is approach tendency, not booking or spending; single category (Airbnb listings) and a PLS-SEM path model rather than a behavioral field test; country and sample composition not specified.

### 17. Telling people your Instagram model isn't real cuts both ways

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing Communications* | **Published:** 2026-09-10
**Work ID:** W7212138832 | **DOI:** 10.1080/13527266.2026.2729146

**The study:** In an online experiment with 606 participants, people saw a brand's Instagram post featuring a hyper-realistic human figure under one of three conditions: no disclosure, a generic "AI-generated" label, or a detailed disclosure explicitly stating that the depicted person does not exist. Trust was measured before and after participants were told the image was synthetic. Detailed disclosure had no direct effect on trust; instead it worked through two opposing channels — it reduced suspicions of manipulative intent (protecting trust) while simultaneously making the image feel more artificial (eroding trust). Younger consumers showed a stronger protective effect from the reduced manipulation inference.

**Why it matters:** As AI-content disclosure rules tighten across platforms and regulators, brands need to know that how specific a label is changes which psychological lever it pulls — and the two levers push trust in opposite directions.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands running synthetic imagery should treat disclosure wording as a testable variable, not a compliance checkbox: a vague "AI-generated" tag and an explicit "this person does not exist" produce different trust dynamics, and the net effect may vary by audience age and category. Worth A/B testing disclosure language in live social campaigns before regulation dictates it.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The models in your feed may never have existed — and this research shows that being told so makes most people feel the brand is less manipulative but the image less real. It is a useful reminder to check for AI labels and to notice how much of your trust rests on a face.

**Caveats:** Online experiment with a single Instagram-style stimulus; trust was self-reported rather than measured through purchase or engagement behavior; one brand/post context; short time horizon with trust reassessed immediately after exposure.

### 18. Dating apps beat social media for reach — but social media delivered the completions

**Journal:** *Health Marketing Quarterly* | **Published:** 2026-09-24
**Work ID:** W7214195424 | **DOI:** 10.1080/07359683.2026.2732799

**The study:** Researchers designed and ran a 2023 promotional campaign to recruit men who have sex with men, transgender and non-binary people across Europe into an online HIV prevention survey (PROTECT), using a logo, slogan, website and tailored creative placed on dating apps, social media and in print. The campaign drew 15,458 completed surveys: 55% came via dating app links and 38% via social media. Dating app ads had a click-through rate of 9.0% versus 2.1% on social media — but people who arrived from social media ads were more likely to finish the survey.

**Why it matters:** It is a rare real-world media comparison showing that the channel with the headline click-through rate is not necessarily the channel with the best downstream conversion — a trap in any funnel-based media plan.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** A concrete argument against optimizing media buys on click-through rate alone: here the high-CTR channel produced lower completion quality. Marketers should pull completion or conversion rates by source before reallocating budget, and consider dating apps as a serious, under-used paid channel for reaching specific communities.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The notion of "hard to reach" audiences often just means advertisers are buying the wrong media; when the campaign went where people already were, more than 15,000 responded.

**Caveats:** A single public-health campaign in Europe with no randomization between channels — audience self-selection, not creative or channel effects, could drive the CTR-versus-completion difference; the "product" is a survey, not a purchase, so generalization to commercial advertising is uncertain.

### 19. Major-key music needs bright color; minor-key needs muted — mismatched travel ads fall flat

**Journal:** *Journal Of Vacation Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-14
**Work ID:** W7213228572 | **DOI:** 10.1177/13567667261487703

**The study:** Two between-subjects experiments (N=308 and N=368) crossed musical tonality (major vs. minor key) with color saturation (high vs. low) in tourism promotional videos and measured intention to visit the destination. Congruent pairings won: major-key music with high saturation and minor-key music with low saturation both outperformed mismatched combinations. The two winning combinations worked through different routes — bright-and-major built a sense of "destination intimacy," while muted-and-minor drove perceived novelty — and incongruent pairings disrupted processing fluency and weakened persuasion.

**Why it matters:** Video ads are usually scored and color-graded by separate people at separate stages; this suggests the two decisions interact and that getting them out of sync actively costs persuasive power.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** A testable brief for video teams: lock soundtrack tonality and color grade as a paired decision, and pick the pair based on the emotional job — warmth and familiarity versus novelty and intrigue. Worth validating with in-platform video tests before assuming the lab effect scales.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The pull you feel toward a destination in a 30-second video may owe less to the beach than to whether the music and the color grade agree with each other.

**Caveats:** Lab-style experiments measuring stated visit intention rather than booking behavior; tourism video context only; sample details and country not specified in the abstract.

### 20. Labeling an ad "AI-generated" mostly produces a shrug — except among AI-savvy consumers

**Journal:** *Journal of Global Scholars of Marketing Science* | **Published:** 2026-09-06
**Work ID:** W7211870608 | **DOI:** 10.1080/21639159.2026.2699660

**The study:** An experiment tested consumer responses to online banner ads whose elements were disclosed as AI-generated, against a non-AI-disclosed control, while measuring participants' dispositional persuasion knowledge (their general skepticism of marketing), prior experience with AI tools, and trust in AI. Overall the researchers found general ambivalence toward AI-disclosed ads regardless of which modality was labeled. But when AI-generated text specifically was disclosed, consumers with higher dispositional persuasion knowledge, more AI experience and more trust in AI responded more positively on several advertising outcomes.

**Why it matters:** With platform rules and proposed laws mandating AI disclosure, the fear is a blanket backlash; this suggests the average response is neutral and that the most AI-literate consumers may actually react favorably to disclosed AI copy.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Disclosure may be less of a brand risk than feared — but the effect is segment-dependent, so brands should profile audience AI familiarity before deciding how prominently to label AI-assisted creative, and treat AI-written text and AI-generated imagery as separate disclosure decisions.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If you use AI tools yourself, you are likely to judge an AI-made ad more generously than someone who doesn't — familiarity, not the technology, is doing the work.

**Caveats:** Single experimental study on banner ads with self-reported advertising outcomes rather than click or purchase behavior; sample size, composition and country not stated in the abstract; hypothetical ad exposure rather than real campaign conditions.

### 21. Eye-tracking finds moviegoers read poster colors differently than the industry assumes

**Journal:** *ReMark - Revista Brasileira de Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-15
**Work ID:** W7213276828 | **DOI:** 10.5585/2026.31526

**The study:** Two within-subjects experiments used biometric eye-tracking with 41 participants aged 18–40 to test how color palettes on movie posters map to film genres (action, animation, romantic comedy, science fiction, horror) and to emotions from Plutchik's wheel using monochrome posters. Several associations departed from industry convention — green was linked to sadness and orange to trust — and the study confirmed a strong central fixation bias, meaning placement in the poster's center heavily shaped where attention went.

**Why it matters:** Entertainment marketing leans on inherited color conventions for genre signaling; this is direct attention and association evidence that some of those conventions may not match how audiences actually decode a poster.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Creative teams working in key art and packaging could pretest color-genre assumptions rather than inheriting them, and should remember that composition and center placement can override color in driving attention.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The poster that made you want to see a film worked on you before you read a word of it — mostly through where your eye landed first.

**Caveats:** Very small sample (N=41), narrow age range (18–40), single country context, within-subjects lab design with artificial monochrome stimuli, and no link to ticket-buying behavior.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**

- **AI authorship is not the thing consumers punish.** The previous digest established this through backlash analysis and qualitative work — "Coke's AI Christmas ad drew 7,800 comments" (W7204147563), "Older consumers don't reject AI-made ads" (W7214074450), "Consumers preferred AI-made ads to human-made ones" (W7203605245). The new batch converges from the experimental side: "Telling people an ad was made by AI made them trust it more, not less" (W7207577485) and "Labeling an ad 'AI-generated' mostly produces a shrug" (W7211870608) both find neutral-to-positive responses to disclosure. The accumulating evidence now points the same way across methods, samples and countries.
- **Processing fluency and congruence keep showing up as the mechanism.** "Major-key music needs bright color" (W7213228572) joins last run's "When a clever visual metaphor confuses people" (W7204599802) and "Cute mascots should talk about warmth" (W7204255947): mismatched creative elements disrupt fluency and cost persuasion, regardless of which elements are mismatched.
- **Headline metrics keep failing as proxies for outcomes.** "Dating apps beat social media for reach — but social media delivered the completions" (W7214195424) extends the previous run's "Eight brands switched off their branded search ads" (W7204491303) and "Text-heavy social ads get remembered — just not the brand" (W7212195252). Clicks, recall and reach each decouple from the thing the advertiser actually wants.
- **Where the ad budget goes under pressure.** "Slashing the ad budget makes your stock look riskier" (W7214298879) pairs directly with "When family firms miss their targets, they cut ads and spend on CSR instead" (W7212229180): one study shows who cuts first, the other prices what the cut costs in the equity market.
- **Virtual endorsers work only under conditions.** "When 'diverse' virtual influencers backfire" (W7202162669) reinforces "Pairing a virtual influencer with a human one works — but only if the ad keeps its distance" (W7213402036). Both find synthetic endorsers are a set of separate design bets, not a uniform modernization.

**Advances**

- **From practitioner unease to organizational structure.** Last run captured tourism marketers improvising ethics around deepfake imagery (W7213453650). This run moves to how firms actually organize around AI: "Ad creatives are splitting into three camps on generative AI" (W7204571530) names skilling, reskilling and "deep skilling" as competing strategies, and "How much authority an agency gives its AI depends on where the client is from" (W7213885424) shows governance is calibrated to perceived client regulatory culture rather than set by policy.
- **Disclosure research gets granular.** Having established that labels don't automatically hurt, the field has moved to wording and modality: benefit versus behavior framing (W7207577485), generic "AI-generated" tags versus "this person does not exist" (W7212138832), and AI-generated text versus imagery as separate disclosure decisions (W7211870608).
- **Green communications moves from message components to messenger.** "Guilt-based green ads only work when all three ingredients are present" (W7172220713) specified what has to be in the message; "Let the AI do the guilt-tripping" (W7216305737) now shows the same appeal lands differently depending on whether a human or a machine signs it — with a field-experiment leg using real purchase data.

**Counterpoints**

- **Detection is not disclosure.** "When shoppers spot an AI-made ad, they like it less — but still buy" (W7214625312) cuts against the broadly reassuring disclosure literature above: being told costs nothing, being caught costs brand attitude. That distinction was not visible in last run's set.
- **Social proof is conditional, not weak.** "What actually makes mobile ads work" (W7204247420) found subjective norms barely registered. "Should your social ad show 10,000 likes or 10?" (W7220966006) suggests the reason may be aggregation: visible engagement lifts functional brands and depresses symbolic ones, so averaged across categories the effect looks close to zero.
- **Engagement on purpose-driven content may be an anti-indicator.** "Watching someone else go green feels good enough" (W7203716020) complicates the previous run's green-claims meta-analysis (W7202096496) by arguing admiration and positive sentiment can substitute for action rather than predict it.

**New themes**

- **Children and young people as a measured, not estimated, advertising audience** — effectively absent last run beyond the #womenshealth content analysis (W7214105022). Now five studies: in-game ad exposure captured by children themselves (W7214801403), motion as an engineered attention lever in kids' video (W7214311385), default-setting tobacco ads on Japanese streaming (W7203907887), gambling normalization among under-35s (W7207692856) and parental gatekeeping of edu-ads (W7116214838).
- **Immersive media changing how risk is appraised** — "In VR, travelers stop asking whether to trust the listing" (W7204201453) is a genuinely new line with no prior-run analogue.
- **Capital-markets consequences of marketing decisions** — W7214298879 is the first finance-side advertising study across these two runs.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Five studies in this batch mark a shift from estimating children's advertising exposure to measuring it directly — and the measurements are unflattering. Children screen-sharing their own phones logged 22.9 ads per hour of mobile gaming, including finance, real estate and alcohol ("Kids recorded their own screens for four days," W7214801403); tobacco ads ran in 1.2% of commercial time on a mainstream Japanese streaming platform but vanished the moment an age was declared ("Tobacco ads still show up on Japanese streaming," W7203907887); randomized YouTube ad campaigns showed raw visual motion mechanically pulls children's attention back to the screen ("The thing that keeps kids glued to a screen," W7214311385); a synthesis of 22 studies traced youth gambling normalization substantially to sports advertising (W7207692856); and parents reacted to ad-formatted educational content with measurable suspicion ("When a lesson looks like an ad," W7116214838). Together they describe an environment where safeguards are opt-in, exposure is high-frequency, and the design variables that hold children's attention are now quantified.

**Why pitch this now:** Children's online-safety codes, gambling-advertising restrictions and scrutiny of in-game and in-app ad inventory are all moving through regulators in the UK, EU and Australia at once — and brands buying programmatic mobile inventory are, on this evidence, far less sure than they assume about who is seeing their ads.

**Angle:** A reported feature on the collapse of the gap between what the ad industry assumes about age-gating and what researchers can now measure — written for media buyers and brand-safety leads, with a parallel track for parents who have never seen the per-hour numbers. The strongest reporting hook is methodological: children, not platforms, produced the exposure data.

**Potential headlines:**
- Children recorded their own phones for four days. The ads were for alcohol, finance and real estate.
- The age check works perfectly. Almost nobody triggers it.
- Your child's game time is ad time — and nobody is counting it but researchers

**Potential outlets:**
- **Digiday** — the programmatic in-app and age-signal failure is squarely their beat, and the buy-side implication (you don't know the age of your audience) is a story their readers can act on.
- **Wired** — the optical-flow motion finding gives them a design-and-attention-engineering story with randomized field evidence behind it, not just anecdote.
- **The Guardian** — has sustained coverage of gambling advertising and children's screen exposure, and would run the parent-facing exposure numbers alongside the regulatory thread.
- **Ad Age** — well positioned for the industry-accountability framing: self-regulation in age-restricted categories measured and found wanting on a mainstream platform.
