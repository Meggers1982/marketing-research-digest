### Study 1: The thank-you note that costs you money — and the one that makes you money
**Work ID:** W7214276420 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Point estimate framed as revenue destruction** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "determines whether an ask in a gratitude message adds revenue or destroys it"
- **Abstract says:** "among donors who have already given, point estimates indicate lower giving. The CTA helps one segment but is associated with lower giving in the other"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports only directional point estimates for the already-given segment, and describes it as "associated with" lower giving. "Destroys it" asserts a demonstrated revenue loss, which the digest's own caveat then walks back.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "adds revenue or is associated with lower giving" to match the abstract's hedged language."

**Issue B. Mechanism stated as a data finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The data suggests donors have an internal sense of appropriate cadence, and organizations that violate it pay for the intrusion."
- **Abstract says:** "they may backfire when the request asks for giving beyond a donor's typical cadence and violates expectations about when requests are appropriate"
- **Problem:** Cadence expectations are the authors' proposed explanation in the framing of the paper; the abstract reports no mediation or direct test of donor expectations.
- **Suggested fix:** "Attribute it: "The authors suggest donors hold expectations about appropriate cadence, though the experiment does not test that mechanism directly.""

### Study 2: Swap a vague word for a precise one and the product starts to feel one of a kind
**Work ID:** W7219889147 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Unstated alternative explanation ruled out** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the authors trace the effect to categorical depth rather than to the words simply sounding more impressive"
- **Abstract says:** "Four experiments provide robust evidence supporting the "precise-unique effect" and its underlying psycholinguistic mechanism driven by categorical depth."
- **Problem:** The abstract establishes categorical depth as the mechanism but says nothing about testing or excluding an "impressiveness" account.
- **Suggested fix:** "Drop the contrast: "the authors attribute the effect to categorical depth.""

**Issue B. Direction of moderation added** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "most strongly among consumers with a high need for unique products"
- **Abstract says:** "consumers' need for product uniqueness moderates the downstream effects of language precision on perceived attractiveness and behavioral intention via perceived uniqueness"
- **Problem:** The abstract states that need for uniqueness moderates the downstream path but does not specify the direction; the digest supplies it.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say the downstream effect on attractiveness and intention "depended on consumers' need for product uniqueness" without asserting the direction, or note the direction is not stated in the abstract."

### Study 13: "2 hours and 59 minutes" feels longer than "3 hours" — the hidden cost of adding a smaller time unit
**Work ID:** W7210281977 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Untested comparison in consumer example** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The same wait feels longer written as "1 hour and 45 minutes" than as "under 2 hours""
- **Abstract says:** "describing time with a larger and a smaller unit (e.g., “by 8 PM on June 30”, “2 hours and 59 minutes”) rather than with a larger unit alone (e.g., “by the end of day on June 30”, “3 hours”) increases perceived duration"
- **Problem:** The abstract's contrast is between a two-unit description and the same duration stated in the larger unit alone (e.g., "2 hours"). "Under 2 hours" adds an approximation/less-than framing that was not part of the tested manipulation, so the illustration goes beyond the evidence.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change the comparison to a plain larger-unit statement, e.g. "The same wait feels longer written as '1 hour and 45 minutes' than as '2 hours.'""

### Study 15: Firms with cash to spare are the least responsive to their own environmental scorecards
**Work ID:** W7214077911 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Over-specified reading of the curve shapes** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Green innovation followed a U-shape when performance fell short of aspirations and an inverted U-shape when it exceeded them — meaning both mild shortfalls and extreme success dampened effort."
- **Abstract says:** "the results reveal a U-shaped relationship between performance below aspirations and GI, and an inverted U-shaped relationship when performance exceeds aspirations"
- **Problem:** The abstract does not specify the orientation of the x-axis (magnitude of shortfall vs. level of performance), so which end of each curve corresponds to "mild" or "extreme" cannot be determined from the abstract. The added gloss, and the related "motivate action mainly at the extremes" line, assert a specific pattern the abstract does not support — and the inverted-U above aspirations implies that mild success also dampens effort, which the gloss omits.
- **Suggested fix:** "Report the two curve shapes without interpreting which end is high or low, e.g. "the relationship was U-shaped below aspirations and inverted U-shaped above them — in both cases effort varied non-linearly with distance from the target," and soften "motivate action mainly at the extremes.""

**Issue B. Organizational slack described as cash** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Firms with cash to spare are the least responsive to their own environmental scorecards"
- **Abstract says:** "Organizational slack weakens both patterns, reducing firms’ responsiveness to both negative and positive environmental performance feedback."
- **Problem:** Organizational slack is a broader construct than spare cash (it covers unabsorbed, absorbed and potential resources); the abstract does not say the measure is cash holdings.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "resource-rich firms" or "firms with slack resources" in the headline rather than "cash to spare.""

### Study 16: On Etsy, Talking Too Much About Handcraft Hurts Sales
**Work ID:** W7214240842 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Market appeal reported as sales in headline** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "On Etsy, Talking Too Much About Handcraft Hurts Sales"
- **Abstract says:** "we examine how the intensity of craft cues ... and industrial cues ... impact market appeal ... we find an inverted U-shaped relationship: craft and industrial cue emphasis increase appeal up to a point, after which additional emphasis reduces it"
- **Problem:** The outcome is market appeal, a platform-derived measure, not revenue or units sold — as the digest's own Caveats acknowledge. The headline asserts a sales effect the study did not measure.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rewrite as "On Etsy, Talking Too Much About Handcraft Dents Your Appeal to Buyers.""

**Issue B. Unsupported equivalence claim** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "refusing to sound "commercial" can cost you as much as sounding too commercial"
- **Abstract says:** "sellers benefit neither from overemphasizing craft cues nor from avoiding industrial cues"
- **Problem:** The abstract says both extremes are suboptimal but makes no claim that the two costs are of comparable magnitude.
- **Suggested fix:** "Drop the magnitude comparison: "refusing to sound 'commercial' carries a cost too, just as sounding too commercial does.""
