### 1. Procurement managers admit they keep buying from suppliers they suspect of greenwashing

**Journal:** *Industrial Marketing Management* | **Published:** 2026-09-18
**Work ID:** W7213538226 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.indmarman.2026.09.002

**The study:** Researchers conducted 19 semi-structured interviews with EU-based procurement managers, corporate sustainability managers and ESG consultants to understand "greenwashing tolerance" — the deliberate decision to keep doing business with a supplier whose sustainability claims look dubious. They found tolerance is propped up by relationship history and switching costs, which lower scrutiny and make confrontation expensive in the short run. Tolerance collapses mainly when a buyer concludes the deception was intentional and when the reputational exposure of being associated with it rises; only then does governance escalate from quiet remediation to sanctions and termination.

**Why it matters:** ESG claims in supply chains are increasingly treated as facts by regulators, investors and downstream customers, yet this suggests the buyers best positioned to police them often choose not to. It reframes greenwashing as a relational problem, not just a disclosure problem.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If your firm's sustainability story depends on supplier disclosures, ask whether your own procurement team has the mandate, evidence and escalation path to challenge a long-standing supplier — the interviewees suggest layered verification and predefined escalation triggers are what convert suspicion into action. Worth auditing how many supplier ESG claims have ever been formally contested.
- **For general readers and consumers:** When a company tells you its supply chain is green, that assurance may rest on a claim someone internally privately doubts but finds too costly to challenge — a reminder that certification labels sit on top of commercial relationships people don't want to break.

**Caveats:** Small qualitative sample (19 interviews), EU only, self-reported accounts of sensitive behavior; the output is a conceptual framework and research propositions rather than measured effects on outcomes.

### 2. Micromanaging how salespeople spend their day makes the job feel meaningless — and performance suffers

**Journal:** *The Journal of Marketing Theory and Practice* | **Published:** 2026-09-09
**Work ID:** W7211996947 | **DOI:** 10.1080/10696679.2026.2730584

**The study:** A survey of 307 B2B salespeople, analyzed with structural equation modeling, tested how three styles of sales management control affect whether reps find their work meaningful. Outcome control (judging results) and capability control (developing skills) both raised perceived job meaningfulness, while activity control — dictating and monitoring day-to-day tasks — reduced it. Meaningfulness in turn predicted resilience, which was linked to higher job performance and lower intention to quit.

**Why it matters:** Sales organizations under pressure often respond by tightening activity tracking; this suggests that lever is the one most likely to drain motivation and increase churn in an already high-turnover role.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Test whether your CRM activity quotas and call-logging mandates are buying you visibility at the cost of retention — and whether shifting the same managerial energy into coaching and capability-building produces better numbers. The correlational design means this is a hypothesis to run on your own salesforce data, not a settled result.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A concrete data point in the wider argument about workplace surveillance: being measured on outcomes or growth feels meaningful, while being measured on activity feels like being watched.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional self-report survey — cannot establish causation; performance and turnover intention are self-reported rather than observed; single sample with limited detail on country and industry mix.

### 3. Hard-closing salespeople damage buyer trust — and the buyers say so themselves

**Journal:** *Journal of Business and Industrial Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-04
**Work ID:** W7172448511 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jbim-03-2026-0200

**The study:** Researchers collected matched survey data from 205 buyer–supplier pairs, asking the supplier side about salespeople's orientations and selling behaviors and the buyer side about trust and repurchase intention. Consultative selling raised both credibility-based and benevolence-based trust, while transactional, deal-pushing behavior was negatively associated with both. Credibility trust (belief in competence) predicted repurchase intention more strongly than benevolence trust (belief in goodwill).

**Why it matters:** Because the two sides of each relationship were surveyed separately, the finding is harder to dismiss as a salesperson's flattering self-assessment: the behaviors reps use to close faster appear to cost them the trust that drives renewal.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Look at whether your incentive structure rewards transactional pushing in accounts you intend to keep for years, and consider that buyers weight demonstrated competence above perceived goodwill — advisory expertise and proof of capability may retain accounts better than relationship warmth.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Buyers can tell the difference between a salesperson solving their problem and one working a quota, and it changes whether they come back — a dynamic that plays out in consumer sales too.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey, so causal direction is unproven; regionally bounded sample; trust and repurchase intention are stated attitudes and intentions, not observed reorders.

### 4. Keeping the same account team in place improves the customer experience — but discounts can undercut it

**Journal:** *European Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-07-25
**Work ID:** W7171153525 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ejm-12-2024-0997

**The study:** A mixed-methods study of B2B customers in the UK used a two-wave, time-lagged survey (predictors and outcomes measured at different points) followed by interviews with a subset of respondents. Stability in the supplier's customer-facing team strengthened the effect of what the supplier offered on customers' factual, emotional and social experience, though not on their "sagacious" (insight-based) experience. Financial incentives had a split effect: they amplified the behavioral payoff of social experience but weakened the link between insight-based experience and buyer behavior.

**Why it matters:** It suggests two common levers — account team continuity and discounting — pull in different directions, and that price incentives can crowd out the value customers place on a supplier's expertise.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Before defending margin with discounts in strategic accounts, consider whether that spend is diluting the advisory value you've built; and treat account team stability as a deliberate investment rather than an HR accident. The time-lagged design strengthens the case, but this is one UK sample.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Continuity matters: dealing with the same people over time genuinely changes how good a service feels, which is part of why constant account-manager churn is so irritating.

**Caveats:** Single country (UK); sample size not reported in the abstract; survey-based measures of experience and behavior rather than transaction records; constructs such as "sagacious experience" are researcher-defined and may not map cleanly onto managerial categories.

### 5. What Salespeople Say — and When They Say It — Decides Who Converts

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing Research* | **Published:** 2026-08-21
**Work ID:** W7203901346 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00222437261481444

**The study:** Researchers analyzed 783 recorded B2B lead-calling conversations comprising 11,566 individual speaking turns, coding salesperson speech into linguistic acts (legitimizing, piquing curiosity, nudging) and paralinguistic ones (volume intensity, speech rate, pitch), then modeling how each affected "lead receptivity" turn by turn and conversion at the call level. A one-standard-deviation rise in lead receptivity raised conversion likelihood by 13.44%. Timing mattered enormously: highly specific nudges lifted receptivity by 11.82% early in a call but cut it by 81.40% late in the call, and talking faster was neutral early but reduced receptivity by 8.26% later on.

**Why it matters:** It converts vague sales-call coaching into timing-specific rules drawn from real conversations, and shows the same tactic can help or backfire depending on where in the call it lands.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Sales leaders can mine their own call recordings for these patterns and rebuild scripts and coaching around conversational phase rather than a fixed checklist — testing, for example, whether front-loading specific asks and slowing down in the back half of calls improves conversion in their own pipeline.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For anyone who has been on the receiving end of a cold call: the discomfort you feel when a rep pushes a concrete "so can we book Tuesday?" late in a conversation is measurable — and it is the single biggest documented killer of the sale.

**Caveats:** Observational data from a single lead-calling setting, so tactics are correlated with receptivity rather than randomized; the industry, product and language context of the calls limits how far the specific percentages generalize.

### 6. Three-Quarters of a Japanese Luxury Resale Chain's Revenue Came From Visitors, Not Locals

**Journal:** *Asia Pacific Journal of Marketing and Logistics* | **Published:** 2026-08-28
**Work ID:** W7204571119 | **DOI:** 10.1108/apjml-06-2026-1628

**The study:** Researchers analyzed a proprietary, complete four-year transaction record (348,745 records, 2018–2021) from a major Japanese second-hand luxury retailer, segmenting buyers by channel and residency into 12 personas and using a gradient-boosted model to characterize the corporate wholesale channel. Non-resident buyers accounted for 74% of consumer revenue (47.9–65.5% under stricter exclusions for likely procurement buying). When COVID-19 halted travel, the travel-dependent segment collapsed while domestic demand persisted; the wholesale-clearing channel recovered a median 54% of list price and was highly predictable (AUC 0.88).

**Why it matters:** It puts hard numbers on how dependent Asia-Pacific luxury resale is on cross-border shoppers, and gives retailers a quantified salvage value for unsold inventory plus a real-time demand indicator in the duty-free transaction share.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Luxury and resale operators should test residency-by-channel segmentation on their own transaction data: concentrating authentication-heavy service on cross-border buyers, treating high-value repeat non-residents as key accounts, and pricing wholesale clearance against a known recovery rate rather than guesswork.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The booming "circular economy" story around second-hand luxury looks different up close — in this market, resale demand was powered largely by travelers and cross-border buyers, not by local shoppers trading up sustainably.

**Caveats:** Single retailer in a single country; buyers are identified by name-keying, and the split between genuine tourists and surrogate/daigou shoppers is bounded but not resolved; the period includes the unusual COVID travel shock.

### 7. Salespeople Proud of Their Employer Sell More — Unless They're Prouder of Their Profession

**Journal:** *Marketing Intelligence & Planning* | **Published:** 2026-08-11
**Work ID:** W7202160517 | **DOI:** 10.1108/mip-10-2025-0815

**The study:** A survey of 239 salespeople across 62 Brazilian retail stores selling building, electrical and painting materials tested how perceived retailer prestige — employees' beliefs about how outsiders rate their firm's status — translates into sales results. Prestige improved sales performance and reduced turnover intentions indirectly, through salespeople identifying with the organization. Counterintuitively, the link between organizational identification and sales performance was weaker among salespeople who strongly identified with their profession, though professional identification did not affect turnover intentions.

**Why it matters:** It suggests employer-brand investment pays off on the sales floor mainly via a sense of belonging — and that the payoff is muted for staff whose primary loyalty is to their craft rather than the company.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Retail and sales leaders can treat employer prestige as a performance lever, not just a recruiting one, while testing differentiated motivation: company-identity appeals for organization-oriented staff and craft, mastery and professional-development framing for those who see themselves as professionals first.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Why does a store's reputation shape the service you get? Staff who feel outsiders admire their employer behave differently — and workers who define themselves by their trade rather than their badge respond to entirely different motivations.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey in one country and one retail category, so causal direction cannot be established; self-reported measures including sales performance; results reflect stated intentions for turnover rather than observed departures.

### 8. An AI model beat seasoned B2B sales pros at calling which deals would close

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing Analytics* | **Published:** 2026-08-27
**Work ID:** W7204437044 | **DOI:** 10.1057/s41270-026-00518-7

**The study:** Researchers worked with an industrial equipment maker that sells configure-to-order machinery, pulling real records from over 400 sales cases in the firm's CRM and Configure-Price-Quote systems. They trained a gradient-boosted decision tree (XGBoost) to predict win/loss outcomes and used SHAP values to explain each prediction, then pitted the model against experienced salespeople judging the same cases. The model hit 83.9% balanced accuracy on the full dataset and 74.1% on a regional subset, versus 63.0% and 54.4% for the human experts — the latter barely better than a coin flip. Five sales experts then reviewed the SHAP explanations and found them understandable and actionable, though some of the model's most predictive variables struck them as counterintuitive or unimportant.

**Why it matters:** Sales forecasting and opportunity prioritization still lean heavily on rep intuition, and this suggests that intuition can be substantially worse than a model trained on the firm's own CRM and quoting data.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Before buying an AI sales-forecasting tool, run the cheaper test this paper models: score a few hundred historical closed deals with your own CRM data and benchmark the model against your reps' retrospective judgments. The explainability piece matters as much as the accuracy — the study's experts accepted the model partly because they could see which deal features drove each prediction, and the features they dismissed as irrelevant are exactly where a pipeline review might be leaving money on the table.
- **For general readers and consumers:** When a salesperson tells you they can 'read' a deal, this is a reminder that expert gut feel in complex commercial negotiations is often near-random — and that the patterns actually predicting outcomes may be ones no one in the room would guess.

**Caveats:** A single industrial firm in one product category (configure-to-order equipment), so results may not generalize; 400-plus cases is a modest dataset for machine learning, and the expert-evaluation component involved only five salespeople. The company was an industry partner in the research.

### 9. Meta-analysis: online product pages persuade along two separate tracks, and emotion doesn't work the way marketers assume

**Journal:** *Journal of Global Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-29
**Work ID:** W7204660594 | **DOI:** 10.1080/08911762.2026.2720673

**The study:** Researchers pooled two decades of fragmented studies on online product presentation — images, video, zoom, interactive and immersive displays — and combined them using meta-analysis and meta-analytic structural equation modeling. The synthesis found that cognitive responses and consumer involvement push purchase intention directly, while emotional responses only work indirectly, fully routed through attitude toward the product or brand. Effect sizes also varied systematically by region, gender, age and product type.

**Why it matters:** It suggests product-page design has two distinct jobs — feeding deliberation and building affect — and that emotional appeal pays off only if it shifts how shoppers feel about the brand, not by prodding them straight to checkout.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Treat the product page as a persuasion system with parallel paths rather than a single conversion lever: test information-rich elements (specs, comparisons, interactivity) against affect-building elements separately, and measure brand or product attitude as an intermediate metric rather than judging emotional creative purely on immediate conversion. The moderation results argue for localizing presentation formats by market, category and audience age rather than rolling out one global template.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The slick, emotive product video may not be what makes you click buy — evidence suggests it mostly changes how you feel about the item, and the click comes from the detail and hands-on interactivity that let you reason your way there.

**Caveats:** A synthesis of prior studies, so it inherits their reliance on stated purchase intention rather than observed purchases, and much of the underlying literature uses lab or online-panel designs. Meta-analytic structural models show associations across heterogeneous studies, not causal effects in any single retail setting; moderator breakdowns by region, gender and age depend on how the original studies were distributed.

### 10. When suppliers play manipulative games, tighter contracts make distributors behave worse, not better

**Journal:** *International Journal of Emerging Markets* | **Published:** 2026-09-04
**Work ID:** W7208824278 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ijoem-04-2026-1067

**The study:** A survey of 327 distributors in China measured how manipulative, Machiavellian behavior by manufacturers related to the distributors' own opportunistic conduct, using hierarchical regression to test moderators. Perceived manufacturer manipulation was strongly associated with more distributor opportunism. Crucially, deeply embedded relationships dampened the effect, whereas more complete, detailed contracts amplified it — as did market uncertainty and intense competition.

**Why it matters:** It challenges the reflex response to a fraying channel relationship, which is to write a tighter contract: if the partner already reads you as manipulative, more formal control may read as more evidence of bad faith.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit whether your channel-governance escalation path is actually making things worse — the study implies that contract tightening works only when trust is intact, and that relationship investment is the better lever once a partner suspects manipulation. Watch especially for markets in turbulence or under heavy competitive pressure, where the study found the manipulation-to-opportunism link strongest.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The finding echoes something familiar outside business: when people sense they're being handled, adding rules and fine print tends to fuel resistance rather than compliance.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey, so causal direction cannot be established — opportunistic distributors may also perceive their suppliers as more manipulative. All measures are distributor perceptions of their own and their partner's conduct, self-reported and vulnerable to bias, and the sample covers distributors in a single country (China).

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Four studies in this batch independently show that the standard corporate reflex — tighten control when a commercial relationship is under strain — makes outcomes worse. Micromanaging reps' daily activity drains job meaningfulness and raises quit intentions while outcome and capability coaching do the opposite ("Micromanaging how salespeople spend their day makes the job feel meaningless," W7211996947); writing more complete, detailed contracts amplifies rather than curbs distributor opportunism once a partner reads the supplier as manipulative ("When suppliers play manipulative games, tighter contracts make distributors behave worse," W7208824278); transactional deal-pushing measurably erodes buyer trust in matched buyer–supplier pairs ("Hard-closing salespeople damage buyer trust," W7172448511); and highly specific nudges that lift receptivity early in a call cut it by 81.40% when deployed late ("What Salespeople Say — and When They Say It — Decides Who Converts," W7203901346). Alongside them, "Procurement managers admit they keep buying from suppliers they suspect of greenwashing" (W7213538226) shows the mirror image: formal verification machinery that nobody is willing to trigger because the relationship costs too much.

**Why pitch this now:** Sales and procurement organizations are being handed more monitoring capability than ever — call recording, CRM activity dashboards, AI conversation scoring, ESG supplier-audit platforms — at exactly the moment revenue pressure makes leaders want to squeeze harder, and this evidence says the squeeze is where the leakage starts.

**Angle:** A reported feature for a management or CMO audience on the limits of control in commercial relationships: when does a contract clause, an activity quota, or a closing push stop buying compliance and start buying resentment — and what the alternative levers (coaching, account-team continuity, relational investment, evidence-based escalation triggers) actually look like in practice.

**Potential headlines:**
- Four new studies find that tightening control over sellers, buyers and channel partners backfires
- The contract clause that makes your distributor cheat you
- What your sales dashboard is quietly costing you in trust and turnover

**Potential outlets:**
- *Harvard Business Review* — the control-versus-trust tension in contracts and sales management is squarely its governance-and-leadership territory, and the channel study gives it a counterintuitive core finding to build on.
- *MIT Sloan Management Review* — strong fit for a piece weighing formal control mechanisms against relational governance, with the procurement/greenwashing material adding a supply-chain dimension.
- *Marketing Week* — B2B sales effectiveness and the incentive structures behind it are recurring beats, and the buyer–supplier trust data speaks directly to its commercial-effectiveness readership.
- *Fast Company* — the activity-monitoring findings slot into its ongoing coverage of workplace measurement and surveillance, with a fresh angle: the monitoring is hurting the numbers it was installed to protect.
