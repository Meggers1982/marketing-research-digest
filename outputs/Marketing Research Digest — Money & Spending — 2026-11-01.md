### 1. "It's customary to leave a tip" beats appeals to reciprocity — and doesn't scare customers away

**Journal:** *Marketing Science* | **Published:** 2026-10-30
**Work ID:** W7224889302 | **DOI:** 10.1287/mksc.2025.0392

**The study:** Researchers ran what they describe as the first field experiments on tip request messages inside a global freelance marketplace, randomly varying three appeals: an injunctive norm ("It's customary to leave a tip for the seller's service"), personalized reciprocity, and impersonal reciprocity. The norms message produced the largest increase in tipping. Critically, it did so without depressing customers' returning demand on the platform.

**Why it matters:** Platforms have worried that nudging customers to tip will annoy them into buying less; this evidence suggests the right framing raises worker compensation without a demand penalty.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Test the wording, not just the presence, of a tip prompt: norm-based language ("it's customary") outperformed reciprocity framing here, and the check-out prompt is one of the cheapest levers a platform has. Measure downstream repeat purchase, not just tip rate, before rolling it out.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Tipping prompts work on us largely because they signal what other people do — a reminder that "customary" is a powerful word, and worth noticing the next time a screen swivels toward you.

**Caveats:** Single platform (a global freelance marketplace) and a single service category; effects on tipping norms over longer horizons are not established.

### 2. Charging a penny for the second item beats giving it away free

**Journal:** *European Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-26
**Work ID:** W7223666241 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ejm-02-2023-0121

**The study:** Across four experiments — two using imaginary purchase scenarios and two incentive-compatible — researchers compared "buy one, get one free" with token-price offers such as "buy one, get the second for only 1¢." Token-price promotions were consistently rated more attractive than freebies, whether the bonus item was identical to the focal item or different. But there was a long-run cost: when the two items were identical, token-price offers made consumers discount the value of the focal item more than freebies did; with nonidentical items the pattern reversed.

**Why it matters:** A penny is economically trivial but psychologically meaningful, and the same tweak that lifts short-term appeal can quietly erode what shoppers think the main product is worth.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you run BOGO mechanics, consider A/B testing a token price against free — but pair short-term redemption with a longer read on reference-price erosion, and note the finding that the bonus item being identical versus complementary flips the long-run effect.
- **For general readers and consumers:** "Free" isn't always the most tempting word in retail. Shoppers often respond more to a token price, even though paying a cent makes them objectively worse off — and it may reset what they believe the product is really worth.

**Caveats:** Two of the four studies used hypothetical scenarios; experiments are lab/online rather than real retail; country and category scope not specified in the abstract; no test of how the size of the token price changes the effect.

### 3. Advice from friends pushes people into crypto; advice from professionals pulls them out

**Journal:** *International Journal of Bank Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-15
**Work ID:** W7221884200 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ijbm-02-2026-0130

**The study:** Using pooled cross-sectional data from 4,425 US respondents in the 2021 and 2024 National Financial Capability Study Investor Survey, researchers modeled how advice sources relate to cryptocurrency ownership. Personal advice from family and friends was positively associated with holding crypto; professional financial advice was negatively associated — a pattern robust across different household decision structures. Objectively measured financial and investment literacy weakened the pull of friends' advice, while people who merely believed themselves investment-savvy were less swayed by professional guidance.

**Why it matters:** It maps who actually moves retail investors into high-risk digital assets, and shows that self-assessed expertise — not real knowledge — is what makes clients tune out their advisers.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For banks, brokerages and advisory brands: the competition for attention on speculative assets is peer networks, not rival firms, and clients with high subjective confidence are the hardest to reach. Worth testing communications designed for self-described experts, who discount professional advice most.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If you got into crypto because a friend or relative told you to, you're in a statistically large group — and real financial knowledge, rather than feeling knowledgeable, is what helps people resist that social pull.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey data, so associations cannot establish causation (people may seek advice that matches decisions already made); US only; self-reported ownership and advice sources.

### 4. Malls are becoming schedules, not maps — and the order of activities changes what shoppers buy

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing* | **Published:** 2026-10-01
**Work ID:** W7221500893 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretai.2026.09.003

**The study:** Researchers extend store-choice theory to account for experiential, non-shopping activities in malls and city centers, arguing that such activities are discretionary, that time spent on them is a benefit rather than only a cost, and that consumers prefer particular, asymmetric orders of activities. An experiment in which participants scheduled a visit to their own city center supported the role of experiential activities in shaping which stores get visited, and follow-up simulations tested how store choices would shift if specific shopping or entertainment anchors were added.

**Why it matters:** It gives center managers a way to think about tenant mix and placement in terms of itineraries and sequencing rather than footfall alone — a cinema or restaurant doesn't just add traffic, it reorders the trip.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Ask what your center's typical itinerary looks like, and where in the sequence your store sits: adding or relocating an entertainment anchor may redistribute store visits in ways a simple gravity model would miss. Simulation results are directional — worth validating against your own footfall and transaction data.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A trip to the mall or high street is increasingly planned like a day out, with coffee, a film or a meal slotted in a preferred order — and that order quietly determines which shops you end up walking into.

**Caveats:** Hypothetical scheduling exercise rather than observed behavior; single study area/city center; sample size and composition not reported in the abstract; store-choice shifts are simulated.

### 5. The cybersecurity paradox: for small, low-tech firms, protection spending can cancel out digital investment

**Journal:** *Journal of Business Research* | **Published:** 2026-10-06
**Work ID:** W7220422914 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jbusres.2026.116584

**The study:** Analyzing Bank of Italy firm data from 2016 to 2023, researchers examined whether digital technology investment raises export sales in low-tech firms, and how cybersecurity spending conditions that return. Digital investment was positively associated with export sales where cybersecurity spending was absent, but the association turned negative in the highest protective-spending brackets, with the reversal located at the top of the distribution. The pattern was clearest among SMEs and manufacturers; among larger firms, digital investment showed no association with exports while protective spending was linked to higher export volume.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that for budget-constrained firms, security and digital growth compete for the same scarce money rather than reinforcing each other — a tradeoff rarely acknowledged in digital transformation pitches.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Before signing off on parallel digital and cybersecurity programs in a resource-tight business unit, ask whether the two budgets are genuinely additive or drawing from the same pot; the data hint that heavy protective spending in small firms coincides with digital investment failing to translate into export growth.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Limited direct relevance for consumers, beyond context on why many smaller exporters struggle to turn technology spending into growth.

**Caveats:** Observational firm-level data from a single country (Italy) over 2016–2023; associations, not causal effects; data source is Bank of Italy survey data and firms self-report investment categories.

### 6. When the app recommends you: what algorithmic visibility actually does for a local restaurant

**Journal:** *Information Systems Research* | **Published:** 2026-10-23
**Work ID:** W7223381933 | **DOI:** 10.1287/isre.2025.1970

**The study:** Researchers analyzed 16,720 restaurants around the official launch of a leading mobile location-based recommendation system, using the launch as quasi-experimental variation and a two-stage least squares instrumental-variable design to separate algorithmic exposure from underlying popularity. A 1% increase in exposure was associated with a 0.078% increase in clicks, and a 1% increase in clicks with a 0.053% increase in visit intentions. Exposure helped geographically disadvantaged restaurants most—but only at the discovery stage; conversion to an actual intended visit was stronger when a listing carried richer information and reputation signals such as reviews, owner-posted photos and menus. Credit-card sales data from a restricted Seoul subsample pointed to a positive link between exposure and real revenue.

**Why it matters:** It quantifies the split between being seen and being chosen: algorithms can hand a small business traffic, but the business still has to close the sale with content and social proof it controls.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For brands and local operators dependent on platform discovery, the actionable implication is that paid or algorithmic visibility is only the first half of the funnel—audit the completeness of listing assets (photos, menus, review volume) before buying more exposure, and treat conversion-stage content as the variable you can actually control. Worth testing whether the same discovery-versus-conversion split shows up in your own category and platform.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The finding is a window into how recommendation apps reshape where people eat: being off the beaten path hurts less than it used to, but diners still hesitate to act on a nudge from an algorithm unless the listing gives them enough to go on—pictures, a menu, other people's reviews.

**Caveats:** Single platform and largely a single country (Korea, with the revenue evidence limited to a restricted Seoul credit-card subsample) and a single industry; the main outcome is clicks and stated visit intentions rather than confirmed visits; instrumental-variable estimates rest on modeling assumptions rather than a randomized design.

### 7. 1.7 million government contracts show who really wins when procurement is decentralized

**Journal:** *Journal of Public Affairs* | **Published:** 2026-10-16
**Work ID:** W7222602776 | **DOI:** 10.1002/pa.70169

**The study:** Using administrative records of more than 1.7 million public procurement transactions in Peru from 2015 to 2025, researchers tracked supplier concentration at the subnational level with several measures at once—Herfindahl–Hirschman Index, Gini coefficient, entropy and concentration ratios—looking at both contract value and contract frequency. Overall the market became less concentrated over the decade, but the pattern breaks down by contract size: for large contracts, the deconcentration trend vanishes once local market conditions are accounted for. The single strongest predictor of concentration was simply how many active suppliers a locality had, and big suppliers rarely survived a change of government term, hinting at political influence over high-value awards.

**Why it matters:** Public procurement is an enormous, often overlooked customer segment, and the data show that winning small contracts and winning large ones are governed by very different dynamics—market thinness for the former, political and institutional churn for the latter.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For B2B and public-sector sales leaders, the takeaway to examine is that incumbency in large government contracts is unusually fragile across electoral cycles—pipeline models built on renewal assumptions may be mispriced, and the real growth opportunity may lie in thin local markets with few active suppliers.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Taxpayers get a concrete picture of how decentralized spending works in practice: more suppliers in a locality means more genuine competition, and the biggest contracts are the ones most likely to change hands when the government does.

**Caveats:** Single country (Peru) and a single procurement system; descriptive and correlational longitudinal analysis, so political influence over large contracts is inferred from turnover patterns rather than demonstrated causally; findings may not generalize to high-institutional-capacity settings.

### 8. The lifetime price tag of smoking, and what quitting at 25 versus 65 is worth

**Journal:** *Tobacco Control* | **Published:** 2026-10-20
**Work ID:** W7223008621 | **DOI:** 10.1136/tc-2026-060216

**The study:** Health economists built a hybrid decision tree–Markov model to estimate the lifetime societal cost of smoking in Thailand, covering direct healthcare and indirect costs across four smoking-attributable diseases: COPD, lung cancer, ischemic heart disease and stroke. Lifetime excess costs came to roughly THB 0.93 million for men and THB 0.96 million for women (about US$26,000–27,000), with life expectancy cut by 9.54 years for men and 8.18 years for women. Savings from quitting fell sharply with age: men quitting at 25 saved about THB 0.58 million versus THB 0.04 million at 65; for women the figures were THB 0.67 million and THB 0.19 million.

**Why it matters:** It puts a defensible per-person number on the economic value of early cessation, which is exactly the currency public health campaigns and finance ministries argue in when setting tobacco taxes and funding quit programs.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For social marketers and health communicators, the steep age gradient suggests the persuasion budget belongs with the youngest smokers, where the economic return per convert is roughly ten to fifteen times higher—and gives campaign messaging a concrete monetary figure rather than an abstract health warning.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For individual readers the arithmetic is blunt: quitting in your twenties is worth more than half a million baht in avoided lifetime costs, and the financial case erodes decade by decade even though the health case never fully disappears.

**Caveats:** A simulation model, not observed behavior or spending; estimates depend on assumptions about disease incidence, costs and discounting; covers four diseases only, so total costs are likely understated; single country (Thailand) with cost structures that will not transfer directly elsewhere.

### 9. People are falling for AI companions — but they aren't letting them do the shopping

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing Analytics* | **Published:** 2026-10-24
**Work ID:** W7223447670 | **DOI:** 10.1057/s41270-026-00556-1

**The study:** Researchers tested whether "relational" AI (companion and romantic chatbots, including the "AI boyfriend" trend) is converging with agentic AI commerce, using three datasets: Google Trends search attention across six markets from 2020 to 2026; 44 AI-companion apps on Google Play; and 181,226 Reddit posts from relational-AI communities. Attention to both relational AI and AI commerce rose sharply and moved together, and the companion-app category showed heavy downloads and universal in-app purchasing — with romantic positioning carrying no adoption penalty. But no app marketed shopping as a core feature, and while about 9.1% of Reddit posts touched on commerce, only 0.3% showed the companion actually involved in a purchase decision.

**Why it matters:** Consumers are already paying real money inside emotionally intimate AI products, yet the obvious commercial next step — letting the companion recommend or buy things — remains almost entirely unbuilt, leaving an open and unclaimed channel.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** The paper names an "attention-market-integration gap": hype and revenue are running ahead of functionality. Brands should watch whether companion apps start bolting on commerce, and treat the 0.3% figure as a baseline to track rather than proof that users would welcome a partner-bot that sells to them — the trust backlash risk is untested.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For readers, it's a snapshot of a strange new spending category: millions of people are paying subscriptions and in-app fees to talk to an artificial partner, but they still draw a line at taking its shopping advice. The intimacy is monetized; the recommendations, so far, are not.

**Caveats:** Observational platform data, not an experiment — no causal claims. Confined to Google Play (no iOS), English-language Reddit communities, and six markets; Reddit users are not representative of companion-app users generally; Reddit commercial discourse was estimated through design-weighted coding rather than full manual review.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- Other people's apparent behavior is the strongest spending lever in both digests. The norms message in "'It's customary to leave a tip' beats appeals to reciprocity" (W7224889302) and the peer-advice effect in "Advice from friends pushes people into crypto; advice from professionals pulls them out" (W7221884200) sit directly alongside the previous run's "Two different forces drive impulse buying in livestream auctions" (W7214649704), where envy of a winning bidder produced purchase urge on its own, and the social-norms component of "Young adults will take a mortgage but won't borrow from mum" (W7214655781).
- Felt expertise beats real expertise as a behavioral driver. The crypto advice study (W7221884200) finds that objectively measured literacy weakened the pull of friends while *subjective* investment confidence weakened the pull of professionals — the same comfort-versus-accuracy split flagged in "When AI gives you financial advice, explaining yourself works on feelings, not logic" (W7204478707) and consistent with the previous digest's finding that debt literacy reduced, rather than increased, receptivity to buffering credit (W7214655781).

**Advances**
- Experiential retail gets a mechanism at the trip level. "Malls are becoming schedules, not maps" (W7221500893) advances the thread opened by "When Tech in Stores Pays Off" (W7215084470) and "A pop-up store on tour cut the band's own online merch sales" (noted in prior continuity): where those showed physical and immersive experience is not reliably additive, the new work argues the determinant is sequence and itinerary — an entertainment anchor reorders the visit rather than simply adding footfall.
- Discovery versus conversion is now quantified. "When the app recommends you" (W7223381933) extends the pre-loaded-demand thread from "Those 'impulse' Shein hauls are actually carefully planned" (W7213342854) and the saved-ad finding in the prior run: exposure moves clicks, but conversion depends on assets the business controls — photos, menus, review volume. The earlier studies said discovery wasn't the bottleneck; this one separates the two stages statistically.

**Counterpoints**
- Parasocial attachment does not automatically become purchasing power. The previous digest showed attachment to a livestream host converting into impulse buying (W7214649704); "People are falling for AI companions — but they aren't letting them do the shopping" (W7223447670) finds deep emotional engagement and universal in-app spending with commerce influence at just 0.3% of relevant Reddit posts. Intimacy with a machine is monetizable as a subscription but, so far, not as a recommendation channel.
- "Free" loses to a penny. "Charging a penny for the second item beats giving it away free" (W7223666241) cuts against the affect-and-reassurance story that dominated the last run: here a purely numerical framing tweak outperforms, and the cost shows up later as reference-price erosion rather than in how the offer feels.

**New themes**
- Institutional and public money enters a topic that had been almost entirely consumer-facing: "1.7 million government contracts show who really wins when procurement is decentralized" (W7222602776) and "The cybersecurity paradox" (W7220422914) treat spending as a budget-allocation and market-structure problem rather than a psychology one.
- Health economics as spending evidence: "The lifetime price tag of smoking" (W7223008621) is the first per-person lifetime-cost modeling in this topic, with no counterpart in the previous digest.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Across this batch, machines and professionals are good at putting options in front of people and bad at closing them; other people close them. Algorithmic exposure lifted clicks for restaurants but converted only where human signals — reviews, owner photos, menus — were present ("When the app recommends you," W7223381933); friends moved people into crypto while professional advisers moved them out ("Advice from friends pushes people into crypto," W7221884200); a tip prompt worked best when it cited what other customers customarily do ("'It's customary to leave a tip,'" W7224889302); and users paying real money for AI companions almost never let them touch a purchase decision ("People are falling for AI companions," W7223447670).

**Why pitch this now:** Platforms and retailers are spending heavily on agentic AI commerce and recommendation surfaces on the assumption that an assistant people like will become an assistant people buy through — and this evidence says the trust needed for discovery is not the trust needed for conversion.

**Angle:** A reported feature on the trust hierarchy of spending — peers at the top, algorithms useful only as a shop window, professionals distrusted most by the people who think they know best, and AI companions monetized for intimacy but shut out of the cart. Written for platform, retail and financial-services strategists, with a consumer cut on why your friend's advice outranks your adviser's.

**Potential headlines:**
- Algorithms can get a business discovered, but reviews and photos still close the sale
- People will pay to date a chatbot and still won't let it pick dinner
- Your next expensive money mistake will probably be recommended by someone you know

**Potential outlets:**
- **Digiday** — tracking agentic commerce build-out closely; the discovery-versus-conversion split and the unbuilt companion-commerce channel are a direct challenge to the current pitch deck.
- **Marketing Brew** — strong on platform discovery mechanics for brands and local operators; the listing-assets-before-more-exposure finding is immediately usable for its readership.
- **MIT Sloan Management Review** — the trust-hierarchy framing, including advisers losing self-described experts, is the kind of strategy-level synthesis it runs rather than a single-study item.
- **Wired** — covers AI companionship and the economics around it; the gap between monetized intimacy and zero purchase influence is a reported tech-culture story with hard numbers behind it.
