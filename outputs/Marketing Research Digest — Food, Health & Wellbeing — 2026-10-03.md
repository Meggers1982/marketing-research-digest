### 1. Asking customers to rate something changes how much they like it

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Research* | **Published:** 2026-09-30
**Work ID:** W7214937598 | **DOI:** 10.1093/jcr/ucag038

**The study:** Across 16 experiments with 16,311 participants, researchers had people repeatedly rate ongoing hedonic experiences — games, foods, songs, videos, artwork — or rate them less often, or not at all. Repeated rating made people more attentive to how the experience was changing moment to moment: when things were getting worse, frequent ratings accelerated boredom and disengagement; when things were improving, frequent ratings amplified enjoyment. The act of measurement, in other words, was not neutral — it shaped the experience it was supposed to record.

**Why it matters:** Marketers run constant pulse surveys, in-app check-ins and post-interaction ratings on the assumption they are passively observing satisfaction; this research suggests that frequency of measurement itself can push customers toward faster disenchantment or deeper enjoyment.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit your measurement cadence as a design variable, not just a data pipeline. The finding suggests firms could test asking for feedback less often during experiences that naturally decay (a subscription's novelty wearing off) and more often during experiences that improve with use (a skill-building app, a loyalty program that gets better over time) — and watch whether survey frequency itself moves retention.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If you want a vacation, a new album or a favorite restaurant meal to stay enjoyable, stop constantly asking yourself how much you are enjoying it — self-monitoring makes the fading sparkle more obvious. The reverse trick also works: tracking something that is slowly getting better makes the improvement feel bigger.

**Caveats:** Primarily lab and online experiments with short-duration hedonic stimuli rather than long-run real consumption relationships; the sample composition across the 16 studies is not detailed in the abstract, and the effects on commercial outcomes like churn remain to be tested in the field.

### 2. Trust, not realism, is what makes AI influencers work — and hyper-realistic avatars backfire

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Behaviour* | **Published:** 2026-09-03
**Work ID:** W7207770543 | **DOI:** 10.1002/cb.70242

**The study:** Researchers combined an expert Delphi study and consumer interviews with a cross-national survey of 3,157 people in the US, UK, Canada and Australia, modeling how trust in AI-driven virtual influencers relates to consumer well-being. Trust was the strongest predictor, explaining close to half the variance, operating both directly and through perceived authenticity, parasocial bonds and persuasion knowledge. Notably, persona realism showed an uncanny valley pattern — more human-looking was not reliably better — and cultural orientation and digital literacy moderated the effects.

**Why it matters:** Brands investing in virtual influencers often optimize for photorealism; this points instead to transparent disclosure, coherent storytelling and audience literacy as the levers that determine whether an AI persona builds or erodes the relationship.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Treat realism as a tunable risk rather than a goal. Brands could A/B test clearly stylized or openly synthetic personas against hyper-realistic ones, and test explicit AI disclosure, to see whether admitting the avatar is artificial actually raises trust rather than lowering it — and whether the answer differs by market.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Audiences are forming genuine one-sided relationships with characters they know are not real — and the study suggests people's digital literacy shapes how much that bond helps or hurts them. Knowing an influencer is synthetic does not necessarily break the spell.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey, so it cannot establish causation; self-reported well-being rather than observed behavior; four English-speaking Western countries only; heavy reliance on structural path modeling. The same author team appears to have a closely related paper in the same window, so findings overlap.

### 3. "Food noise" went viral with Ozempic — but nobody has defined what it actually is

**Journal:** *Appetite* | **Published:** 2026-09-22
**Work ID:** W7214039691 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.appet.2026.108791

**The study:** This narrative review examines "food noise," the term that spread on TikTok and Reddit to describe persistent, intrusive, unwanted preoccupation with food, which many users of GLP-1 drugs such as semaglutide and tirzepatide report being dramatically quieted. The authors find the construct remains poorly defined and barely measured, and propose five candidate theoretical explanations: homeostatic signaling, food cue reactivity, food addiction, thought suppression and memory inhibition.

**Why it matters:** A consumer-coined concept is now shaping how millions of people describe their relationship with eating and with weight-loss drugs, with direct implications for food marketing, cue-based advertising and the fast-growing GLP-1 wellness category.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Food and beverage marketers should watch this construct closely: if GLP-1 users genuinely experience reduced cue reactivity, the core mechanics of appetite-triggering advertising, in-store displays and impulse placement may simply stop working on a growing segment. The review is a prompt to start measuring cue responsiveness within your customer base rather than assuming it is constant.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The constant mental chatter about food that many people assume is a personal failure of willpower now has a name and several competing scientific explanations — and at least some of it may be biology rather than character.

**Caveats:** This is a theoretical/narrative review, not new empirical evidence; the construct lacks a validated measure, and existing evidence is largely qualitative and self-reported from social media and interview accounts.

### 4. Put the animal welfare label on the veggie burger too

**Journal:** *Food Quality and Preference* | **Published:** 2026-10-01
**Work ID:** W7216368733 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.foodqual.2026.106135

**The study:** Researchers ran two experiments in a simulated online supermarket testing what happens when animal welfare labels — normally reserved for meat, dairy and eggs — are also placed on plant-based products. Extending the labels to plant-based items increased the number of plant-based foods chosen by 28% versus an unlabeled control and 33% versus the standard setup where only animal products carried the label, while also shifting choices toward higher-welfare options within the meat and dairy aisle. The effect held even when the welfare score was embedded in a busier multidimensional label showing biodiversity and climate scores alongside it, and was explained by shoppers perceiving more animal-friendly options available and stronger social norms around choosing them.

**Why it matters:** It suggests a label's biggest job is not informing shoppers but redrawing the boundaries of the category they think they are choosing within — and that plant-based brands may be leaving an easy credibility signal on the table.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Retailers and plant-based brands could test extending existing welfare certifications across the full assortment rather than confining them to animal products; the mechanism here is perceived availability and social norms, so the test should be run in a real store environment where competing cues and prices are present, not just in a simulated basket.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Labels quietly define what counts as a "choice set." If a welfare logo only ever appears on meat, shoppers mentally file animal welfare as a meat-aisle decision — and never notice that the simplest animal-friendly option is sitting one shelf over.

**Caveats:** Simulated online supermarket rather than real purchases with real money; choices recorded in a controlled setting over a short horizon; the abstract does not report sample size, composition or country, and effects on actual sales remain untested.

### 5. Mothers rarely eat in front of their babies — and when they do, babies eat more

**Journal:** *Appetite* | **Published:** 2026-09-19
**Work ID:** W7213662492 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.appet.2026.108787

**The study:** Researchers observed mothers feeding their infants at 6 months (n=103), 9 months (n=104) and 12 months (n=101), coding whether mothers demonstrated eating the food themselves and how many bites the infant took. Mothers who demonstrated eating more often had infants who took more bites — but fewer than 30% of mothers demonstrated eating at any age point. Mothers were no more likely to model eating with unfamiliar foods than familiar ones, even though infants ate less of the unfamiliar items, and demonstrations were more common during shared family meals and with older infants.

**Why it matters:** A free, simple behavior that tracks with infant food acceptance is almost never used at precisely the moment it would help most — the introduction of new foods.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For baby food and infant nutrition brands, packaging and feeding guidance built around "take a bite yourself first," especially for new flavors and vegetables, is a low-cost claim worth testing — though this is correlational and would need a controlled trial before any efficacy messaging.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Parents agonize over getting babies to accept vegetables, but the most instinctive tool — visibly eating it yourself, at a shared meal — turns out to be the one most families skip during solo infant feedings.

**Caveats:** Observational and cross-sectional in design, so causality is unproven (more responsive infants may simply elicit more demonstrating); samples of roughly 100 at each age; single study population with limited detail on demographics; measured bites in an observation session rather than long-term diet.

### 6. Nearly 90,000 hotel reviews show where dietary needs go wrong — and it is rarely the food itself

**Journal:** *Journal of Services Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-01
**Work ID:** W7215031814 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jsm-04-2026-0384

**The study:** Researchers mined 88,986 text comment fields from Booking.com reviews of 20 four-star hotels in Bratislava and Prague, using computational text analysis to isolate food-related comments and manual validation to identify 439 that referenced dietary restrictions. Such mentions were rare overall but appeared disproportionately in negative rather than positive food commentary, and guests judged medical, lifestyle and religious dietary needs differently. Five service conditions recurred: availability, variety, staff responsiveness, labeling clarity and whether the accommodation felt adequate rather than token.

**Why it matters:** It reframes dietary accommodation as an operational and informational problem — labeling and frontline staff response — rather than purely a menu-range problem, and shows it surfaces mainly as complaint rather than praise.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Hotel and hospitality operators should audit the cheap half of the problem first: clear allergen and ingredient labeling at the buffet and trained frontline responses, since guests penalize vague information and dismissive staff as much as a thin selection. Treat silence in reviews as a weak signal, not a pass.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If you travel with celiac disease, an allergy, or religious dietary rules, the recurring failure point at hotel breakfasts is not the absence of food but the absence of reliable information about what is in it.

**Caveats:** Self-selected online reviews, which capture only what guests chose to write and may contain traces rather than direct evidence of harm; only 439 relevant comments after filtering; 20 four-star hotels in two Central European cities; no link to bookings, revenue or repeat stays.

### 7. Anger at other people's food waste predicts who actually eats their leftovers

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-04
**Work ID:** W7208792358 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jcm-07-2025-8043

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 226 students online and tested a model of leftover consumption based on the Theory of Interpersonal Behavior. Beyond the usual driver—attitude toward eating leftovers—the emotion of anger felt when witnessing other people waste food significantly strengthened intention to eat one's own leftovers. That anger was itself partly rooted in how strongly the person identified as environmentally conscious.

**Why it matters:** Social marketing campaigns against food waste typically preach guilt or thrift; this suggests moral indignation at others' waste may be a more potent lever, and one brands and public agencies rarely use.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For sustainability and food brands: worth testing creative that surfaces other-directed anger ("look what's being thrown away") against conventional guilt or money-saving appeals—but this is a survey of intentions among students, so any campaign should be validated with real behavioral tracking before scaling.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A look at the emotional mechanics of everyday household waste: the people who finish their leftovers may be driven less by frugality than by irritation at seeing others throw food out.

**Caveats:** Student sample; cross-sectional online survey measuring intentions rather than observed leftover consumption; cannot establish causation; N=226 total.

### 8. Eating the same meal for years doesn't make you like it—it makes you hack it

**Journal:** *Food Quality and Preference* | **Published:** 2026-09-06
**Work ID:** W7210275208 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.foodqual.2026.106107

**The study:** Researchers conducted a focused ethnography with 113 Brazilian Army personnel across early, mid and advanced career stages, combining contextual observation and semi-structured interviews with thematic coding and AI-assisted sentiment classification of ration experiences. Negative sensory and physiological reactions to the food stayed roughly constant across all career stages—repeated exposure did not breed liking. What changed was behavior: early-career personnel ate under supervision, mid-career personnel became more critical and discarded more, and advanced-career personnel routinely modified and recombined ration components to make meals workable.

**Why it matters:** It challenges the familiar assumption in food marketing and institutional catering that repeated exposure reliably builds acceptance; instead, long-term users may stay dissatisfied and simply develop workarounds that mask the problem.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For food manufacturers and institutional caterers: high repeat consumption is not evidence of liking. Consider measuring how heavy users modify, combine or discard your product—customization behavior may be a better dissatisfaction signal than satisfaction scores, and a product roadmap opportunity.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Anyone stuck in a repetitive food environment—school canteens, hospital trays, meal-prep routines—will recognize the pattern: you don't learn to love it, you learn to doctor it.

**Caveats:** Single country and a highly specific, hierarchical institutional setting (Brazilian Army); qualitative ethnographic design with no comparison group and no causal test; sentiment classification partly AI-assisted; findings may not generalize to voluntary consumer contexts.

### 9. Australia's smokers have quietly deserted the supermarket — and it's reshaping tobacco policy

**Journal:** *Tobacco Control* | **Published:** 2026-09-30
**Work ID:** W7214949285 | **DOI:** 10.1136/tc-2026-060347

**The study:** Researchers pooled four waves of Australia's national cross-sectional drug strategy survey (2013 to 2022/23), covering 10,940 people who smoked, and tracked where they got their tobacco. Supermarket purchasing fell from 51% to 35%, convenience stores held steady, and tobacconists surged from 17% to 33% while informal sources (friends, unregulated sellers) rose to 14%. Tobacconist buyers skewed male, lower socioeconomic status and metropolitan; informal sourcing was concentrated among 18–24 year olds and occasional smokers, who were up to 11 times more likely than daily smokers to source informally. Both tobacconist and informal channels were strongly linked to using illicitly traded tobacco.

**Why it matters:** A decade of tax rises and plain packaging has pushed a mass-market category out of mainstream retail and into specialist and gray channels, which blunts the policy levers that depend on licensed retailers. It is also a case study in what happens to any product when price pressure and regulation make the compliant channel uncompetitive.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For anyone selling a heavily regulated or heavily taxed category, this is a live example of channel migration: as the mainstream retailer's price advantage and willingness to stock erode, demand relocates to specialists and informal sellers that are harder to monitor. Worth watching as a template for how retail licensing restrictions — the policy this data is meant to inform — would reshape distribution overnight.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The data quietly documents a two-tier tobacco market: lower-income and metropolitan smokers increasingly buy from tobacconists, and young, occasional smokers increasingly get cigarettes from informal sources — both routes associated with illicit product of unknown content.

**Caveats:** Self-reported cross-sectional survey data, so purchasing source and illicit use are reported rather than observed, and the design cannot establish causation; single country; the final wave spans the COVID era, which may have affected shopping patterns.

### 10. The country that pays farmers to grow tobacco while promising to curb smoking

**Journal:** *Tobacco Control* | **Published:** 2026-09-30
**Work ID:** W7214928046 | **DOI:** 10.1136/tc-2025-059680

**The study:** Researchers combined descriptive economic analysis, correlation and interrupted time series analysis of production and subsidy data with a survey of farmers to examine North Macedonia's tobacco subsidies. Tobacco is the country's most subsidized crop, absorbing roughly 40% of all crop-related support, and subsidies materially supplement farmer income — yet cultivated area and the number of participating farmers are both falling. Most surveyed farmers said they would quit tobacco growing without the subsidy.

**Why it matters:** It documents a direct contradiction between a government's public health commitments under the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control and its agricultural spending, and shows the subsidy is the main thing keeping growers in the crop — which means a managed exit is feasible.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** A reminder for anyone navigating ESG and policy-aligned sourcing that supply chains can be propped up by state money that is politically, not economically, durable; mapping subsidy dependency in your upstream categories is a risk exercise, not an academic one.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Public money flows in both directions on smoking — taxing it at the till while funding it at the farm — and most of the farmers themselves say they would switch crops if the payments stopped.

**Caveats:** Single country; observational economic data rather than an experiment, so subsidy effects are inferred from time series rather than causally isolated; the farmer survey's size and sampling are not reported in the abstract.

### 11. Twenty days of diaries from naval cadets: making work playful cut daily exhaustion

**Journal:** *Journal of Business Research* | **Published:** 2026-09-30
**Work ID:** W7214901172 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jbusres.2026.116566

**The study:** Researchers ran a 20-day diary study with 57 Norwegian naval cadets, collecting 1,046 day-level observations and analyzing them with multilevel structural equation modeling. On days when cadets deliberately added fun to routine tasks — or turned them into a self-imposed competition — they reported more activated positive affect and less exhaustion that same day. Combining both forms of play amplified the benefit, and the effect appeared even though cadets had almost no control over what tasks they did or when.

**Why it matters:** Most wellbeing interventions assume employees need more autonomy over their workload; this suggests discretion over *how* a task is approached can deliver same-day energy gains in rigidly structured roles where job redesign isn't on the table.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For leaders of frontline, shift-based or compliance-heavy teams who can't hand out flexibility, the testable idea is to explicitly license playful framing and friendly competition within fixed tasks — and to measure daily energy and exhaustion rather than annual engagement scores.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If your job is tightly scripted, the small game you invent to get through it isn't procrastination — in this sample it tracked with feeling more energized and less drained by the end of the day.

**Caveats:** Small sample (57 participants) from a single highly distinctive setting — military cadets in one country; all measures self-reported within the same day, so same-source bias and reverse causality (feeling good makes play easier) cannot be ruled out; short time horizon.

### 12. Brand tracking for federal agencies: ICE is the most recognized and least liked

**Journal:** *Public Relations Review* | **Published:** 2026-09-26
**Work ID:** W7214439490 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.pubrev.2026.102780

**The study:** Applying the advertising world's Hierarchy of Effects model to public institutions, researchers surveyed a national MTurk sample (N=512) on unaided and aided recall, evaluations and support-related intentions for five US federal law enforcement agencies. The FBI emerged with the strongest attitudinal profile; ICE showed high awareness paired with low evaluations; and the US Marshals Service suffered from simply not being recognized. The pattern mirrors classic brand diagnostics, where awareness and favorability can move in opposite directions.

**Why it matters:** It is a reminder that salience is not equity: an organization can be extremely well known and still carry negative sentiment that recognition-building campaigns will only amplify.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** The diagnostic logic travels to any brand with a reputation problem — before spending on awareness, check whether your gap is recognition or regard, because for a high-awareness/low-favorability brand more visibility makes things worse. Treat the specific agency rankings as a snapshot of one online panel at one politically charged moment, not a stable score.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Public institutions are now measured with the same tools as consumer brands, and the results show how differently people hold agencies that do broadly similar work.

**Caveats:** MTurk online panel, which skews unrepresentative of the US public and is a poor base for national attitude estimates; cross-sectional and descriptive, no causal claims; stated support intentions rather than behavior; fielded amid highly salient news events that likely depress or inflate specific agency scores.

### 13. How street vendors defend their dignity when the market is stacked against them

**Journal:** *Journal of Public Policy & Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-23
**Work ID:** W7214083501 | **DOI:** 10.1177/07439156261494552

**The study:** The authors conducted immersive ethnographic fieldwork in a weekly village market in Odisha, India, observing how subsistence entrepreneurs operate in a marketplace stratified by caste, gender, literacy and class. They identify three strategies deployed across the stages of an exchange: curating which interactions to have beforehand, fluidly signaling identity during the encounter, and crafting self-affirming narratives afterward. The paper builds these into a "dignitarian" framework for market policy.

**Why it matters:** Most subsistence marketplace research emphasizes warm, relationship-dense exchange; this documents the opposite condition and shows that emotional self-protection is itself a core business skill for the world's poorest sellers.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For brands and development programs building distribution through micro-entrepreneurs in emerging markets, the finding is that training and tools which ignore status and dignity dynamics will miss a major determinant of whether sellers stay in the channel.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A close-up view of what commerce feels like at the bottom of the income distribution, where a sale can also be an exercise in managing other people's contempt.

**Caveats:** Qualitative ethnography in a single village market in one Indian state; findings are interpretive and not intended to generalize or quantify.

### 14. Short brand names feel warmer — and that warmth shows up in sales pitches and crowdfunding totals

**Journal:** *Psychology and Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-28
**Work ID:** W7214622498 | **DOI:** 10.1002/mar.70273

**The study:** Researchers combined four main experiments plus a follow-up with analyses of two datasets covering 7,775 real brands to test why short brand names outperform long ones. Shorter names consistently signaled greater warmth (friendliness, approachability), which in turn drove more favorable brand evaluations, stronger purchase intentions and, in the real-brand data, more money raised through crowdfunding. The effect held across industries including food and clothing, across hedonic and utilitarian products, across familiar and unknown brands, and in both Latin and non-Latin scripts such as Amharic. Notably, shorter names were not always easier to process, so the usual "processing fluency" explanation did not account for the advantage, nor did memorability or distinctiveness.

**Why it matters:** Naming is one of the few brand decisions that is essentially free to get right at the outset and expensive to change later, and this gives founders and brand teams a concrete mechanism — perceived warmth — rather than a vague preference for brevity.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If warmth is the mechanism, the naming brief changes: test short candidate names specifically on warmth perceptions, not just recall and fluency. It also suggests longer names may be salvageable by building warmth through other cues (tone of voice, mascot, founder story) — a hypothesis worth testing in-market rather than assuming the lab result transfers wholesale.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A reminder that first impressions of a company can be shaped by something as arbitrary as syllable count — a crowdfunding project may feel more likable simply because its name is shorter, before you know anything about the product.

**Caveats:** Experimental components rely on evaluations and purchase intentions rather than observed purchases; the real-brand evidence (including crowdfunding) is correlational, so other factors associated with short names may contribute.

### 15. Bright packaging makes kids nag harder — but only when parents don't know much about nutrition

**Journal:** *Young Consumers Insight and Ideas for Responsible Marketers* | **Published:** 2026-10-01
**Work ID:** W7215082714 | **DOI:** 10.1108/yc-02-2026-2995

**The study:** A Brazilian mixed-methods study paired 30 in-depth interviews with caregivers of children aged 4–11 with a survey and choice experiment among 431 parents from socioeconomic classes B/C, manipulating snack and cookie package colors (vibrant versus neutral) while holding front-of-package warning labels constant. Vibrant colors significantly increased the perceived intensity of children's pestering, which in turn raised purchase intention for ultra-processed foods; pestering accounted for roughly 29% of the color effect. Critically, parental nutrition knowledge acted as a filter: the color-to-pestering link was strong among low-knowledge parents and statistically non-significant among high-knowledge parents.

**Why it matters:** It suggests mandatory warning labels alone may not neutralize child-directed packaging design, because color operates through the child's demand rather than the parent's reading of the label — and that nutrition education changes which cue parents actually process.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For food brands and regulators alike, the finding reframes package color as a child-targeting lever that operates independently of label compliance. Brands with child-oriented portfolios should expect color-focused scrutiny next; the open question is whether this experimental pattern holds at the shelf, where price, promotion and habit also drive the basket.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Parents often blame themselves for giving in at the supermarket. This suggests the packaging is engineered to route the pressure through the child — and that knowing how to read a label actually weakens the pull of bright colors.

**Caveats:** Single country (Brazil) and a specific socioeconomic segment; purchase intention in a choice experiment rather than observed buying; pester power is parent-reported perception, not measured child behavior; cross-sectional survey component limits causal claims about the knowledge moderator.

### 16. Internal documents suggest a tobacco giant kept working the smuggling channels it promised to help shut down

**Journal:** *Tobacco Control* | **Published:** 2026-10-01
**Work ID:** W7215133135 | **DOI:** 10.1136/tc-2024-058843

**The study:** Researchers keyword-searched roughly 15,000 internal British American Tobacco documents dated 2000–2014, mostly covering East and Central Africa and the Middle East, and thematically analyzed the 210 relevant files. The documents indicate BAT remained involved in the illicit tobacco trade throughout the period, using strategies similar to those that drew legal action in the 1990s — working with distributors linked to smuggling and supplying products into poorly regulated markets. The authors also report that while the company partnered with authorities on anti-smuggling efforts, documents suggest it concealed illicit activity.

**Why it matters:** It is direct documentary evidence that a major multinational's public anti-illicit-trade positioning and its internal commercial practice can diverge, with implications for any government or NGO considering industry partnerships on enforcement.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** A case study in the reputational risk of partnership-based corporate responsibility: when a company co-administers the very problem it profits from, the partnership itself becomes the story. Brand and public affairs leaders in scrutinized categories should consider how internal records would read if released a decade later.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Illicit cigarettes are often framed as the work of criminal outsiders undercutting legitimate business; this research suggests the supply chain can run through the branded manufacturers themselves, which matters for how smoking-related tax and health policy is debated.

**Caveats:** Document analysis is interpretive and based on a partial, non-random set of internal files; covers specific regions and a period ending in 2014; findings are the authors' reading of documents, not legal findings; company has not been adjudicated on these specific claims in this paper.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- **Repeat consumption is not evidence of liking.** The previous digest's "Free vegetable-rich school lunches changed nothing — because the kids didn't eat them" (W7202165920) showed supply solved and consumption unsolved; the new "Eating the same meal for years doesn't make you like it — it makes you hack it" (W7210275208) extends the same lesson to long-run captive users, where negative sensory reactions held flat across an entire career and only workaround behavior changed.
- **Labels redraw category boundaries rather than inform.** "Consumers file 'cell-based sausage' by how it was made, not by what it is" (W7211919306) found explicit process labeling carves out a separate mental category; "Put the animal welfare label on the veggie burger too" (W7216368733) shows the same mechanism running in the brand-friendly direction, with an extended welfare label shifting plant-based choices up 28% by changing perceived availability and norms.
- **GLP-1 users as a distinct, under-measured segment.** "People on GLP-1 drugs show a gut-level preference for healthy food — and don't know it" (W7213351764) and the new "'Food noise' went viral with Ozempic — but nobody has defined what it actually is" (W7214039691) converge on the same uncomfortable point: the shift is happening below the level of self-report, and the field still lacks a validated measure of it.
- **Cheap design cues keep outperforming expensive messaging.** Plate geometry (W7212607085) and nostalgic packaging (W7204661572) are joined by "Short brand names feel warmer" (W7214622498) and "Bright packaging makes kids nag harder" (W7215082714) — in both new cases, with a named mechanism (warmth, pester power) rather than a vague fluency story.

**Advances**
- **From AI harms to AI trust mechanics.** The previous run mapped wellbeing damage from synthetic and augmented media — beauty filters and surgical intent (W7171940513), AI companions and power imbalance (W7170143709), AI doctors and evidence format (W7212302631). "Trust, not realism, is what makes AI influencers work" (W7207770543) advances this by identifying the lever brands actually control, and by reporting an uncanny-valley pattern where more photorealism is not reliably better.
- **Food waste moves from design to emotion.** "Nostalgic packaging nudges people to waste less food" (W7204661572) worked through perceived effort; "Anger at other people's food waste predicts who actually eats their leftovers" (W7208792358) adds a social-emotional route that conventional guilt-and-thrift campaigns ignore.
- **Consumer vulnerability widens.** The rural algorithmic exclusion work (W7170190396) and gender-invariant financial anxiety study (W7203676845) now sit alongside "How street vendors defend their dignity when the market is stacked against them" (W7214083501), pushing the vulnerability frame from buyers to subsistence sellers.

**Counterpoints**
- **Knowledge sometimes does matter.** The previous digest's strongest thread was the failure of the information-deficit model (W7202165920, W7213326632, W7206215832). "Bright packaging makes kids nag harder — but only when parents don't know much about nutrition" (W7215082714) is a partial corrective: nutrition knowledge did not change what the child wanted, but it neutralized the color effect on the person holding the wallet.
- **A methods challenge to almost everything above.** "Asking customers to rate something changes how much they like it" (W7214937598) undercuts the stated-preference and repeated-rating designs that carried much of the previous batch, including the intention-based promotion (W7213550700) and waste (W7204661572) studies.

**New themes**
- **Regulated categories and industry conduct.** Three tobacco studies with no precedent in the previous digest: channel migration out of supermarkets in Australia (W7214949285), subsidy dependency in North Macedonia (W7214928046), and internal BAT documents on illicit trade (W7215133135).
- **Service operations and workplace wellbeing.** "Nearly 90,000 hotel reviews show where dietary needs go wrong" (W7215031814) and "Twenty days of diaries from naval cadets" (W7214901172) open hospitality operations and employee energy as new territory for this topic.
- **Brand tools applied to public institutions.** "Brand tracking for federal agencies" (W7214439490) imports hierarchy-of-effects diagnostics into government reputation, a move the previous digest did not touch.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Four studies in this batch, from four different fields, all land on the same conclusion: the standard signals brands read as satisfaction aren't measuring satisfaction at all. "Asking customers to rate something changes how much they like it" (W7214937598) shows the measurement itself bends the experience; "Eating the same meal for years doesn't make you like it — it makes you hack it" (W7210275208) shows heavy repeat use masking persistent dislike, visible only in how users modify the product; "Nearly 90,000 hotel reviews show where dietary needs go wrong" (W7215031814) finds a whole class of guest need surfacing almost exclusively as complaint, never as praise; and "Brand tracking for federal agencies" (W7214439490) demonstrates awareness and regard moving in opposite directions. Taken together: ratings, repeat purchase, review silence and recall are each being read as evidence of something they do not reliably indicate.

**Why pitch this now:** Feedback prompts have become ambient — every app, delivery, ride and checkout asks — while CX and brand-tracking budgets are under hard scrutiny, and platform review scores now drive ranking and discovery in travel and retail; a measurement reckoning is overdue and the evidence base has just thickened.

**Angle:** A reported feature on the limits of the customer-feedback industrial complex — what each of the standard proxies actually measures, what the alternative diagnostics look like (customization behavior, complaint composition, favorability versus salience), and why the volume of feedback collection may itself be a variable. Aimed at CMOs, CX and insight leaders, with enough everyday texture — the ration hack, the hotel breakfast buffet — to carry a wider business readership.

**Potential headlines:**
- Four new studies say the customer-feedback numbers aren't measuring what firms think
- The more often you ask people how they're enjoying it, the faster they stop
- Your happiest-looking customers may just be the ones who gave up telling you

**Potential outlets:**
- **Harvard Business Review** — it published the article that made a single loyalty metric a boardroom standard, and would run a serious re-examination of what satisfaction proxies can and can't carry.
- **MIT Sloan Management Review** — fits its taste for evidence-led pieces on measurement design, including the argument that survey cadence is itself an intervention.
- **Marketing Week** — its readership defends brand-tracking and CX budgets on exactly the awareness-and-score metrics the agency-brand study complicates.
- **Skift** — the hotel-review strand is directly operational for travel brands, where guest scores feed distribution and dietary accommodation is a live service-design issue.

## Story Ideas by Study

- **Asking customers to rate something changes how much they like it (Work ID W7214937598):** Survey frequency as a design variable — asking more often accelerates boredom in decaying experiences and amplifies enjoyment in improving ones — for a CMO-audience column or a consumer-psychology explainer in a general-interest outlet.
- **Trust, not realism, is what makes AI influencers work (Work ID W7207770543):** Brands are spending on photorealism when disclosure and coherent storytelling are the trust levers, and hyper-realism can backfire, for a digital-marketing or influencer-economy trade feature.
- **"Food noise" went viral with Ozempic — but nobody has defined what it actually is (Work ID W7214039691):** A TikTok-coined construct is now steering how millions describe eating and how food marketers think about cue-based advertising — with no validated measure behind it, for a health or food-business feature, or a general-interest science explainer.
- **Put the animal welfare label on the veggie burger too (Work ID W7216368733):** Plant-based brands are leaving a free credibility signal unused, and welfare certification bodies face a scope question, for a grocery or food-policy trade explainer.
- **Mothers rarely eat in front of their babies — and when they do, babies eat more (Work ID W7213662492):** Fewer than 30% of mothers modeled eating, and almost never with the unfamiliar foods where it would matter most, for a parenting or family-health section, with an infant-nutrition brand angle for a food trade brief.
- **Nearly 90,000 hotel reviews show where dietary needs go wrong (Work ID W7215031814):** Dietary accommodation fails on labeling clarity and staff response more than menu range, and shows up only as complaint — so quiet scores are not a pass, for a hospitality or travel trade explainer.
- **Anger at other people's food waste predicts who actually eats their leftovers (Work ID W7208792358):** Moral indignation at others may beat personal guilt or thrift in waste campaigns — an untested creative route for a sustainability-marketing or social-marketing column (flag the student sample).
- **Eating the same meal for years doesn't make you like it — it makes you hack it (Work ID W7210275208):** Customization and discard behavior as a better dissatisfaction signal than satisfaction scores, for a food-manufacturing or institutional-catering trade feature.
- **Australia's smokers have quietly deserted the supermarket (Work ID W7214949285):** Supermarket share of tobacco fell from 51% to 35% while tobacconists doubled and informal sourcing concentrated among 18–24s — a channel-migration case study for any taxed category, for a retail trade analysis or a business news feature.
- **The country that pays farmers to grow tobacco while promising to curb smoking (Work ID W7214928046):** Roughly 40% of crop support going to tobacco while most farmers say they'd quit without it — a policy contradiction with a feasible exit, for an international business or global-health news feature, plus an ESG-sourcing brief for a corporate-affairs outlet.
- **Twenty days of diaries from naval cadets (Work ID W7214901172):** Wellbeing gains without autonomy — licensing playful framing inside rigid tasks cut same-day exhaustion — for an HR or workplace-culture column aimed at frontline and shift-based employers.
- **Brand tracking for federal agencies: ICE is the most recognized and least liked (Work ID W7214439490):** Salience is not equity, and for high-awareness/low-favorability institutions more visibility makes things worse — for a PR and public-affairs trade piece, handled as a single-panel snapshot rather than a national score.
- **How street vendors defend their dignity when the market is stacked against them (Work ID W7214083501):** Emotional self-protection as a core business skill for the world's poorest sellers, with implications for brands building micro-entrepreneur distribution, for a development-business feature or a long-read consumer-culture section.
- **Short brand names feel warmer (Work ID W7214622498):** Naming briefs should test warmth rather than recall and fluency, with real crowdfunding money attached to the effect across 7,775 brands, for a branding trade feature or a startup and founder-audience column.
- **Bright packaging makes kids nag harder — but only when parents don't know much about nutrition (Work ID W7215082714):** Package color routes commercial pressure through the child and operates independently of mandatory warning labels — the next regulatory frontier after front-of-pack, for a food-policy trade explainer or a parenting and consumer-affairs section.
- **Internal documents suggest a tobacco giant kept working the smuggling channels it promised to help shut down (Work ID W7215133135):** The reputational hazard of partnership-based corporate responsibility when a company co-administers the problem it profits from, for an investigative business or corporate-accountability outlet, with a corporate-affairs angle for a PR trade column.
