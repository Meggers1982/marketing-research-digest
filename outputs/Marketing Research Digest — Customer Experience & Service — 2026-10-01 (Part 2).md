### 1. Making AI avatars look human matters less than making them useful

**Journal:** *Asia Pacific Journal of Marketing and Logistics* | **Published:** 2026-08-28
**Work ID:** W7204579696 | **DOI:** 10.1108/apjml-04-2026-0802

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 607 consumers who had interacted with AI-generated "digital humans" (virtual avatars used in online sales and service), then analyzed the data with both structural equation modeling and fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis. Intelligent service capability and social influence were positively linked to both solo and shared (socially shared, co-viewed) customer experience, while sensory anthropomorphism — how lifelike the avatar looks and sounds — was linked only to solo experience. In the configurational analysis, capability and social influence emerged as core conditions for a strong shared experience, with lifelike appearance only a peripheral one.

**Why it matters:** Brands pouring budget into photorealistic AI presenters may be investing in the least consequential attribute; competence and social proof appear to do the heavier lifting, especially when experiences are shared or talked about.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit where your digital-human budget goes: this survey evidence suggests testing a functionally smarter, less visually polished avatar against a hyper-realistic one, and measuring not just individual satisfaction but whether the experience is worth sharing.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For shoppers, it's a reminder that the uncanny polish of a virtual assistant isn't what makes the encounter good — being actually helped is. Lifelike design can look like value without delivering it.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional self-report survey, so no causal claims; single questionnaire sample of consumers with prior AI digital-human experience (likely a single national market, Asia-Pacific); outcomes are perceptions of experience rather than observed purchases.

### 2. When an AI agent does your shopping, trust in the bot becomes trust in the product

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing Management* | **Published:** 2026-08-17
**Work ID:** W7203638544 | **DOI:** 10.1080/0267257x.2026.2715539

**The study:** Drawing on behavioral reasoning theory and trust transfer theory, the researchers modeled consumer responses to generative AI shopping agents that search, compare and recommend on a shopper's behalf. Reasons for adoption clustered around contextual relevance, time and effort saved, usefulness of advice and informativeness; reasons against included perceived complexity, privacy concerns, irritation and attachment to traditional shopping. Crucially, purchase intention formed through trust transferring from the agent to the goods it recommended, moderated by how much the consumer generally trusts algorithms and by what kind of product was recommended.

**Why it matters:** If credibility flows from the AI agent to whatever it surfaces, the agent — not the brand — becomes the gatekeeper of consideration, and early journey stages that marketers currently buy their way into may be automated away.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Start planning for a world where your first "customer" is an agent: test how your product data, context signals and reviews read to an AI intermediary, and watch the finding that contextual relevance can substitute for invasive preference-based personalization.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The study suggests a quiet risk for shoppers: once you trust the assistant, you tend to trust its picks without re-examining them. Knowing that halo exists is the first defense against outsourcing judgment entirely.

**Caveats:** Reported as a theory-driven model of stated reasons and purchase intentions rather than observed purchasing; the abstract does not specify sample size, country or recruitment method; agents described as "upcoming," so responses are likely to anticipated rather than actual use.

### 3. Giving salespeople digital tools makes them improvise better — but only under a certain kind of boss

**Journal:** *Journal of Service Theory and Practice* | **Published:** 2026-09-26
**Work ID:** W7214497259 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jstp-01-2026-0001

**The study:** Across three studies with business-to-business frontline employees — a survey of 115, interviews with 12, and an experiment with 210 — researchers examined "bricolage," the practice of making do and improvising solutions with whatever resources are at hand. Employees more integrated with digital technologies showed more bricolage, which in turn predicted more adaptive selling and higher job performance. The digital-to-bricolage link was stronger when managers displayed intellectual stimulation, that is, prompting employees to question assumptions and rethink problems.

**Why it matters:** It suggests technology rollouts to frontline teams pay off through improvisation rather than standardization, and that the manager's coaching style is a necessary multiplier rather than a soft extra.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Pair any frontline tech deployment with manager training in questioning and reframing; the evidence here implies tools alone produce less improvisational problem-solving than tools plus an intellectually stimulating boss, which is a testable hypothesis for your next rollout.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Limited angle for general readers, beyond the familiar experience that the salesperson or service rep who solves your odd problem is usually improvising — and is more able to do so when well equipped and encouraged to think.

**Caveats:** Small samples (N=115 survey; 12 interviews; 210 in the experiment); performance is largely self-reported; B2B frontline employees only, with country and industry context unspecified; survey component is cross-sectional and cannot establish causation.

### 4. Long-standing B2B customers aren't automatically loyal ones

**Journal:** *Journal of Business and Industrial Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-11
**Work ID:** W7202159462 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jbim-12-2025-1181

**The study:** Researchers combined a qualitative study with a survey of 251 supplier–customer relationships in manufacturing, using partial least squares structural equation modeling to test how functional, social and emotional relationship value feed into satisfaction and then into two kinds of loyalty. Behavioral loyalty (repeat buying) was driven by satisfaction plus functional and emotional value, while attitudinal loyalty (advocacy and commitment) was driven by satisfaction plus functional and social value. Notably, the length of the relationship did not strengthen the link between satisfaction and either form of loyalty.

**Why it matters:** It suggests account tenure is not a loyalty moat in price-sensitive industrial markets: satisfaction has to be re-earned, and different value levers move repeat purchase versus advocacy.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** B2B marketers could test whether their key-account programs are over-crediting relationship longevity. The data hint that if you want repeat orders, invest in functional performance and the human, frustration-reducing side of the relationship; if you want customers who champion you, social value and status matter more. Worth validating against actual order and referral data before restructuring account plans.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The finding mirrors something familiar in personal life: knowing someone a long time doesn't make you more forgiving when they let you down. Business buyers, like consumers, appear to re-evaluate suppliers on current performance rather than accumulated history.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey, so causation cannot be established; single industry (manufacturing) and a modest sample of 251 questionnaires; loyalty is self-reported rather than observed in purchase records.

### 5. Why the same in-store technology delights shoppers in one setting and flops in another

**Journal:** *Journal of Strategic Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-15
**Work ID:** W7213352682 | **DOI:** 10.1080/0965254x.2026.2733893

**The study:** Using in-depth interviews across two smart retail settings, researchers applied a critical realist approach to identify why identical technologies produce very different customer experiences. They isolated four underlying mechanisms—goal congruence, task fitness, brand synergy and social consonance—and surfaced emotional reactions rarely discussed in retail tech research, including respect, contradiction, warmth and love. The upshot: context and surrounding actors, not the technology itself, drive whether smart retail lands.

**Why it matters:** Retailers routinely roll out the same kiosks, apps and smart mirrors across formats and markets and are surprised when results diverge; this offers a diagnostic vocabulary for why.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Before scaling a smart retail pilot, brand leaders could screen each site against the four mechanisms: does the technology match what shoppers came to do, fit the task, reinforce the brand's promise, and suit the social setting? A useful framing for post-mortems on technology deployments that tested well but underperformed in rollout.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Shoppers' feelings about store technology are more emotional than "convenient or not"—people report feeling respected or disrespected by a machine. That helps explain why self-checkout or an in-store screen can feel helpful in one shop and insulting in another.

**Caveats:** Small-scale qualitative interview study in two retail settings only; no quantitative test of the proposed mechanisms, and findings may not generalize to other formats or countries.

### 6. AI at the front line: the employees who shape the tools go above and beyond

**Journal:** *Journal of service management* | **Published:** 2026-09-22
**Work ID:** W7213983383 | **DOI:** 10.1108/josm-08-2025-0421

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 234 matched employee-manager pairs in China's service industry, measuring how deeply and broadly AI had been routinized into daily work and how managers rated each employee's proactive service behavior. Employees who had assimilated AI more fully were rated as more proactive, and the mechanism was "AI crafting" — actively reshaping how they used the tools. Notably, employees scored highest on proactive service when they combined approach crafting (expanding and experimenting with AI) and avoidance crafting (deliberately limiting or bounding its use) in balance, rather than leaning on one. Job autonomy amplified the approach route and dampened the avoidance route.

**Why it matters:** Most AI rollouts are measured by adoption rates, but this suggests the payoff in frontline service comes later — from employees who are allowed to adapt, bound and personalize the tools rather than simply use them as issued.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Worth testing whether your own AI deployment metrics are measuring the wrong thing: track how employees modify and set limits on AI use, not just login and usage rates, and check whether teams with more discretion over how they apply AI deliver more initiative-taking service. The study is correlational, so treat autonomy-plus-crafting as a hypothesis to pilot, not an established lever.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A counterintuitive note for anyone anxious about AI at work: the employees doing the best service work here weren't the heaviest AI users — they were the ones who had figured out where the tools help and where to deliberately switch them off.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey, so causation cannot be established; single country (China) and a single sector; behavior is manager-rated rather than directly observed; modest sample (234 pairs).

### 7. Virtual try-on is becoming a mirror — and over-flattering filters will cost you

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-17
**Work ID:** W7203598766 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jcm-12-2024-7415

**The study:** A two-phase study of augmented reality shopping combined netnography and depth interviews with 22 consumers to build a model, then tested it with survey data from 771 consumers using structural equation modeling. The researchers found that when AR felt genuinely augmenting, it triggered cognitive, emotional and "self-extension" responses that led consumers to report discovering things about themselves — their style, their self-image — that they hadn't recognized before. Perceived realism moderated the effect: the self-extension experience depended on consumers believing the AR image was true to life.

**Why it matters:** It reframes AR try-on from a conversion tool into an identity tool, and warns that beautifying or over-glamorizing the virtual image undercuts the very realism that makes the experience meaningful — and sets up disappointment when the real product arrives.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands running virtual try-on should consider A/B testing realism itself: compare a flattering, smoothed AR rendering against a deliberately accurate one and track not just add-to-cart but returns and post-purchase satisfaction. The upside this study points to is engagement and self-exploration, which may justify features that help shoppers explore rather than simply confirm a choice.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For shoppers, this is a reminder that AR mirrors are designed objects: the version of you in the app has been rendered by a brand with an interest in the sale, and the research suggests people genuinely update their self-image based on what they see there.

**Caveats:** Survey-based and cross-sectional, so the causal chain is inferred rather than demonstrated; self-reported self-discovery rather than observed purchase behavior; the qualitative phase is small (N=22); fashion-heavy context limits generalizability across categories and age groups.

### 8. Custom work deals make salespeople better at rescuing unhappy customers

**Journal:** *Journal of Business and Industrial Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-18
**Work ID:** W7203686382 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jbim-03-2026-0234

**The study:** Using survey data from 348 matched salesperson-manager dyads, researchers examined "i-deals" — personalized, individually negotiated work arrangements — in B2B sales. Salespeople with i-deals scored higher on polychronicity (comfort juggling multiple tasks at once), which in turn was associated with more deep acting (genuinely regulating felt emotion) and less surface acting (faking it). Deep acting predicted better service recovery performance, surface acting predicted worse, and a manager's trust strengthened the link between polychronicity and deep acting.

**Why it matters:** It connects an HR lever many firms already use informally — bespoke scheduling, flexibility and role arrangements — to a hard customer outcome: how well reps fix things when deals and deliveries go wrong.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If your service recovery scores are weak, the fix may sit in the employment arrangement rather than the training module. Consider auditing which reps have negotiated personalized arrangements and whether that tracks with recovery outcomes — while noting that the evidence here is correlational and from a single survey.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Customers can usually tell the difference between a rep who is genuinely engaged with their problem and one performing concern; this research suggests that difference partly reflects how well the company treats that rep.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey with no experimental manipulation, so causality is unestablished; outcomes are manager- and self-reported rather than drawn from customer records; country and industry context not specified in the abstract; common-method concerns despite the dyadic design.

### 9. Older guests judge hotel robots on trust, not on how good the tech is

**Journal:** *Journal of Hospitality Marketing & Management* | **Published:** 2026-08-27
**Work ID:** W7204434854 | **DOI:** 10.1080/19368623.2026.2721650

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 283 senior consumers about their experiences with service robots in hotels and tested an Information Systems Success model using PLS-SEM. Information quality and system quality did not directly shape seniors' sense that their privacy was protected — a notable null. What did carry through to hotel brand image was trust and satisfaction with the robot interaction.

**Why it matters:** Hotels deploying robots to cut labor costs often pitch them on technical capability; for an older guest segment, this suggests brand benefit flows through felt trust and comfort rather than feature quality.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If robots are part of your service model, consider testing reassurance and transparency cues — a visible explanation of what the robot records and who sees it — against upgrades in the robot's technical performance, and segment satisfaction data by guest age before scaling further.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Older travelers aren't uniformly hostile to service robots, but the research suggests what wins them over is feeling they can trust the machine, not how slick it is.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey with a modest sample (N=283) reporting significant paths in a structural model, so no causal claims; single sector and a single consumer segment; self-reported perceptions rather than observed booking or spending behavior; country and recruitment method not specified in the abstract.

### 10. Shoppers don't reward green talk — and heavy discounts are where sustainability claims pile up

**Journal:** *Electronic Commerce Research and Applications* | **Published:** 2026-08-20
**Work ID:** W7203821753 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.elerap.2026.101630

**The study:** Researchers combined natural language processing, image color analysis and machine learning across two e-commerce datasets: 1,465 Amazon product listings and 182,275 product-review pairs from BaSalam, a Persian-language marketplace. Sustainability-related language in customer reviews showed no association with higher product ratings or satisfaction — reviewers judged products on performance, usability and value instead. Sustainability mentions clustered most heavily in the deepest-discount category, and the team's multimodal greenwashing detector flagged mismatches between seller claims and buyer experience with 81.6% accuracy on Amazon and 97% on BaSalam.

**Why it matters:** Green claims on product pages may be doing far less for satisfaction and ratings than brands assume, while automated detection tools capable of spotting claim–experience mismatches are now accurate enough for platforms or regulators to deploy at scale.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit whether your sustainability copy is actually moving review sentiment or ratings in your own category data — and prepare for a world where platforms and regulators can algorithmically flag the gap between what a listing claims and what buyers report. The concentration of green language in deep-discount listings is a reputational risk signal worth checking in your own assortment.
- **For general readers and consumers:** When you write a review, you're probably talking about whether the thing works, not whether it's eco-friendly — and this analysis suggests that's true of shoppers generally. It also hints that the loudest sustainability claims often sit on the most heavily discounted items, which is a reason to read such labels skeptically.

**Caveats:** Observational text analysis, not an experiment, so no causal claims; two platforms only (one US-facing, one Iranian), and the Amazon sample of 1,465 listings is small relative to the review-level dataset; sustainability "mentions" in review text are a rough proxy for consumer concern.

### 11. Inside the Facebook groups where a drug reformulation turned patients into activists

**Journal:** *Corporate Communications An International Journal* | **Published:** 2026-09-04
**Work ID:** W7208731674 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ccij-11-2025-0368

**The study:** Researchers analyzed 2,006 comments from Facebook communities formed during France's Levothyrox crisis, when a reformulated thyroid medication triggered widespread reports of side effects. Using qualitative content analysis, they tracked why patients posted, what emotions they expressed, who they blamed, and what they intended to do next. Patients voiced both gratitude, hope and joy toward each other and anger, anxiety and hatred toward institutions, assigning responsibility chiefly to manufacturer Merck and to health authorities — and those emotions translated into information sharing, collective mobilization, boycotts, formal complaints and heightened medical vigilance.

**Why it matters:** It shows how online patient communities convert private distress into organized collective action against a brand, and that supportive, positive emotions within a group can fuel protest rather than defuse it.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Crisis playbooks usually treat negative sentiment as the thing to monitor; this suggests the warmth and solidarity inside an affected-customer community is itself a mobilization engine. For regulated categories especially, consider whether your crisis communications engage those communities directly or cede the narrative to them.
- **For general readers and consumers:** When institutions go quiet, patients build their own information infrastructure — and the comfort people find in those groups is also what gives them the confidence to complain, boycott and push back.

**Caveats:** Single crisis in a single country; qualitative netnographic analysis of public Facebook comments, so it captures the most vocal participants rather than all affected patients; stated behavioral intentions expressed in posts, not verified behavior.

### 12. Happy reviews get social credit; angry reviews get believed

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing Analytics* | **Published:** 2026-09-28
**Work ID:** W7214683938 | **DOI:** 10.1057/s41270-026-00543-6

**The study:** Researchers analyzed more than 172,000 Yelp reviews of leading US quick-service restaurant brands, linking the language of each review to the votes other users gave it. Positive sentiment predicted more "Cool" votes (a marker of social value) but fewer "Useful" votes, while negative sentiment showed the reverse pattern. Reviews written with more social language — references to people, relationships and shared experience — also drew more Cool votes.

**Why it matters:** The same review can be socially popular and informationally discounted, which means the reviews that spread furthest on a platform are not necessarily the ones shoppers treat as credible evidence.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands tracking review performance often treat engagement counts as a single signal; this suggests separating social amplification from perceived informativeness, and testing whether prompts that encourage reviewers to describe the experience socially (who they went with, the occasion) change how far positive reviews travel.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If you read reviews before ordering, note the asymmetry: the warm, chatty five-star posts you enjoy most are precisely the ones other readers rate as least useful, while the complaints you skim past are the ones people treat as real information.

**Caveats:** Observational and correlational — associations, not causation. Single platform (Yelp), single country (US) and a single category (quick-service restaurants); vote counts are a proxy for social and informational value rather than a direct measure.

### 13. People behave worse toward chatbots because they don't feel watched

**Journal:** *Service Industries Journal* | **Published:** 2026-08-11
**Work ID:** W7202155921 | **DOI:** 10.1080/02642069.2026.2690257

**The study:** Across three experiments, researchers tested how consumers behave in post-service interactions — complaints and compensation requests — when the agent on the other side is AI rather than human. Dealing with an AI agent lowered perceived social presence and the sense of being monitored, which loosened moral constraints and increased unethical behavior such as inflated or dishonest claims. Consumers with higher AI self-efficacy cheated more, apparently because they saw the AI as a controllable tool rather than a social entity.

**Why it matters:** Automating complaint handling and refunds may quietly raise fraud and claim-inflation costs, and the most AI-savvy customers are the ones most likely to exploit it.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Before fully automating refunds and goodwill payments, test design cues that restore a sense of being observed — visible audit language, human review notices, agent identity, or mixed human-AI handoffs — and monitor whether claim values drift upward after automation; these are lab findings, so measure the effect in your own claims data.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It turns out most of us are a little more honest when a person might be watching. Chatbots remove that social witness, and the experiments suggest many people stretch the truth without quite noticing they are doing it.

**Caveats:** Experimental studies, likely with scenario-based tasks rather than real money and real service claims; sample details and country not specified in the abstract. Effect sizes in the field may differ from the lab.

### 14. An audit of 12 Spanish fashion flagships found most "phygital" stores are just stores with pickup counters

**Journal:** *Journal of Fashion Marketing and Management* | **Published:** 2026-08-12
**Work ID:** W7202296676 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jfmm-07-2025-0393

**The study:** Researchers conducted structured, non-participatory observation of 12 flagship stores of major Spanish fashion retailers in Madrid, scoring each against 18 technology and experience indicators and completing click-and-collect journeys to build a phygital maturity index. Average maturity was 34.25%, with practical tools (self-checkout, order pickup) the strongest pillar at 53.3% and immersive or sensory experiences the weakest at 16.6%. Zara, Lefties and Springfield scored highest at 55.5%; Hackett and Façonnable scored 5.5%.

**Why it matters:** It quantifies a gap between the industry's rhetoric about immersive retail and what is actually installed on the shop floor — most investment has gone into operational convenience, not experience.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If your store digitization roadmap is dominated by checkout, inventory and BOPS tools, you are in the majority — and that is now table stakes rather than differentiation; the audit framework is replicable, so brands can score their own flagships against competitors on immersivity, personalization and sociability.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The "store of the future" you keep reading about mostly turns out to be a pickup desk and a self-checkout. Shoppers walking into flagship stores in a major European capital encountered very little genuinely immersive technology.

**Caveats:** Small sample of 12 stores in one city (Madrid) in one sector; binary presence/absence coding measures whether tools exist, not whether they work or whether shoppers like them; no consumer outcomes measured.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- *Frontline staff, not the tool, determine whether service technology pays off.* "AI at the front line: the employees who shape the tools go above and beyond" (W7213983383) and "Giving salespeople digital tools makes them improvise better — but only under a certain kind of boss" (W7214497259) both land on employee adaptation plus managerial conditions as the mechanism, extending the previous digest's "When frontline staff see robots as supportive coworkers, they start redesigning their own jobs" (W7203840311) and "Your new tech fails because two people have to adopt it, not one" (W7168115520).
- *Trust, not technical quality, carries AI service into brand outcomes.* "Older guests judge hotel robots on trust, not on how good the tech is" (W7204434854) echoes "Patients want AI in the exam room — but only alongside a doctor" (W7171856573), where trust rather than blame drove reuse, and the authority finding in "Your AI complaint agent only works if it's allowed to issue the refund" (W7203673228).
- *Lifelike design is the weakest lever.* "Making AI avatars look human matters less than making them useful" (W7204579696) finds sensory anthropomorphism only a peripheral condition, consistent with "When robots fail badly, giving them a human face backfires" (W7203488123) and in tension with the cheap relational tweaks in "Hotel robots with child-like voices are rated as better service" (W7203856104).
- *Retention and tenure are poor proxies for loyalty.* "Long-standing B2B customers aren't automatically loyal ones" (W7202159462) matches the consumer-side finding in "Rejected for a loan, but staying with the bank anyway" (W7206172982): the account survives while the satisfaction underneath it does not.
- *Employment conditions show up in customer outcomes.* "Custom work deals make salespeople better at rescuing unhappy customers" (W7203686382) sits alongside "Salespeople who want to look good deliver warmer service — and weaker technical quality" (W7204989107) and "Tech-curious B2B buyers push salespeople onto social media — until they push too hard" (W7204562935) in treating rep-level conditions as a service-quality variable.

**Advances**
- The AI agent thread moves upstream. Last run's AI studies were almost all about service recovery; "When an AI agent does your shopping, trust in the bot becomes trust in the product" (W7203638544) relocates the question to consideration and purchase, where trust transfers from the intermediary to whatever it surfaces — a different gatekeeping problem than the one posed by W7203673228.
- Review signals get disaggregated. "Happy reviews get social credit; angry reviews get believed" (W7214683938) splits engagement into social amplification versus perceived informativeness, sharpening both "Replying to every bad review may be the wrong strategy" (W7207820376) and the inflated-praise mechanism in "When customers help build it and it fails, they praise you anyway" (W7171377067).
- Immersive retail gets an installed-base reality check. "An audit of 12 Spanish fashion flagships found most 'phygital' stores are just stores with pickup counters" (W7202296676) and "Why the same in-store technology delights shoppers in one setting and flops in another" (W7213352682) ground last run's design-logic finding in "In VR retail displays, showing the product in use beats letting shoppers imagine it" (W7169838255) in what is actually deployed and why identical kit diverges by site.

**Counterpoints**
- The clearest tension in this run: "People behave worse toward chatbots because they don't feel watched" (W7202155921) and "Want silent customers to tell you what went wrong? Send the bot, and make it about them" (W7202198898) rest on the same mechanism — reduced social presence and no face to save — but reach opposite conclusions about what gets disclosed. Candor and claim inflation may be two outputs of one design choice.
- "Older guests judge hotel robots on trust, not on how good the tech is" (W7204434854) reports a null for information and system quality, which complicates the competence-above-all reading built from W7203609438 and W7203673228, at least for an older segment.

**New themes**
- Sustainability claims as a credibility and detection problem: "Shoppers don't reward green talk — and heavy discounts are where sustainability claims pile up" (W7203821753) has no antecedent in the previous digest.
- Organized customer counter-power: "Inside the Facebook groups where a drug reformulation turned patients into activists" (W7208731674) extends blame-and-crisis work such as "Who gets blamed when a gig worker misbehaves?" (W7202074508) from individual attribution to collective mobilization.
- Augmented reality as identity work rather than conversion tooling: "Virtual try-on is becoming a mirror — and over-flattering filters will cost you" (W7203598766).

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Across this batch, the verification layer of commerce is breaking down on both sides of the counter, and machines are simultaneously the cause and the proposed cure. Customers inflate claims when an AI handles the refund, and the most AI-literate cheat most ("People behave worse toward chatbots because they don't feel watched," W7202155921); sellers cluster sustainability language on their deepest-discount listings while an automated detector catches the claim–experience gap at 81.6–97% accuracy ("Shoppers don't reward green talk," W7203821753); flattering AR renderings undercut the realism that makes try-on meaningful ("Virtual try-on is becoming a mirror," W7203598766); the warmest reviews are precisely the ones other readers discount ("Happy reviews get social credit; angry reviews get believed," W7214683938); and credibility increasingly transfers from an AI agent to whatever it recommends ("When an AI agent does your shopping, trust in the bot becomes trust in the product," W7203638544).

**Why pitch this now:** Agentic shopping tools are shipping into mainstream retail at the same moment that green-claims rules in the EU and UK and FTC scrutiny in the US are tightening what a product page can assert — and retailers are already fighting returns abuse and claim fraud in an automated service stack.

**Angle:** A reported feature on who or what still verifies anything in an automated marketplace — written for commerce, retail and CX decision-makers, with a consumer-facing version on why the reviews, eco-labels and try-on images people lean on are each getting less reliable for different reasons.

**Potential headlines:**
