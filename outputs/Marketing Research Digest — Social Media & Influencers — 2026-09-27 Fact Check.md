### Study 1: Paid blue checks stopped working: 8 million days of X data show bought verification backfires
**Work ID:** W7214149214 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Effect overstated as elimination** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "under fully paid verification, reversed — so the badge stopped boosting engagement."
- **Abstract says:** "its effect on perceived influence is reduced and reversed under fully paid verification, weakening its effect on engagement."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports a weakened effect on engagement, not a complete loss of the engagement benefit; the reversal is specific to the authenticity-to-perceived-influence path.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "...reversed, weakening the badge's effect on engagement.""

**Issue B. Headline wording stronger than abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "8 million days of X data show bought verification backfires"
- **Abstract says:** "we found verification increases perceived authenticity, which in turn enhances perceived influence... its effect on perceived influence is reduced and reversed under fully paid verification, weakening its effect on engagement"
- **Problem:** "Backfires" implies net harm to engagement, whereas the abstract only reports a reversed intermediate path and a weakened engagement effect. Also, "8 million days" recasts "over 8 million daily observations" as a time span.
- **Suggested fix:** "Try "more than 8 million daily observations show bought verification loses its engagement payoff"."

### Study 2: When Universal pulled its catalog from TikTok, Spotify streams fell — but only for the hits
**Work ID:** W4399061707 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Settles the argument" overclaim** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "It settles a high-stakes industry argument with real market data"
- **Abstract says:** "Recent studies using this quasi-natural experiment have reached different conclusions... We show that these differences arise in part because the estimand changes with the specification."
- **Problem:** The paper's contribution is to explain why prior estimates diverge and to guide estimand choice, not to deliver a definitive settlement; the digest's own caveat about consequential modeling choices sits awkwardly with "settles".
- **Suggested fix:** "Rephrase as "It reconciles conflicting prior estimates and shows where the value is concentrated.""

### Study 8: Personalized ads make people like you — and talk about you badly anyway
**Work ID:** W7214160679 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal framing of survey data** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Personalized ads make people like you — and talk about you badly anyway"
- **Abstract says:** "We conducted an online survey of Instagram users in three target countries... The results consistently indicate that higher personalization fosters more positive attitudes toward ads"
- **Problem:** A cross-sectional survey analyzed with SEM supports association, not causation; the headline asserts that personalized ads cause both liking and complaining.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rephrase as "Liking a personalized ad goes with wanting to complain about it" or add "linked to" phrasing."

**Issue B. Country characterization not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "three EU countries under the same GDPR regime but with differing privacy cultures"
- **Abstract says:** "comparing three EU countries (Poland, Portugal and Sweden) that operate under GDPR but differ in digital literacy and privacy concerns"
- **Problem:** "Privacy cultures" substitutes for the abstract's specific dimensions of digital literacy and privacy concerns; minor but changes the stated basis for comparison.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "differing digital literacy and privacy concerns"."

### Study 11: When a customer swears at your brand online, a gracious reply wins fewer bystanders
**Work ID:** W7214100498 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Consumer angle reverses the implication** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "If you want a company to look bad — or want other customers to take your side — swearing may backfire."
- **Abstract says:** "Complaint tone moderated this indirect effect such that the positive impact of accommodative responses was stronger for civil complaints and weaker for uncivil complaints."
- **Problem:** The study measured bystanders' brand attitude and purchase intentions, not how bystanders judge or side with the complainer. And on the measured outcome, an uncivil complaint reduces the goodwill the brand earns — which works in favor of, not against, a complainer who wants the company to look bad.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as: 'An uncivil complaint blunts the credit a brand earns for responding well — though the study did not measure how bystanders judge the complainer themselves.'"

### Study 15: Inside China's livestream selling machine: what the hosts and their agencies say actually drives sales
**Work ID:** W7204239811 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. 'Dominant' overstates 'prominent'** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Participants identified influencer capability, influencer–agency coordination and platform strategy as the dominant success factors"
- **Abstract says:** "Prominent factors in LSIM campaign success, from LSIs' and MCN team members' perspectives, include influencer capabilities, LSI–MCN coordination and platform strategies."
- **Problem:** The abstract lists these as prominent factors among others, not as the dominant ones; 'dominant' implies a ranking the abstract does not report.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to 'as prominent success factors'."

**Issue B. Specific tension domains not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the study documents the tensions between hosts and agencies — over tools, targets and platform policy — and the workarounds both sides develop"
- **Abstract says:** "Results reveal a dynamic LSIM activity system involving interactions among LSIs, MCN team members, tools, culture and policies... shows how LSIs and MCN team members respond to activity-system contradictions through adaptive strategies."
- **Problem:** The abstract names tools, culture and policies; 'targets' (sales quotas) is not verifiable from the abstract alone.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use the abstract's own list — 'over tools, culture and platform policy' — or note that the specifics come from the full text."

### Study 16: The harsh reviewer problem: negativity reads as helpful but makes the critic look less trustworthy
**Work ID:** W7172001493 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. 'Disappeared' overstates 'mitigated'** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The penalty disappeared when expectations were ambiguous and flipped entirely when people expected negativity"
- **Abstract says:** "the effect on credibility ... is mitigated when consumers have ambiguous prior expectations, and reverses when expectations are negative (studies 3–5)"
- **Problem:** The abstract says the effect is mitigated (reduced) under ambiguous expectations, not eliminated.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to 'The penalty was reduced when expectations were ambiguous and reversed when people expected negativity.'"

### Study 17: When your doctor is also an influencer, your skepticism switches off
**Work ID:** W7213976385 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Proposed mechanism presented as demonstrated, and extended to commercial recommendations** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "the same mechanism that improves adherence to genuine medical advice also lowers consumers' guard against commercial recommendations"
- **Abstract says:** "These strategies help them maintain authority while mitigating perceptions of overt persuasion and may foster increased recommendation adherence. We propose a theory-building mechanism, the mere social media presence effect..."
- **Problem:** The abstract hedges adherence ('may foster') and frames the mechanism as proposed theory-building; it says nothing about commercial or sponsored recommendations, which the digest headline ('your skepticism switches off') and this line treat as established.
- **Suggested fix:** "Soften to 'the mechanism the authors propose may both support adherence to genuine medical advice and, they argue, mute audiences' recognition of persuasion attempts — though commercial endorsements were not directly tested.'"

### Study 18: Fast-moving brands on social media earn more word of mouth — but mainly from collectivist consumers
**Work ID:** W7213466946 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Self-presentation moderator dropped from study description** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Three online experiments tested whether consumers' perception that a brand is agile on social media — quick, responsive, adaptive — drives positive electronic word of mouth."
- **Abstract says:** "a significant three-way interaction reveals that self-presentation behaviors play a more pronounced moderating role for UK consumers"
- **Problem:** Self-presentation is a central moderator in Studies 1-2 (a three-way interaction), but it appears only glancingly in the story angles and not in the description of what was tested.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add that the model also tested self-presentation behaviors as a moderator, which mattered more for UK than Turkish participants."

**Issue B. 'Substantially stronger' firmer than abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "but the effect was substantially stronger among Turkish participants"
- **Abstract says:** "the positive impact of PSMA on e-WOM is greater for Turkish participants than for UK participants"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports a greater effect but gives no magnitude, so 'substantially' is not supported.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to 'the effect was stronger among Turkish participants'."

### Study 20: "The AI did it" is a risky excuse: blaming the algorithm cuts blame but signals moral indifference
**Work ID:** W7214331895 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. 'Most people' implies a proportion the study does not report** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "research suggests most people accept that the brand is less factually to blame while trusting it less as a moral actor"
- **Abstract says:** "At the factual level, this strategy increases consumers' perceived responsibility reduction... At the value level, it also triggers perceived moral indifference, thereby producing a backfire effect that damages trust."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports average condition differences, not the share of participants showing each response; 'most people' reads as a headcount finding.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to 'research suggests people on average see the brand as less factually to blame while trusting it less as a moral actor'."

### Study 21: ASMR ads land very differently for "masculine" and "feminine" brands — 15,807 YouTube comments show why
**Work ID:** W7214311277 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline implies mechanism from associational text mining** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "ASMR ads land very differently for "masculine" and "feminine" brands — 15,807 YouTube comments show why"
- **Abstract says:** "Feminine brands are associated with higher immersion scores, whereas masculine brands are associated with more sexualization-oriented discourse."

### Study 22: Crowdfunding titles that brag about market traction win; titles that get personal lose
**Work ID:** W7213972386 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Platform nationality not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Researchers analyzed 2,064 startup campaigns on the Chinese crowdfunding platform Kaishiba"
- **Abstract says:** "we examine 2,064 startups that sought funding on the Kaishiba crowdfunding platform"
- **Problem:** The abstract names the platform but never identifies it as Chinese; the descriptor (which also drives the caveat about generalizing to "Kickstarter-style Western platforms") is unverifiable from the abstract alone.

**Issue B. Win/lose phrasing for correlational data** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Crowdfunding titles that brag about market traction win; titles that get personal lose"
- **Abstract says:** "market validation information in a project title is positively correlated with crowdfunding performance, whereas low psychological distance information is negatively associated with crowdfunding performance"
- **Problem:** The headline states an outcome as if causal, while the abstract reports only correlation; the digest's own caveat says causality is not established.

### Study 23: Being outnumbered changes what people buy: gender-minority settings boost appeal of gender-neutral products
**Work ID:** W7214404629 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline states behavior, study measured intention** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Being outnumbered changes what people buy: gender-minority settings boost appeal of gender-neutral products"
- **Abstract says:** "individuals in a gender-minority context show significantly higher gender alignment and purchase intentions towards gender-neutral products, with self-verification serving as a mediator"
- **Problem:** The outcome was stated purchase intention in experiments, not observed purchasing; the headline asserts a change in actual buying, which contradicts the digest's own caveat that intentions rather than purchases were measured.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "Being outnumbered changes what people say they'd buy" or "...shifts stated interest in gender-neutral products"."
