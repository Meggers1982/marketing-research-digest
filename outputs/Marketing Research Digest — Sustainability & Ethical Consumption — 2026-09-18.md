### 1. Consumers reward brands for adding green features — and barely notice when they take bad stuff away

**Journal:** *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* | **Published:** 2026-09-10
**Work ID:** W7212134127 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11747-026-01201-0

**The study:** Across nine studies, including a field experiment and a lab experiment, researchers tested how people judge a company's sustainability when it adds an eco-friendly component (say, bolting on a recycled material or a green add-on) versus when it removes or reduces one. Adding eco-components consistently boosted sustainability judgments; subtracting them earned almost no credit, even when the subtraction delivered real environmental gains. The asymmetry was specific to eco-components — people did recognize the benefit of removing conventional, less green elements — and it was driven by the perception that adding signals more corporate effort. The bias shrank when the component in question was a prototypical example of sustainability.

**Why it matters:** Genuine sustainability progress often means using less — less packaging, fewer materials, fewer features — yet the market appears to pay for visible additions instead, creating an incentive to do the more photogenic rather than the more effective thing.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If your biggest environmental win is a subtraction, assume it will be undercommunicated by default. Worth testing: framing removals as deliberate effort (engineering work, redesign, cost borne by the company) or pairing them with a prototypical green cue, and checking whether that closes the credit gap in your own category before assuming the lab result scales.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A useful shopper heuristic: the greenest change a company makes is often invisible. When you see a product loudly advertising an added eco-feature, the honest question is whether anything was actually taken out of the footprint — or whether something was simply added to the label.

**Caveats:** Most of the nine studies are experiments, several likely with online panels using hypothetical offerings; the abstract reports only one field experiment and does not specify samples, countries, or categories.

### 2. AI clones of famous people leave fans cold — unless the dialogue gets intimate

**Journal:** *European Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-16
**Work ID:** W7213257365 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ejm-12-2025-1096

**The study:** Six experiments compared consumer reactions to AI-powered celebrity avatars versus the real human celebrities they represent, using Chinese Generation Y and Generation X celebrities and globally recognized Western celebrities, with both Chinese and Western participants. Avatars produced lower commitment, emotional value and celebrity attachment, and the effect ran through reduced perceived intimacy and then weaker enduring involvement — meaning existing parasocial bonds did not simply transfer to the digital stand-in. When researchers manipulated the avatar's dialogue to convey more intimacy, the negative pathway weakened and could even flip positive.

**Why it matters:** Talent agencies, platforms and brands are racing to license AI versions of famous faces; this suggests the asset's value depends on conversational design, not on the likeness itself.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Treat a celebrity avatar as a reach extender for routine fan contact, not a substitute in emotionally loaded campaigns. The testable lever here is dialogue quality — personalized replies, behind-the-scenes detail, emotionally resonant scripting — and brands should measure attachment and commitment, not just engagement, before scaling an avatar into core communications.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It turns out fans are quietly tracking who is actually on the other end. Knowing a message comes from a machine wearing a familiar face drains the sense of closeness that makes celebrity endorsement work in the first place.

**Caveats:** Experiments used primarily static stimuli, which the authors acknowledge limits ecological validity; outcomes are self-reported attitudes rather than observed behavior; sample sizes and recruitment sources are not specified in the abstract.

### 3. Inside zero-waste living: the exhausting self-audit behind 'responsible' consumption

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Research* | **Published:** 2026-09-05
**Work ID:** W7211935268 | **DOI:** 10.1093/jcr/ucag036

**The study:** A multi-sited ethnography of zero-waste lifestyles in Denmark traced how people actually construct themselves as responsible consumers. The researcher identifies a threefold process — self-problematization, moralization and intensification — in which participants continually scrutinize whether their own conduct is good enough, reinterpret shopping as a moral obligation, and ratchet up their commitment over time, all sustained through everyday interactions where they hold themselves and each other accountable. Central tensions included acting under uncertainty about what is truly better, trading personal well-being against the common good, and advocating without seeming judgmental.

**Why it matters:** It shows that ethical consumption is less a preference to be targeted than an identity project under constant self-surveillance — which shapes how such consumers hear brand sustainability claims and how sustainable they find the lifestyle over time.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For brands courting the sustainability-committed segment, the insight is that these consumers are auditing themselves, and any claim you make becomes evidence in that audit. Messaging that resolves uncertainty and reduces moral effort may land better than messaging that raises the bar — and there is a real risk that campaigns amplifying individual responsibility fuel the fatigue that drives people out of the practice.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If you have ever felt guilty in a supermarket aisle, this is a portrait of that feeling scaled into a way of life: constant self-scrutiny, awkwardness about preaching to friends, and the nagging sense that you are still not doing enough — while the structural sources of waste sit largely untouched.

**Caveats:** Interpretive ethnography in a single country (Denmark) with a self-selected, highly committed group; no quantitative generalization to mainstream shoppers and no causal claims.

### 4. How the community that helps you save can end up trapping you

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Research* | **Published:** 2026-09-07
**Work ID:** W7211969452 | **DOI:** 10.1093/jcr/ucag035

**The study:** An ethnographic study of Chama, a community-based savings and support group in Kenya, examined why members stay bound to a practice that once helped them. The author identifies three mechanisms — prefiguration, socialization and entrenchment — through which participation accumulates commitments, dependencies and obligations, generating recurring misalignments between what members want and what the group requires. Members work continuously to repair those misalignments, but that repair work itself reproduces the dependencies, deepening what the paper calls consumer entrapment.

**Why it matters:** It offers a mechanism for the dark side of community-based financial products and brand communities: the same social obligations that drive commitment and retention can leave participants unable to exit.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Anyone building loyalty programs, subscriptions or brand communities on social obligation should look at this as a diagnostic of how stickiness turns into entrapment — and consider whether graceful exit paths are an ethical requirement, and a reputational safeguard, rather than a leak to be plugged.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For readers in savings circles, group buying schemes or tight-knit membership communities: the paper is a reminder that obligations to other members can quietly outlast the benefits, and that the effort you put into keeping the arrangement working is often what keeps you in it.

**Caveats:** Qualitative ethnography of a single community form in one country; findings are interpretive and not intended to be statistically generalizable.

### 5. Small firms that trust their partners most are the slowest to adopt AI

**Journal:** *Industrial Marketing Management* | **Published:** 2026-09-15
**Work ID:** W7213367223 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.indmarman.2026.09.001

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 488 managers of small and medium-sized enterprises in Türkiye and the UK about cross-sector partnerships and their intentions to adopt frontier AI for sustainable value creation. Partnerships raised perceived usefulness and ease of use of the technology, as expected — but high trust within those partnerships was associated with lower AI adoption intentions, a pattern the authors call the trust paradox. Their interpretation is that firms hold back on disruptive tools to protect existing relationships and the stable flow of resources those relationships guarantee, and the pattern appeared in both countries.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the strongest B2B relationships can act as a brake on technology-driven change, which reframes partner trust as something to be managed rather than simply maximized.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you sell AI tools or orchestrate supplier ecosystems, the blocker may not be skepticism about the technology but loyalty to incumbent partners. Worth probing in your own pipeline: whether deals stall where relationships are closest, and whether bringing partners into the adoption plan — rather than around them — unlocks them.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Limited direct relevance for consumers, beyond explaining why some small suppliers you deal with are slower to modernize than their stated enthusiasm for technology would suggest.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey measuring adoption intentions, not actual adoption, so causal direction cannot be established; results rest on self-reported paths from managers in two countries; SME context only.

### 6. Consumers will pay more for "sustainable" beer — but Fair Trade labels actually cost you money

**Journal:** *British Food Journal* | **Published:** 2026-08-24
**Work ID:** W7204076930 | **DOI:** 10.1108/bfj-02-2026-0193

**The study:** Researchers ran a discrete choice experiment with 1,046 Spanish beer consumers, each facing eight choice sets of two beers plus a no-purchase option, and estimated willingness to pay with a mixed logit model. A generic "Sustainable" claim earned a premium of €0.68 on average, rising to €2.18 among consumers who were both health-oriented and active in sports. Social claims went the other way: Fair Trade carried a mean willingness to pay of −€0.91 and community support −€0.95, though the large dispersion around those means signals two coexisting camps — genuinely ethical buyers and outright skeptics. Reusable glass packaging earned a clear +€0.35 premium, while recyclable glass drew no significant valuation at all.

**Why it matters:** It puts euro figures on individual ESG attributes and shows that not all sustainability messaging pays — some of it repels mainstream buyers, meaning undifferentiated green labeling can destroy value rather than create it.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Test sustainability claims attribute by attribute and segment by segment before rolling them out on-pack: a choice experiment like this suggests reuse beats recyclability in consumer eyes, and that social-ethics claims may need to be targeted rather than broadcast. Treat the negative Fair Trade result as a hypothesis about skepticism in your own category, not a verdict — it comes from one country and one product.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If you buy beer, note which claims are doing real work: "recyclable" glass is so standard that shoppers place no value on it, while "reusable" earns a genuine premium — a reminder that many eco-labels are priced on perception, not impact.

**Caveats:** Hypothetical choice sets rather than observed purchases; single country (Spain) and single category (beer); stated willingness to pay is generally inflated relative to real spending; lifestyle segments are self-reported.

### 7. Does corporate social responsibility make people consume better — or just consume more guilt-free?

**Journal:** *International Marketing Review* | **Published:** 2026-09-10
**Work ID:** W7212036936 | **DOI:** 10.1108/imr-03-2025-0111

**The study:** The authors pooled 51 studies in a meta-analytic structural equation model to test whether corporate social responsibility activity actually shifts consumers toward sustainable consumption or merely legitimizes business-as-usual buying. They found green marketing works mainly indirectly — through brand loyalty, which in turn predicts consumer sustainable action — and that environmental concern feeds perceptions of corporate ethicality and value alignment. Cultural collectivism strengthened the CSR-to-loyalty link, and visible employee involvement boosted sustainable action by signaling authenticity, leading the authors to argue for a "glocalized" CSR strategy adapted to local social norms.

**Why it matters:** It suggests CSR messaging pays off through loyalty and perceived authenticity rather than by directly changing consumption, and that the same campaign will land differently in collectivist versus individualist markets.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If CSR's route to behavior runs through brand loyalty and perceived ethicality, the measurable KPI is trust and authenticity signals — including employee participation — not green-claim recall. The collectivism moderation is a concrete argument for localizing sustainability creative across markets rather than running one global spot.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A sharp question for shoppers: does a company's good-citizen branding actually change what you buy, or mostly make you feel better about buying it? The synthesis suggests the effect works on loyalty first — behavior change is the weaker link.

**Caveats:** Meta-analysis inherits the limits of its inputs, most of which are cross-sectional surveys that cannot establish causation; measures of "sustainable action" are largely self-reported; the abstract reports directional paths without effect sizes; cultural moderators are inferred at the country level.

### 8. In 14,579 Vietnamese firms, stacking digital tools on top of each other starts to backfire

**Journal:** *Marketing Intelligence & Planning* | **Published:** 2026-08-31
**Work ID:** W7204753244 | **DOI:** 10.1108/mip-12-2025-0984

**The study:** Using two-stage least squares on data from 14,579 firms operating in Vietnam, the researchers examined how digital technology, intellectual capital and corporate social responsibility jointly affect marketing performance. Digital technology and intellectual capital each raised marketing performance, but their interaction showed diminishing returns: pairing digital investment with heavy structural capital and capital-employed intensity reduced performance, while pairing it with human capital efficiency improved it. CSR raised sales per employee across the board but reduced sales and marketing costs only at large firms.

**Why it matters:** It is rare, large-scale firm-level evidence that digital investment pays off through people rather than systems, and that CSR's financial payoff is uneven by firm size.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Before adding another platform, ask whether the constraint is technology or the human capability to use it — this dataset suggests returns to digital tooling turn negative when it outpaces the workforce. Note too that CSR appeared to lower marketing costs only for large firms, so smaller brands should not assume the same efficiency dividend.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Little direct relevance for consumers, beyond the reminder that companies' digital and sustainability spending does not automatically translate into better performance or better products.

**Caveats:** Single country (Vietnam) and observational firm data; 2SLS mitigates but does not eliminate endogeneity concerns; CSR and digital technology are measured with firm-level proxies rather than observed practices; no consumer-level outcomes.

### 9. Shoppers will buy the fish nobody wants — if you tell them it was caught sustainably

**Journal:** *Food Quality and Preference* | **Published:** 2026-09-08
**Work ID:** W7204740815 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.foodqual.2026.106084

**The study:** Researchers ran a discrete choice experiment asking UK consumers to value fish fingers made from under-utilized bycatch species that are usually discarded, testing three port-specific versions (Plymouth, Lowestoft and Grimsby) and a novel set of product-design and provenance attributes. Every attribute tested was valued positively, but by far the strongest driver of choice was a sustainable-catch claim. Notably, how close shoppers lived to the sourcing port barely moved their valuations, and the three regional versions performed similarly.

**Why it matters:** It suggests a commercial route to markets for low-value bycatch species — a real fisheries waste problem — and shows that in processed seafood, sustainability credentials, not local provenance, carry the pricing power.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For food and retail brands, this is a test case for turning waste streams into products: the experiment implies sustainability labeling should lead the pack copy while "local" works as a value-add rather than the headline. Because choice experiments measure stated trade-offs, the obvious next step is a limited in-store launch to see whether the willingness to pay survives a real shelf and a real price tag.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Much of the fish pulled up in nets gets thrown back because it has no market. This study shows consumers are open to eating those species once they are processed into a familiar format — the fish finger — and told the catch was sustainable, which hints at how quickly "trash fish" can become dinner.

**Caveats:** Hypothetical choice experiment rather than observed purchases; single country (UK) and a single product category; results specific to three ports and a co-created prototype not yet sold at scale.

### 10. Tying executive bonuses to ESG goals appears to lower a company's reputational risk

**Journal:** *Journal of Business Research* | **Published:** 2026-09-14
**Work ID:** W7213035748 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jbusres.2026.116533

**The study:** Using panel data on S&P 1500 firms from 2010 to 2021, researchers examined "CSR contracting" — linking top executive pay to corporate social responsibility criteria — and its relationship to measured reputational risk. Firms that used CSR contracting showed lower reputational risk, an effect partly explained by reduced stakeholder pressure; the authors interpret this through expectancy violation theory, arguing such contracts raise stakeholder expectations that firms then work to avoid breaching. The link was stronger for firms with more experience using these contracts and weaker where the CEO also chaired the board.

**Why it matters:** It offers evidence that a governance lever — how executives are paid — translates into how exposed a brand is to reputational blowups, which matters for boards deciding whether ESG-linked pay is substance or window dressing.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For brand and communications leaders, the finding reframes ESG-linked compensation as risk management rather than PR: worth examining internally whether your pay metrics are specific enough to change behavior, and noting that combined CEO/chair roles appear to dilute the effect. This is observational panel data, so the direction of causality deserves scrutiny.
- **For general readers and consumers:** When a company announces that executive bonuses now depend on sustainability or social targets, it is easy to dismiss as spin — but the data suggest firms that do it subsequently behave less badly, if only because they have raised the bar they will be judged against.

**Caveats:** Observational panel data on US-listed firms only — associations, not proven causation; relies on third-party reputational-risk and CSR measures; no consumer-level evidence.

### 11. Religious young shoppers buy secondhand clothes for the thrill and the status, not out of moral duty

**Journal:** *International Journal of Retail & Distribution Management* | **Published:** 2026-09-15
**Work ID:** W7213254319 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ijrdm-11-2025-0869

**The study:** A survey of 316 religious young Muslims in Indonesia who had previously bought secondhand fashion online tested whether spirituality drove their purchase intentions, using PLS-SEM to examine brand consciousness, felt responsibility for sustainability and perceived social benefits as mediators. Spirituality had no direct effect on purchase intention; the paths that mattered ran through brand consciousness and perceived social benefits. Felt responsibility for sustainability was not a significant route to purchase at all, though familiarity with the circular economy did strengthen the link between spirituality and that sense of responsibility.

**Why it matters:** In the world's largest Muslim market, and a fast-growing e-retail one, the motive for buying pre-owned clothing looks self- and status-oriented rather than ethical — which changes what resale platforms should actually say in their marketing.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Resale and thrift platforms may be over-investing in moral and environmental messaging: the study suggests brand access (getting labels you otherwise could not afford) and social payoff are the live buttons, with sustainability framing better suited to consumers already familiar with circular-economy ideas. Worth A/B testing hedonic versus ethical creative rather than assuming values-led copy converts.
- **For general readers and consumers:** People often narrate their thrifting as an environmental choice, but when researchers traced the actual drivers among religious young shoppers, the pull was brand names and social standing. It is a reminder that sustainable behavior frequently arrives dressed up as a bargain.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey, so no causal claims; stated purchase intentions rather than observed behavior; single country and a narrow sample of religious young Muslims with prior secondhand experience; results depend on self-reported scales in a structural model.

### 12. Say what you did or what you achieved — but don't do both in the same sustainability post

**Journal:** *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* | **Published:** 2026-07-22
**Work ID:** W7170105320 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11747-026-01181-1

**The study:** Across seven studies, researchers tested how companies should frame environmental messages: process frames ("We deployed electric vehicles") versus outcome frames ("We reduced our carbon footprint by 50%"). The package included four online experiments, a field experiment, an analysis of 12,311 environmental posts on X from 110 US firms, and 533 eco-oriented crowdfunding projects. Both frames lifted engagement (likes, shares, comments) and downstream interest in the firm, but through different psychological routes — and combining them in one message backfired because the two frames clash. Pairing warmth language with process frames amplified engagement, while competence language boosted both frames' post engagement but only helped outcome frames convert into interest in the firm itself.

**Why it matters:** Sustainability communication is a high-stakes, low-guidance area for brands, and this gives a concrete rule of thumb: pick one frame per post and match the emotional tone to it, rather than stuffing activity and achievement into the same message.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit your last quarter of sustainability posts for frame mixing — the field and secondary-data evidence suggests combined process-plus-outcome messages underperform, so an A/B test splitting them into separate single-frame posts, with warmth language on process and competence language on outcome, is a cheap thing to try.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For readers, this is a window into why corporate green messaging feels engineered: the difference between "we installed solar panels" and "we cut emissions 50%" is not just accounting, it is a deliberate choice about which mental picture you form and how much you trust the company.

**Caveats:** Several components are online experiments measuring engagement and stated purchase or investment intentions rather than actual spending; the secondary data covers US firms on a single platform (X) and one crowdfunding context, so generalization to other markets and channels is untested.

### 13. People who pay to upgrade their fitness app track more — for about a month, and still don't lose weight

**Journal:** *Marketing Science* | **Published:** 2026-08-06
**Work ID:** W4312971961 | **DOI:** 10.1287/mksc.2022.0437

**The study:** Using large-scale behavioral data from a fitness-tracking app and a staggered difference-in-differences design, researchers compared users before and after they upgraded from free to premium. Paying immediately increased food and exercise logging, calorie-budget adherence, and exercise calories — but the lift faded within several weeks and never produced sustained weight loss. Users who had barely used the free version showed much bigger post-upgrade jumps than heavy free users, a pattern the authors read as novelty wearing off rather than sunk-cost motivation.

**Why it matters:** Freemium upgrades are the main monetization lever in mobile health, yet this shows the paywall buys a short burst of engagement rather than lasting behavior change — and that naive analyses that ignore who self-selects into paying dramatically overstate the benefit.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If your subscription business reports "premium users are more engaged" as proof of product value, this is a warning that most of that gap is selection plus a novelty window; the testable question is what retention or coaching intervention you deploy at week four, when the lift evaporates.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Paying for the premium tier of a health app is unlikely to be what makes you healthier — the money buys a few weeks of renewed enthusiasm, and consumers may get the same effect by simply restarting their logging habit for free.

**Caveats:** Single app and single category, observational data with quasi-experimental identification rather than a randomized trial, and outcomes are app-recorded (self-logged food, weight) rather than clinically measured.

### 14. When CSR looks like compliance, it can damage a firm's marketing muscle

**Journal:** *Journal of Business and Industrial Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-10
**Work ID:** W7212064195 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jbim-08-2025-0806

**The study:** Analyzing panel data on 3,877 firm-year observations of Chinese A-share listed companies from 2008 to 2017 (fixed-effects regression with a Heckman correction), researchers found that corporate social responsibility activity was associated with weaker marketing capability — the opposite of the trust-building effect usually assumed. The negative relationship was stronger where government intervention was heavier and where CSR reporting was mandatory, and weaker in regions with higher Buddhist belief density, which the authors treat as conditions shaping whether stakeholders read CSR as sincere.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the payoff from social responsibility spending depends less on what a company does than on whether customers and partners believe it chose to do it — with mandated disclosure potentially stripping out the relational benefit entirely.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Before scaling CSR reporting in regulated markets, ask whether your B2B customers will read it as commitment or as paperwork; the implication worth testing is that voluntary, specific, category-relevant initiatives earn relational credit that mandated boilerplate does not.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It offers a reason ordinary buyers are cynical about sustainability reports: when everyone is required to publish one, the document stops signaling anything about the company behind it.

**Caveats:** Single country and a listed-firm sample over 2008–2017; the design is observational panel data, so causal claims rest on modeling assumptions; "marketing capability" and the Buddhist-belief-density moderator are proxy measures open to alternative interpretation.

### 15. Italy's food future: algae and plant protein get a hearing, insects and lab-grown meat don't

**Journal:** *Food Quality and Preference* | **Published:** 2026-09-08
**Work ID:** W7211939433 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.foodqual.2026.106104

**The study:** Researchers surveyed a nationally stratified sample of 2,000 Italian consumers (by age, gender and region) about seven innovative foods and food technologies, measuring food neophobia, food technology neophobia, disgust sensitivity, trust, sensation seeking and health interest alongside perceived risk, benefit, familiarity and emotional responses. Italians scored high on both food and technology neophobia, with low trust and strong health concerns. Plant-based proteins, algae and unusual fruits came closest to consumers' ideals; insect-based proteins and cultured meat faced the steepest resistance. Curiosity was the emotion most strongly linked to acceptance, disgust the most damaging, and acceptance skewed toward younger, higher-income, better-educated respondents.

**Why it matters:** Sustainable protein launches are often marketed as a single category, but this shows the acceptance gap between algae or plant protein and insects or cultured meat is enormous in a country where food is bound up with identity.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Test curiosity-led framing rather than sustainability-led framing for novel foods: the data suggest emotional response, not environmental argument, tracks acceptance most closely. It also suggests sequencing matters -- lead a market like Italy with algae and plant protein and treat insects and cultured meat as a later, trust-dependent phase.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Why the 'yuck' reaction matters more than the argument: the same shopper who buys plant-based burgers may refuse cricket flour, and the study maps who is most and least open to eating differently.

**Caveats:** Survey of perceptions and stated acceptability, not observed purchases; single country (Italy); cross-sectional, so no causal claims; clustering analysis described as exploratory.

### 16. From Nairobi to Norway, two food motives show up everywhere: convenience and sustainability

**Journal:** *Food Quality and Preference* | **Published:** 2026-09-04
**Work ID:** W7208710969 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.foodqual.2026.106103

**The study:** Researchers administered a 39-item Food Choice Questionnaire to urban consumers in four African countries (South Africa, Kenya, Uganda, Ethiopia) and three European ones (Norway, Finland, France), with N=500 per country. The standard questionnaire's assumed factor structure held up poorly across countries, so the team ran separate exploratory factor analyses per country and compared loading patterns. Convenience and sustainability emerged as near-identical motives in all seven markets; meat-related motives appeared everywhere but varied more in structure, and emotional eating showed up in most countries. Men leaned more on meat-related motives, women scored higher on emotional eating.

**Why it matters:** It suggests two pillars of food positioning -- convenience and sustainability -- translate across very different income levels and cultures, while meat messaging does not travel as cleanly.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For brands running cross-border food research, the finding that the standard motive instrument has low reliability across markets is a warning: imported WEIRD-country scales may be measuring different things in different places. Convenience and sustainability look like the safest shared axes for global positioning; meat appeals likely need local rebuilding.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Despite huge differences in wealth and cuisine, urban shoppers in Addis Ababa and Helsinki reach for food for recognizably similar reasons -- they just express those reasons through very different plates.

**Caveats:** Self-reported motives, not purchase behavior; urban consumers only, deliberately chosen as early adopters and not representative of national populations; cross-sectional; the contribution is partly methodological.

### 17. Wanting to stand out doesn't make luxury shoppers buy green -- unless they already see themselves as green

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Behaviour* | **Published:** 2026-09-01
**Work ID:** W7204951358 | **DOI:** 10.1002/cb.70241

**The study:** A mixed-methods project in an emerging market combined in-depth interviews with luxury fashion entrepreneurs and industry practitioners (Study 1) with a survey testing how consumers' need for uniqueness translates into sustainable luxury purchase intentions (Study 2). Green self-identity was the only significant mediator: the desire to be different converted into sustainable luxury intent when consumers had internalized an environmental identity. Minimalism, cultural fusion and "quiet distinction" did not mediate the relationship.

**Why it matters:** It challenges the common luxury playbook of selling sustainability as understated taste or craft heritage, suggesting those aesthetic routes don't convert uniqueness-seekers unless environmental identity is already in place.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Worth testing whether your sustainable luxury line is recruiting customers on aesthetics (quiet luxury, minimalism, artisanal fusion) when the persuasive lever may be identity affirmation -- helping customers see themselves as environmentally minded people. In emerging markets especially, the exclusivity-plus-sustainability pitch may only land on an already-green self-concept.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A reminder that "quiet luxury" and "sustainable luxury" are not the same purchase: understated design signals taste, not values, and consumers appear to know the difference.

**Caveats:** Study 2 is a cross-sectional survey measuring purchase intentions rather than actual purchases, so causal direction is not established; single emerging market; sample size for the quantitative study not reported in the abstract; qualitative component is a small practitioner sample.

### 18. Customers can tell when a company is still blaming the crisis -- and they call it opportunism

**Journal:** *Journal of Service Research* | **Published:** 2026-08-18
**Work ID:** W7203694398 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10946705261471935

**The study:** Researchers analyzed 2,371 public social media comments using thematic, sentiment and emotion analysis to examine what they call crisis-induced service decline rationalization: using crisis language to justify sustained cuts in service availability, quality or transparency. Four themes dominated the commentary -- inconsistent frontline policies and communication, perceived opportunism in service reductions, operational failures and declining reliability, and frustration with access constraints. The authors map these onto four frontline mismatches (rule coherence, motive, capability versus promise, and access fairness) that drive attributions about a company's motives, competence and fairness.

**Why it matters:** Cuts made under crisis cover -- reduced hours, thinner staffing, removed services -- accumulate into trust and legitimacy damage once customers conclude the crisis is an excuse rather than a cause.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit which pandemic- or crisis-era service reductions are still in place and still being explained by the crisis; the study suggests the reputational risk sits less in the cut itself than in inconsistent frontline messaging about why it exists. Consider legitimacy monitoring of customer commentary as a standing metric, not a crisis-only exercise.
- **For general readers and consumers:** That feeling that shorter hours, vanished phone lines and skeleton staffing never came back after the emergency ended? Customers are voicing it publicly, and they read it as companies pocketing the savings.

**Caveats:** Observational analysis of self-selected public social media commentary, which skews toward complaints; no experimental test of consumer response; the causal mechanisms are offered as propositions rather than tested; context and platform of the commentary are narrowly defined.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Across this batch, sustainability gets rewarded when it is visible, voluntary and singular — and gets nothing, or is actively penalized, when it is invisible, mandated or moralizing. "Consumers reward brands for adding green features — and barely notice when they take bad stuff away" (W7212134127) shows subtraction earning almost no credit even when it delivers real gains; "Consumers will pay more for 'sustainable' beer — but Fair Trade labels actually cost you money" (W7204076930) puts a negative euro figure on social-ethics claims; "When CSR looks like compliance, it can damage a firm's marketing muscle" (W7212064195) finds the relational benefit stripped out where reporting is mandatory; "Say what you did or what you achieved — but don't do both in the same sustainability post" (W7170105320) shows even well-intentioned messages cancel each other out when stacked. Add "Religious young shoppers buy secondhand clothes for the thrill and the status, not out of moral duty" (W7213254319) and "Shoppers will buy the fish nobody wants — if you tell them it was caught sustainably" (W7204740815), and the picture is a market that pays for a legible sustainability signal rather than for environmental substance.

**Why pitch this now:** Mandatory sustainability disclosure and anti-greenwashing rules are landing across Europe and beyond at exactly the moment this evidence suggests mandated, undifferentiated and morally framed claims stop earning any commercial return — so compliance budgets are rising while the marketing payback is falling.

**Angle:** A reported feature on the widening gap between doing sustainability and getting credit for it: why the greenest decisions are the ones consumers can't see, why "everyone must report" turns reporting into noise, and what brands are doing to re-earn signal value. Aimed at CMOs, sustainability leads and brand strategists, with a consumer-facing read on why green labels feel increasingly hollow.

**Potential headlines:**
- The greenest thing a company does is usually the thing nobody pays it for
- Making sustainability reports compulsory may have made them worthless
- What your eco-label is actually selling you

**Potential outlets:**
- **Marketing Week** — has an established audience arguing about sustainability claims, effectiveness and brand-building ROI, and this is a direct evidence-led challenge to current green comms practice.
- **MIT Sloan Management Review** — suits the governance-meets-marketing framing, including the mandated-versus-voluntary CSR distinction and the executive-pay evidence.
- **The Grocer** — the food and drink specifics (beer claims, bycatch fish fingers, novel proteins) give this a concrete category spine for a retail and FMCG readership facing on-pack claim decisions.
- **Vox** — runs consumer-behavior and climate explainers for general readers and would take the "why eco-labels stopped meaning anything" angle without needing trade framing.
