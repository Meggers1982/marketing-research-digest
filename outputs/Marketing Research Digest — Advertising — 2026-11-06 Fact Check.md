### Study 1: Go dark for one quarter, lose a tenth of your market share
**Work ID:** W7139097509 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Design detail not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "comparing market share for brands that advertised continuously against those that paused"
- **Abstract says:** "this large-scale study quantifies the cost of quarterly advertising stops, analysing 7,029 observations from over 1,000 brands across 22 product categories. On average, market share changed-10% after a single quarter of no advertising and-14% after four."
- **Problem:** The abstract does not describe the comparison design (continuous advertisers vs. pausers); it only reports average share change after pauses. The digest attributes a specific analytic structure that cannot be verified.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say the study "quantified average market share change following quarterly advertising stops" and note the analytic approach is not described in the abstract (as the Caveats already hint)."

### Study 3: Crowded food ads make the food look more fattening
**Work ID:** W7212718074 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Fullness goals" rendered as "urge to splurge"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Dense layouts improved responses when more calories were desirable (indulgence goals, feeling the urge to splurge, indulgence-positioned products)"
- **Abstract says:** "higher visual density improves consumer responses when higher calories are desirable, such as under fullness or indulgence goals and for indulgence-positioned foods"
- **Problem:** The abstract names two distinct goal states — fullness (satiety) goals and indulgence goals. "Feeling the urge to splurge" restates indulgence rather than fullness, so one of the two named conditions disappears.
- **Suggested fix:** "Replace "feeling the urge to splurge" with "fullness/satiety goals" to match the abstract's two conditions."

### Study 5: Rainbow washing worked — even on LGBTQIAPN+ consumers
**Work ID:** W7224213924 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Country not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Two sequential Brazilian experiments compared a rainbow-washing ad against a neutral ad"
- **Abstract says:** "A hypothetical-deductive approach was used through two sequential experiments, comparing two advertisements (characterization of rainbow washing × neutral): first, exploratory (n = 20) ... and second, explanatory (n = 74)"
- **Problem:** The abstract never states the country of data collection. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Drop "Brazilian" or note that the country is inferred and not stated in the abstract."

**Issue B. "Reverse" overstates the media-coverage effect** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "prepare for the effect to reverse once coverage lands"
- **Abstract says:** "However, monitoring negative media repercussions related to the brand can enhance consumers’ critical thinking, thus mitigating this influence."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports mitigation (dampening) of the rainbow-washing effect, not a reversal into backlash. Advising brands to expect a reversal goes beyond the data.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "prepare for the effect to be dampened once negative coverage lands"."

**Issue C. Media exposure described as a manipulation** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Exposure to negative media coverage about the brand restored some critical scrutiny and dampened the effect."
- **Abstract says:** "monitoring negative media repercussions related to the brand can enhance consumers’ critical thinking, thus mitigating this influence"
- **Problem:** The abstract's wording ("monitoring negative media repercussions") does not clearly indicate an experimental exposure manipulation; the digest presents it as one.
- **Suggested fix:** "Phrase as "awareness of negative media coverage about the brand was associated with more critical scrutiny, mitigating the effect"."

### Study 9: AR and VR ads moved the needle in Nepal — but only barely
**Work ID:** W7225266445 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Exposure described as researcher-administered** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Researchers surveyed 408 consumers aged 18 and over across five Nepalese cities after exposing them to real-world AR and VR brand content"
- **Abstract says:** "Data were collected from 408 consumers aged 18 years and above across five cities in Nepal who were exposed to real-world AR and VR brand marketing content."
- **Problem:** The abstract says participants had been exposed to real-world AR/VR content, not that the researchers administered the exposure; the digest implies a controlled exposure step in a design it elsewhere (correctly) calls cross-sectional.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rephrase as "surveyed 408 consumers ... who had been exposed to real-world AR and VR brand content"."

**Issue B. Interactivity condition not tested** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "this gives a sober benchmark suggesting the gains are real but modest unless the content is genuinely interactive and informative"
- **Abstract says:** "Marketers should focus on designing interactive and easy-to-use content that clearly demonstrates product benefits."
- **Problem:** Interactivity is an authors' recommendation, not a tested moderator; the digest implies the study shows gains are larger with interactive content.
- **Suggested fix:** "Attribute it as the authors' recommendation rather than a finding ("the authors recommend interactive, benefit-demonstrating content")."

### Study 10: ASMR ads may work least well on the people most sensitive to ASMR
**Work ID:** W7223005849 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Moderation restated as lower purchase likelihood** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "whether your most ASMR-responsive viewers are the ones least likely to buy"
- **Abstract says:** "ASMR responsiveness negatively moderated the relationship between affective mechanism and purchase intention, indicating attenuation rather than reversal of the positive effect."
- **Problem:** The finding is that the affect-to-intention link is weaker among highly ASMR-responsive consumers, not that they have lower purchase intention or are less likely to buy.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rephrase as "whether, for your most ASMR-responsive viewers, enjoyment translates less strongly into purchase intention"."

### Study 12: Retargeted ads work better when you don't explain how they work
**Work ID:** W7225262891 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Transparency effect overstated** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "But adding transparency about how the targeting worked triggered surveillance concerns, undercutting that helper perception."
- **Abstract says:** "However, if the system is transparent about its targeting mechanism, it triggers surveillance concerns."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports that transparency triggers surveillance concerns, but it does not state that transparency reduced the helper perception, lowered ad effectiveness or raised avoidance. The headline ("Retargeted ads work better when you don't explain how they work") and the consumer angle ("What flipped the feeling was being told how the tracking worked") assert an outcome the abstract does not report.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say transparency about the targeting mechanism triggered surveillance concerns, and note that the abstract does not report whether this translated into lower effectiveness or higher avoidance; soften the headline accordingly."

**Issue B. Study context not stated in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Single online experiment with simulated ads; sample size and recruitment source not stated in the abstract"
- **Abstract says:** "we conducted a 4 (retargeting persistence: non-retargeting, low persistence, medium persistence, and high persistence) × 2 (transparency: absence and presence) between-subjects experiment"
- **Problem:** The abstract does not say the experiment was conducted online or that the ads were simulated, nor that there was a single product context (the digest also asserts "one product/ad context").
- **Suggested fix:** "Mark the online/simulated-ad and single-context details as unverifiable from the abstract."

### Study 15: Why the smartest geofenced ad may not be drawn around your own store
**Work ID:** W7214312085 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Dropped modeling condition** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The model finds geofencing wins on three fronts — market expansion, higher margins and lower ad spend"
- **Abstract says:** "We first compare geofencing and traditional advertising under location-independent awareness (i.e., informed consumers are uniformly distributed along the city). We show that geofencing outperforms the alternative for three reasons, namely, market expansion, profit-margin enhancement, and cost savings."
- **Problem:** The three-way advantage over traditional advertising is established in the location-independent-awareness case; the abstract separately notes that awareness distribution significantly affects when geofencing is applicable and profitable. The digest states the result unconditionally in the study summary.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add that the three advantages hold in the baseline case where informed consumers are uniformly distributed, and that profitability changes once awareness varies by location."

### Study 17: Scaring young people about disease doesn't work; making health feel like a lifestyle does
**Work ID:** W7212126619 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal headline on survey data** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Scaring young people about disease doesn't work; making health feel like a lifestyle does"
- **Abstract says:** "A quantitative cross-sectional survey was conducted among 500 Indonesian Millennials and Generation Z respondents... Health consciousness emerged as the strongest predictor of diabetes prevention intention, followed by perceived susceptibility, while perceived severity showed no significant direct effect."
- **Problem:** The headline asserts what works and what does not, based on cross-sectional associations with stated prevention intention; perceived severity lacked a direct effect but was still enhanced by health literacy, and nothing in the study tested actual campaign exposure effects on behavior.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rephrase as an association, e.g. "Among young Indonesians, health consciousness predicted prevention intention — disease severity did not.""
