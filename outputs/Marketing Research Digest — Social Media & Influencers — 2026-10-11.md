### 1. On TikTok, a tight-knit audience beats a broad one — and posting more can erode the advantage

**Journal:** *European Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-09
**Work ID:** W7221178082 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ejm-01-2025-0027

**The study:** Researchers scraped publicly available TikTok data covering 278 target posts and 5,722 earlier posts from the same creators, using network metrics to score each influencer's "bonding" social capital (dense, close-knit ties with followers) versus "bridging" social capital (reach across diverse, loosely connected groups). Bonding capital predicted more contributing engagement (likes, comments, shares) and more creating engagement (followers making their own content in response), while bridging capital predicted less of both. High posting frequency dampened both effects — it weakened the payoff from bonding ties and softened the penalty attached to bridging ties.

**Why it matters:** Brands routinely select influencers on reach and audience breadth, but this data suggests that the depth of a creator's relationship with a relatively homogeneous following is what converts attention into active engagement.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** A reason to re-weight influencer selection criteria: test whether creators with dense, interconnected follower communities outperform broader-reach creators on engagement per post, and ask partners about their posting cadence, since volume appears to flatten whatever relational advantage they have. This is observational platform data, so treat it as a hypothesis to validate against your own campaign results rather than a selection rule.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It helps explain why a niche creator with a modest following can feel more influential than a celebrity account: engagement seems to follow intimacy and shared identity, not audience size — and a creator who floods your feed may be diluting exactly what made them compelling.

**Caveats:** Single platform (TikTok) and a relatively small set of target posts; observational, correlational data that cannot establish causation; social capital is inferred from online metrics rather than measured directly.

### 2. Draw more stages on the progress bar and people replace the product sooner

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing Research* | **Published:** 2026-10-09
**Work ID:** W7221366370 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00222437261498165

**The study:** Across ten studies, researchers tested how visualizations of a product's remaining life — the kind found on water filters, printer ink indicators and engine oil guides — shape when people decide to replace it. Splitting the same lifespan into more visually distinct stages raised replacement intentions even when actual usage was identical. The mechanism was mental simulation: more stages prompted consumers to replay past use, making the product feel further along its life. The effect vanished when stages were described non-visually or weren't visually distinct, and weakened when deterioration was directly observable.

**Why it matters:** A purely cosmetic design choice in a usage indicator can shift replacement timing — which means it can shift consumable revenue, and equally can be used to stretch replacement cycles and cut waste.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit every lifespan graphic you ship — filter indicators, app-based wear meters, maintenance guides — and treat segment count as a testable variable, not a design detail. The honest framing is two-sided: more stages can accelerate replacement where safety or performance demands it, fewer can reduce needless waste, and regulators may eventually notice which lever firms pull.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The next time a progress bar tells you your filter is "nearly done," notice how it's drawn. The same remaining life, chopped into more chunks, makes people feel they've used more than they have — which may be nudging households into buying replacements earlier than necessary.

**Caveats:** Primarily experimental studies measuring replacement intentions rather than observed purchase or replacement behavior; sample details and recruitment sources are not specified in the abstract.

### 3. After a death or divorce, household shopping baskets shift toward products that carry meaning

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing* | **Published:** 2026-10-01
**Work ID:** W7218361443 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretai.2026.09.005

**The study:** Using a longitudinal dataset of US household purchases spanning 998 fast-moving consumer goods sub-categories, researchers applied difference-in-differences models with propensity score matching to compare spending before and after major relationship disruptions — the death of a spouse or a divorce — against matched households. Spending rose modestly in categories high in eudaimonic value (products tied to meaning and purpose) and did not shift significantly toward utilitarian or hedonic categories. The effect was weaker when other family members remained in the household, consistent with a meaning-restoration explanation.

**Why it matters:** It identifies a life-event-driven, behaviorally observable shift in category demand, which has implications for category management, targeting and the ethics of marketing to people during periods of grief.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Life-event triggers are already used in targeting; this gives evidence on which categories actually move — meaning-oriented ones, not indulgence — and the effect sizes are modest. The sharper question for brand leaders is governance: how close do you want to get to bereavement and divorce signals in your data, and what would customers think if they knew?
- **For general readers and consumers:** A widely held intuition is that people comfort-shop after loss. This large purchase dataset suggests something more specific: households lean toward purchases that restore a sense of meaning rather than toward treats — and lean less when other family members are still in the home.

**Caveats:** Single country (US) and FMCG categories only; observational panel data with quasi-experimental controls rather than randomization; effects described as modest; value classification of 998 sub-categories involves researcher judgment.

### 4. 360-degree hotel videos make viewers more willing to post about the brand — especially with "touch this" prompts

**Journal:** *Australasian Marketing Journal (AMJ)* | **Published:** 2026-10-09
**Work ID:** W7221071870 | **DOI:** 10.1177/14413582261490946

**The study:** Across four studies in the luxury hospitality setting, researchers compared 360-degree videos with traditional video formats and measured intention to share word of mouth on social media. The immersive format outperformed standard video, working through a sequential chain: telepresence (feeling physically present in the space) leading to a sense of luxury experience. Adding haptic instructions — directions encouraging viewers to imagine touching surfaces — and higher hedonic value strengthened the effect via the luxury experience pathway.

**Why it matters:** It gives hospitality and premium brands a concrete content specification: immersive format plus explicit sensory prompts, rather than immersive format alone.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you are investing in 360-degree or immersive property content, the added instruction layer appears to matter as much as the format. Worth A/B testing haptic-style prompts in captions and voiceover against plain immersive footage — bearing in mind these are intention measures in controlled studies, not booking or share data from live campaigns.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Immersive video is doing more than showing you a room: feeling virtually "present" in a luxury space makes people more inclined to talk about it publicly, which is a reminder of how much marketing payoff comes from the audience's own posts.

**Caveats:** Measures social media word-of-mouth intention rather than actual sharing behavior; single sector (luxury hospitality); sample sizes, recruitment sources and country not reported in the abstract.

### 5. Beautiful or ugly, but never in between: the U-shaped curve of social media imagery

**Journal:** *Journal of Advertising* | **Published:** 2026-10-07
**Work ID:** W7220857638 | **DOI:** 10.1080/00913367.2026.2725646

**The study:** Researchers scored the visual aesthetic quality of 11,559 social media advertising images using a deep-learning model (reported 90.56% accuracy), then linked those scores to actual engagement with econometric analysis, and followed up with a randomized experiment using sampled stimuli. Engagement followed a U shape: both strikingly polished and conspicuously low-quality images outperformed middling ones. The experiment pointed to two different routes — high-quality visuals won on perceived attractiveness, low-quality ones on perceived credibility — and the pattern was stronger for informational than emotional ad appeals.

**Why it matters:** It challenges the default assumption that more production value always buys more engagement, and suggests the worst place for a brand's creative to sit is competent mediocrity.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit where your social creative actually falls on the polish spectrum: this evidence suggests mid-tier, adequately professional imagery may be the weakest performer, and that deliberately raw, phone-shot visuals can earn engagement through credibility rather than beauty. Worth A/B testing within your own categories before reallocating production budget, since the causal evidence comes from a controlled experiment rather than live campaigns.
- **For general readers and consumers:** That slightly scrappy brand post in your feed may not be a mistake — low-fi visuals read as more honest, which is exactly why some of them stop your scroll.

**Caveats:** Engagement data are correlational; the causal test is a randomized experiment with sampled stimuli rather than a live campaign. Platform and category coverage of the image dataset is not specified in the abstract, and aesthetic quality is machine-scored.

### 6. On review pages, "felt great in my hand" beats "good battery life"

**Journal:** *Marketing Intelligence & Planning* | **Published:** 2026-10-09
**Work ID:** W7221191923 | **DOI:** 10.1108/mip-12-2025-0950

**The study:** Researchers analyzed 8,119 online consumer reviews covering 12 smartphone models on BestBuy.com, comparing platform-generated review tags that describe product attributes with tags that describe the user's experience, using logistic and zero-inflated negative binomial models plus predictive checks. Experience-based tags were more strongly associated with a review being rated helpful than attribute-based tags. The benefit showed diminishing returns as more tags piled up, and was stronger for higher-involvement products.

**Why it matters:** Platforms increasingly auto-generate review tags, and this suggests the informational flavor of those tags — not just how many there are — shapes which reviews shoppers trust.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you run or depend on a review interface, test whether surfacing experience-oriented tags lifts helpfulness votes and conversion, and watch for tag overload: the data show returns flattening as tag counts rise. Also worth examining whether your automated tagging system is skewed toward spec language.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Shoppers say they want specs, but they reward reviews that tell them what owning the thing feels like — a useful reminder when you're writing your own review.

**Caveats:** Single retailer, single category (smartphones) and a single country's site; observational data on helpfulness votes rather than a controlled test or purchase outcomes.

### 7. If your ad takes a shot at a rival, tell a story rather than list facts

**Journal:** *International Journal of Advertising* | **Published:** 2026-10-10
**Work ID:** W7221340448 | **DOI:** 10.1080/02650487.2026.2740975

**The study:** Across one exploratory study and four experiments, researchers tested brand advertising that uses rivalry appeals — content positioned against a competitor — comparing storytelling versions with fact-based versions on YouTube and Instagram, using real video ads containing rivalry appeals as stimuli. Storytelling rivalry ads produced higher purchase intention than factual rivalry ads, with narrative transportation (getting absorbed in the story) as the mediating mechanism. Customer engagement did not act as an additional serial mediator.

**Why it matters:** Competitive, comparative advertising is common on social platforms; this suggests the execution format, not just the comparative claim, determines whether it moves purchase intent.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you are running competitor-referencing creative, test narrative formats against claim-led formats and measure absorption, not just likes and comments — the study found engagement metrics did not carry the effect through to intent. Treat this as a hypothesis to validate in live campaigns rather than a settled rule.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The ads that take a swipe at a rival work on you least when they argue and most when they pull you into a story — worth noticing the next time a brand picks a fight in your feed.

**Caveats:** Outcome is stated purchase intention, not actual purchase; experimental studies likely use online panel participants and exposure in a controlled setting; sample sizes and country are not specified in the abstract; limited to two platforms.

### 8. Virtual influencers look more real when they're photographed with friends

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-10-05
**Work ID:** W7220426162 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105135

**The study:** Researchers combined an Instagram dataset of 17,878 posts from 109 virtual influencers collected between 2023 and 2026 (8,558 non-video posts in the main analysis) with two controlled experiments. Group photos — images placing the computer-generated influencer alongside other people — drew significantly higher engagement than solo shots or person-free images. The experiments traced the effect to a chain of perceived social connectedness and then perceived authenticity, and found the advantage was strongest for human-like avatars and weaker for cartoon-style ones.

**Why it matters:** Authenticity is the central obstacle to virtual influencer marketing, and this suggests a cheap, purely compositional fix: put the avatar in company rather than alone.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands running or sponsoring CGI personas could A/B test group-composition content against solo portraits in their own feeds, and check whether the effect holds for cartoon-styled mascots, where the field and lab evidence suggests it fades.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A reminder of how easily social cues hack our instincts: seeing a digital character surrounded by other people makes audiences judge it as more genuine, even knowing it isn't a person at all.

**Caveats:** Engagement study is correlational Instagram data, not randomized; the two supporting experiments rely on post evaluations rather than purchases; single platform.

### 9. Chatbots that type word by word win more trust than ones that dump whole lines

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Behaviour* | **Published:** 2026-10-04
**Work ID:** W7219615860 | **DOI:** 10.1002/cb.70256

**The study:** Three experiments spanning everyday question-answering, online shopping, and crowdfunding tested whether generative AI responses that stream word by word (high dynamism) versus line by line (low dynamism) change users' willingness to accept the output. The dynamic, word-by-word format increased acceptance, and the effect ran through users' feelings of immersion. The advantage shrank when users had a strong situational need for understanding — when accuracy and diagnosticity mattered, the delivery style counted for less.

**Why it matters:** The visual pacing of an AI's answer, a pure interface choice unrelated to content quality, measurably shifts whether people take its advice.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Teams shipping AI assistants in commerce or service contexts could test streaming speed and granularity as a design variable — while also asking the uncomfortable question of whether a persuasion effect untethered from answer quality is one they want to exploit.
- **For general readers and consumers:** That hypnotic typing effect in your chatbot isn't a technical necessity — it's a design choice that makes you more likely to believe what you're reading.

**Caveats:** Experimental studies measuring stated willingness to accept output rather than real decisions; sample details and country not specified in the abstract; short exposure.

### 10. Tell people how the chicken was raised and the identical meat tastes better

**Journal:** *Food Quality and Preference* | **Published:** 2026-10-03
**Work ID:** W7218807019 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.foodqual.2026.106136

**The study:** In a within-subject tasting study, 657 consumers evaluated two identical samples of chicken meat — one presented with information about a novel dual-purpose organic production system, the other with basic origin information only. The sample with production-system information scored higher on overall liking, taste liking, and texture liking, despite being the same meat. Half of participants preferred the informed sample, 24% preferred the uninformed one, and 26% had no preference; notably, when asked about grocery shopping values in general, price ranked first, ahead of animal welfare and taste.

**Why it matters:** Credence claims about how food is produced don't just justify a price premium — they physically alter the eating experience consumers report, which matters for launching unfamiliar sustainable production systems.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Food brands introducing alternative production methods should treat on-pack and point-of-sale storytelling as part of the product's sensory profile, not just a trust signal — but note the gap between what shoppers say they value in a tasting booth and the price-first priorities they report for actual shopping.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Your palate is suggestible: the same chicken genuinely tasted better to people who'd just read about outdoor access and minimal waste.

**Caveats:** Single country/category; within-subject design means participants compared two samples side by side; liking measured in a test setting rather than at home; stated shopping values are self-reported.

### 11. "Delayed a week" annoys customers more than "delayed seven days"

**Journal:** *Journal of Services Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-01
**Work ID:** W7215115595 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jsm-10-2025-0767

**The study:** Three experiments examined how the unit used to describe a service delay changes consumer reactions. Failures described in contracted temporal units ("a week") produced less favorable company evaluations and lower repurchase intentions than the mathematically identical expanded framing ("seven days"). The authors attribute the difference to how unit framing shapes the perceived magnitude of the delay.

**Why it matters:** Delay notifications are written by the thousands in logistics, travel, and service recovery, and this points to a free lever in the wording.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Customer service and ops teams could run a live test on delay-notification copy, comparing expanded versus contracted time units in real shipping or appointment messages before assuming the lab effect survives contact with frustrated customers.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Numbers aren't neutral. The way a company counts your wait — in days versus weeks — nudges how long and how bad it feels, even when nothing about the delay has changed.

**Caveats:** Lab/online experiments with hypothetical service failure scenarios and stated intentions, not observed behavior; sample size and composition not reported in the abstract; effect size and durability untested in the field.

### 12. A bigger bill makes patients expect a more painful procedure

**Journal:** *Psychology and Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-05
**Work ID:** W7220360473 | **DOI:** 10.1002/mar.70261

**The study:** Three experiments tested whether the "pain of paying" spills into expectations of physical pain in healthcare. Higher out-of-pocket costs raised anticipated physical pain from a medical procedure through a sequence of pain of paying and financial anxiety, and in a second study that discomfort reduced stated willingness to schedule the procedure. A third study found the effect on scheduling intentions was strongest among consumers already under general financial stress.

**Why it matters:** How providers and insurers present costs may be shaping not just whether patients book care but how much they expect it to hurt — with the heaviest effect on financially vulnerable people.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Healthcare marketers and provider communications teams should scrutinize when and how cost figures appear in patient-facing materials, and test whether sequencing or framing price disclosure differently changes booking rates without hiding information patients need.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Money worry doesn't stay in its lane: reading a high price tag for a procedure can make people brace for more physical pain and put off care they need.

**Caveats:** Experimental studies using hypothetical procedures and stated scheduling intentions rather than real medical decisions; US context; sample details not given in the abstract.

### 13. When a product goes wrong, who should apologize depends on the country

**Journal:** *Journal of Product & Brand Management* | **Published:** 2026-10-06
**Work ID:** W7220681469 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jpbm-09-2025-6347

**The study:** Two studies compared consumer reactions to low-severity product-harm crisis messages delivered by a corporate voice versus a social media influencer. In the first, US and South Korean consumers responded differently: in the higher power-distance context, corporate-led recovery messages produced significantly greater satisfaction and purchase intention than influencer-led ones, while in the lower power-distance context the two sources performed about the same. A second study with US consumers measured power distance orientation at the individual level and found the same moderating pattern.

**Why it matters:** Brands increasingly lean on creator partners for communications, but this suggests the messenger who builds affinity in good times may be the wrong one when something breaks.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Build messenger selection into crisis playbooks by market and audience segment rather than defaulting to whoever has the biggest following — and treat this as a hypothesis to validate with your own customers, since it was tested on one product category and only minor failures.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Who you want to hear from after a product problem says something about your assumptions regarding authority — some people want the company itself to answer, others are satisfied with a trusted creator.

**Caveats:** Scenario-based experiments with stated satisfaction and purchase intention, not real crisis behavior; limited to low-severity crises and a single product category; two countries only; sample sizes not reported in the abstract.

### 14. Why "ugly-cute" sells: the Labubu effect, tested

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-10-05
**Work ID:** W7220433704 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105139

**The study:** Researchers ran one field experiment and three online experiments with consumers in China and the United States to test how ugliness and cuteness interact in product design, using the ugly-cute aesthetic popularized by Pop Mart's LABUBU dolls. Normally, products that deviate from conventional beauty suffer a purchase-intention penalty, but pairing ugliness with cuteness largely cancels that penalty. The mechanism was perceived social value — the sense that owning the item wins approval and acceptance from others — and the effect was strongest when the product would be consumed or displayed publicly rather than privately.

**Why it matters:** It gives design and product teams a concrete reason to stop sanding down odd-looking concepts: strangeness plus cuteness can convert an aesthetic liability into social currency, particularly for items people carry, wear or post.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Test whether "deviant but endearing" design variants outperform conventionally attractive ones in your category — and note the boundary condition: the advantage showed up in public-facing consumption, so bag charms, phone cases and apparel are better candidates than products used at home. These are experiments, so treat the result as a hypothesis worth running in-market, not a guaranteed lift.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If you cannot explain why you want the weird little monster doll, this is the explanation: the research suggests much of the appeal is not private delight but the social approval that comes from being seen with something odd-but-cute.

**Caveats:** Three of the four studies were online experiments measuring purchase intention rather than purchases; two countries only (China and the US); the effect is tied to a specific trend-driven aesthetic that may not generalize beyond collectibles and accessories.

### 15. In luxury stores, "cool" sells — but "high status" backfires

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-10-03
**Work ID:** W7218728341 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105130

**The study:** Researchers interviewed 16 frequent luxury-store visitors, then surveyed 213 shoppers immediately after they left luxury fashion stores to test which dimensions of "store coolness" convert service, display and store creativity into repeat-visit intentions and positive word of mouth. The extraordinary and subcultural dimensions of coolness carried the positive effects of display and creativity; the iconic dimension did nothing; and the high-status dimension actually mediated negatively, dragging down intentions and word of mouth. Price-conscious shoppers also got less out of store creativity.

**Why it matters:** Luxury retailers spend heavily to signal exclusivity and status in-store, but this suggests status signaling can suppress the advocacy that creative, unexpected environments generate.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit what your flagship store is actually signaling: the evidence points toward investing in the surprising and subculturally coded elements of a space rather than overt status cues, and toward checking whether your most price-aware visitors are getting anything from the creative spend at all.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Shoppers apparently reward luxury stores that feel original or scene-adjacent, and quietly penalize those that feel mainly like a display of expensive superiority — a hint that status alone is losing its pull as a retail atmosphere.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey of 213 shoppers measuring intentions and stated word of mouth rather than observed behavior, so causation cannot be established; single category (luxury fashion) and a limited set of stores; mediation findings rest on a structural model.

### 16. When you're in pain, you follow the crowd

**Journal:** *Psychology and Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-29
**Work ID:** W7212201010 | **DOI:** 10.1002/mar.70264

**The study:** Across a pilot field study, a laboratory experiment and two preregistered online experiments, researchers tested whether physical pain changes how much people lean on social proof when choosing. Participants experiencing pain were consistently more likely to pick the majority-endorsed option — and did so even in a setup where the popular choice was objectively the worse one.

**Why it matters:** Physical discomfort is extremely common — headaches, sore backs, period pain, post-workout aches — and this suggests it quietly shifts consumers toward "what everyone else picked" rather than what suits them best.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Social-proof cues ("bestseller," "most popular," star-rating counts) may be disproportionately persuasive in contexts where customers are physically uncomfortable — pharmacy aisles, urgent-care and telehealth flows, dental or clinic waiting rooms, gyms. Worth testing whether the same creative performs differently in those contexts, and worth an internal ethics check before leaning on it.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A useful self-defense rule: when you're hurting, your judgment is more likely to default to the herd. Big or unfamiliar decisions are better postponed until the headache passes.

**Caveats:** Mix of lab and online-panel experiments with choices that are largely hypothetical; the authors explicitly leave the underlying mechanism unresolved; field component is only a pilot.

### 17. "Organic" plus "ancient grain" raised expectations — then the crackers disappointed

**Journal:** *Food Quality and Preference* | **Published:** 2026-10-02
**Work ID:** W7215857329 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.foodqual.2026.106132

**The study:** In an Italian sensory study, 118 consumers evaluated organic crackers made from an ancient wheat (Verna) and a modern one (Altavista) under three conditions: blind tasting, label information only, and tasting plus information. Attitudes were captured both by conventional scales and by an indirect text-highlighting method. Combining the organic label with an ancient-wheat claim lifted expectations for liking, healthiness, sustainability, naturalness and novelty above the organic label alone — but after tasting, liking did not move toward those raised expectations.

**Why it matters:** Stacking virtuous claims on a pack can inflate expectations the product itself cannot meet, which risks disappointment and non-repeat rather than loyalty.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Before piling on heritage, organic and sustainability cues, run a blind-versus-informed taste test: if the sensory experience does not deliver against the expectation the label creates, extra claims may be buying trial at the cost of repeat purchase. Also note the measurement lesson — explicit attitude scales and the indirect highlighting method produced completely different consumer segments, so segmentations built only on self-reported values may not reflect what shoppers actually prioritize in the aisle.
- **For general readers and consumers:** "Ancient grain" and "organic" on the same pack measurably change how good you expect food to taste before you've tried a bite — and this study suggests the taste itself often doesn't catch up.

**Caveats:** Single country, single product category (crackers), 118 participants in a within-subjects lab setting rather than a real purchase; no measurement of repeat buying.

### 18. Shoppers won't talk to Alexa about big purchases — until they try it once

**Journal:** *International Journal of Retail & Distribution Management* | **Published:** 2026-10-05
**Work ID:** W7220476960 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ijrdm-09-2025-0741

**The study:** Researchers ran an 8-week self-report diary study with 23 consumers shopping for eight different high-involvement products via voice commerce. The diaries revealed a self-reinforcing loop: direct experience built trust and attitude, which raised intention and actual use, with "learning by doing" and a sense of immersive shopping emerging as the main drivers. Technophilic orientation and regulatory focus moderated how quickly people warmed to the channel.

**Why it matters:** Voice commerce has been stuck in low-involvement replenishment; this suggests the barrier is lack of experience rather than a hard ceiling on what people will buy by voice.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If the constraint is experiential rather than structural, retailers could test guided first-use moments — a voice-assisted configuration or comparison task on a considered purchase — to seed the trust loop, instead of writing voice off as a reorder channel.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Why people trust voice assistants with laundry detergent but not a laptop: comfort appears to be built through repetition, and the first successful voice purchase changes what you're willing to do next.

**Caveats:** Qualitative diary study with only 23 participants, no control group, self-reported; findings are exploratory and cannot be generalized to the wider market.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- **Reach is the wrong selection metric.** "On TikTok, a tight-knit audience beats a broad one" (W7221178082) finds dense bonding ties beat broad bridging reach on engagement, extending the previous digest's "For luxury brands, a smaller influencer may protect the aura of exclusivity" (W7214325049) and the meta-analytic warning in "Meta-analysis of 97 studies: looks drive engagement, expertise drives purchase" (W7213954552). Two runs now point the same way: follower count is a weak, sometimes negative, proxy for influence.
- **Engagement metrics keep failing as a bridge to intent.** "If your ad takes a shot at a rival, tell a story rather than list facts" (W7221340448) reports that customer engagement did *not* serially mediate the path to purchase intention — the same null the previous digest's meta-analysis (W7213954552) found for credibility-through-engagement.
- **Narrative beats claim-led formats, again.** W7221340448 shows storytelling rivalry ads outperform fact-based ones via narrative transportation, converging with "Sponsorship labels hurt 'here are the specs' influencer posts more than stories" (W7212040284) and with narrative transportation as the mechanism in "In influencer persuasion, the voice may matter more than the face" (W7214151150).
- **States and contexts push people toward the majority option.** "When you're in pain, you follow the crowd" (W7212201010) parallels "Creepy targeting doesn't just annoy people — it makes them want to blend into the crowd" (W7214349274): two different aversive states, same conformity outcome, including when the popular choice is worse.
- **Culture keeps moderating messenger and channel effects.** "When a product goes wrong, who should apologize depends on the country" (W7220681469) adds power distance to the previous digest's collectivism and privacy-culture moderators in "Fast-moving brands on social media earn more word of mouth" (W7213466946) and "Personalized ads make people like you — and talk about you badly anyway" (W7214160679).

**Advances**
- **Virtual influencers get a fix, not just a diagnosis.** The previous digest established the problem — "Some people are just wired to bond with influencers — and virtual ones can't cash in" (W7213316341). "Virtual influencers look more real when they're photographed with friends" (W7220426162) supplies a cheap compositional remedy working through social connectedness and perceived authenticity.
- **Crisis response moves from *what you say* to *who says it*.** Previous-run work covered the excuse ("'The AI did it' is a risky excuse," W7214331895) and the complainant's tone ("When a customer swears at your brand online," W7214100498). W7220681469 adds messenger choice — corporate voice versus creator — as a third contingency in the same playbook.
- **From visual consistency to visual quality.** "The hidden variable in influencer campaigns: how much the pictures look alike" (W7172486915) established campaign-level visual coherence as a lever; "Beautiful or ugly, but never in between" (W7220857638) adds production value itself as a U-shaped lever, with credibility as the route for low-fi work.
- **AI delivery style as a persuasion variable.** "Chatbots that type word by word win more trust" (W7219615860) extends "Let the robot be casual and the human be formal" (W7213543402) from tone to pacing — in both cases a non-content interface choice shifts acceptance.

**Counterpoints**
- **Anthropomorphism cuts both ways.** W7213316341 found that making a virtual influencer *more* humanlike canceled the trust benefit for parasocially inclined viewers; W7220426162 finds the group-photo authenticity gain is *strongest* for human-like avatars and weakest for cartoon styles. Human likeness appears to help composition-based authenticity cues while hurting dispositional parasocial transfer — a real tension worth flagging in any virtual-influencer story.
- **Information about provenance can lift or deflate experience.** "Telling people an environmental image was AI-generated drains the guilt out of it" (W7213456793) and the new "'Organic' plus 'ancient grain' raised expectations — then the crackers disappointed" (W7215857329) both show disclosure/claim effects that undercut the brand's intent, while "Tell people how the chicken was raised and the identical meat tastes better" (W7218807019) shows the opposite. Claim stacking inflates expectations; a single credible production story improves the experience itself.

**New themes**
- The new batch moves well outside creator marketing into interface and packaging design ("Draw more stages on the progress bar," W7221366370; "'Delayed a week' annoys customers more than 'delayed seven days,'" W7215115595), physical retail atmospherics ("In luxury stores, 'cool' sells — but 'high status' backfires," W7218728341), healthcare cost framing ("A bigger bill makes patients expect a more painful procedure," W7220360473), life-event demand shifts ("After a death or divorce," W7218361443) and voice commerce ("Shoppers won't talk to Alexa about big purchases — until they try it once," W7220476960). None of these had any counterpart in the previous digest.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** A large share of this batch finds that purely cosmetic presentation choices — things with no bearing on what is actually being offered — measurably move money and trust. Splitting a lifespan indicator into more segments makes people replace a product sooner ("Draw more stages on the progress bar," W7221366370); describing a delay as "a week" rather than "seven days" damages brand evaluations more ("'Delayed a week' annoys customers more," W7215115595); streaming an AI answer word by word rather than line by line raises acceptance of it ("Chatbots that type word by word win more trust," W7219615860); mid-polish imagery underperforms both glossy and scrappy visuals ("Beautiful or ugly, but never in between," W7220857638); putting a CGI avatar in a group photo makes it read as authentic (W7220426162); and adding "imagine touching this" prompts to immersive video raises willingness to post about a brand (W7221071870). Add the claim-stacking and tasting studies (W7218807019, W7215857329) and the picture is consistent: form is doing persuasive work untethered from substance.

**Why pitch this now:** Dark-pattern enforcement is live — the EU is consulting on a Digital Fairness Act aimed squarely at manipulative interface design, and US regulators have been pursuing deceptive design and subscription friction — while generative AI makes it trivially cheap to A/B test the cosmetic layer of every notification, progress bar and chat window at scale.

**Angle:** A reported feature on the thin line between good design and quiet manipulation, using the new experimental evidence to show which "neutral" presentation choices are actually levers, who inside companies is pulling them, and how a rule written for pop-ups and cancel buttons would cope with a progress bar that just has more tick marks on it. Written for a business and policy-literate audience, with enough consumer self-defense material to work for general readers.

**Potential headlines:**
- The progress bar, the typing chatbot and the word "week": small design choices that change what people buy
- Nobody lied to you, and you still bought the filter three weeks early
- Your appliance decides when it is worn out, and a designer decides how worn out it looks

**Potential outlets:**
- **Marketing Brew** — already covers creative and UX decisions as commercial levers, and the progress-bar plus chatbot-pacing findings give it a concrete, newsroom-friendly spine.
- **Retail Dive** — the replacement-cycle and delay-notification results land directly on consumables revenue, logistics comms and the retail design teams its readers run.
- **Fast Company** — a natural home for the design-ethics framing, pairing the U-shaped polish finding with the question of when interface craft becomes a nudge.
- **Vox** — its consumer-behavior and money coverage would carry the "you were steered without being deceived" argument to general readers, with the pricing-and-pain and tasting studies as accessible examples.

## Story Ideas by Study

- **On TikTok, a tight-knit audience beats a broad one (Work ID W7221178082):** Hard data arguing brands should screen creators on community density and posting cadence rather than reach — and that prolific posters dilute their own advantage, for a CMO-audience column or influencer-marketing trade explainer.
- **Draw more stages on the progress bar (Work ID W7221366370):** Ten studies showing a cosmetic change to a wear indicator shifts replacement timing, framed as both a revenue lever and a waste lever, for a product-design or sustainability-business feature.
- **After a death or divorce, household shopping baskets shift (Work ID W7218361443):** Real panel data debunking the comfort-shopping cliché — grieving households buy for meaning, not treats — plus the governance question of targeting bereavement signals, for a consumer-psychology explainer in a general-interest outlet or a data-ethics piece in trade press.
- **360-degree hotel videos make viewers more willing to post (Work ID W7221071870):** The instruction layer matters as much as the format — "imagine touching this" prompts outperform immersive footage alone, for a hospitality or travel-marketing trade brief.
- **Beautiful or ugly, but never in between (Work ID W7220857638):** Nearly 12,000 images showing competent mediocrity is the worst place for creative to sit, with credibility, not beauty, driving low-fi performance, for an advertising creative-strategy section.
- **On review pages, "felt great in my hand" beats "good battery life" (Work ID W7221191923):** Auto-generated review tags skew toward specs, but shoppers reward experience language — and tag overload has diminishing returns, for a retail or e-commerce trade explainer.
- **If your ad takes a shot at a rival, tell a story (Work ID W7221340448):** Comparative advertising works through absorption, not argument, and engagement metrics do not carry the effect to intent, for a brand-strategy column.
- **Virtual influencers look more real when they're photographed with friends (Work ID W7220426162):** A nearly free compositional fix for the authenticity problem that has dogged CGI personas — and it fades for cartoon avatars, for a marketing-trade news brief or an AI-in-advertising explainer.
- **Chatbots that type word by word win more trust (Work ID W7219615860):** The streaming typing effect is a persuasion design choice, not a technical constraint, and it works independent of answer quality, for a tech-business piece or a consumer-tech explainer in a general-interest outlet.
- **Tell people how the chicken was raised and the identical meat tastes better (Work ID W7218807019):** Production-story labeling changes reported taste, not just willingness to pay — with the catch that the same shoppers rank price first, for a food-industry trade feature or a food-science column.
- **"Delayed a week" annoys customers more than "delayed seven days" (Work ID W7215115595):** A free wording lever in delay notifications that logistics, travel and service teams send by the million, for a customer-experience trade brief.
- **A bigger bill makes patients expect a more painful procedure (Work ID W7220360473):** Price disclosure raises anticipated physical pain and suppresses booking, hitting financially stressed patients hardest — a live issue amid healthcare price-transparency rules, for a health-business outlet or a consumer-affairs column.
- **When a product goes wrong, who should apologize depends on the country (Work ID W7220681469):** Creators build affinity but may be the wrong messenger in a recall, with power distance determining which voice works, for a PR or crisis-communications trade piece.
- **Why "ugly-cute" sells: the Labubu effect, tested (Work ID W7220433704):** Experimental evidence that cuteness cancels the beauty penalty through social value — and only for products consumed in public, for a retail trade feature or a general-interest piece on the collectibles boom.
- **In luxury stores, "cool" sells — but "high status" backfires (Work ID W7218728341):** Overt status signaling in flagship stores suppresses the advocacy that creative, subculturally coded spaces generate, for a luxury-retail or store-design trade feature.
- **When you're in pain, you follow the crowd (Work ID W7212201010):** Physical discomfort makes shoppers defer to "bestseller" cues even when the popular choice is worse — relevant to pharmacy aisles, telehealth flows and waiting rooms, for a consumer-psychology explainer or a personal-finance self-defense column.
- **"Organic" plus "ancient grain" raised expectations — then the crackers disappointed (Work ID W7215857329):** Claim stacking can buy trial at the cost of repeat, plus a sharp measurement lesson that explicit and indirect attitude methods yield different segments, for a food and beverage marketing trade piece.
- **Shoppers won't talk to Alexa about big purchases — until they try it once (Work ID W7220476960):** Small and exploratory, but a usable argument that voice commerce's ceiling is experiential rather than structural, worth a short retail-tech trade item rather than a standalone feature.
