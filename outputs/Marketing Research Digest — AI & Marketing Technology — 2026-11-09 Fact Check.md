### Study 3: Empathetic chatbot language only pays off if customers know it's a bot — and think it's competent
**Work ID:** W7225314486 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Schema activation framed as disclosure/knowing it's a bot** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Empathetic chatbot language only pays off if customers know it's a bot — and think it's competent"
- **Abstract says:** "anthropomorphic language and chatbot schema (a customer's cognitive framework for understanding and interacting with a chatbot) jointly influence customer electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM)"
- **Problem:** An active chatbot schema is a cognitive frame, not necessarily explicit disclosure that the agent is a bot. The abstract does mention implications for "chatbot disclosure," but the headline treats schema activation as equivalent to the customer knowing they are talking to a bot.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "only pays off when customers are already framing the interaction as a chatbot interaction" or note that the authors link this to disclosure as an implication rather than a manipulation."

**Issue B. Direction of the competence contingency specified beyond the abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Study 2 found the effects on perceived authenticity and eWOM depended on the chatbot's competence: warm language appears to help only when the bot actually handles the request well."
- **Abstract says:** "Study 2 demonstrates that the effects of anthropomorphic language on authenticity and eWOM are contingent on the chatbot's competence."
- **Problem:** The abstract states contingency but does not specify the shape or direction (help only under high competence vs. backfire under low competence). The digest hedges with "appears," but still supplies a direction the abstract doesn't give.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say the effects were contingent on competence without asserting which side of the contingency drove the result."

**Issue C. eWOM measured as stated intent** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "measures outcomes as stated word-of-mouth intent rather than observed sharing behavior"
- **Abstract says:** "Across two experiments, we examine how anthropomorphic language (such as empathetic language and emojis) impacts customers' engagement with chatbots."
- **Problem:** The abstract does not specify whether eWOM was measured as intention or behavior; the caveat, while conservative, asserts a detail not verifiable from the abstract alone.
- **Suggested fix:** "Note that the abstract does not specify how eWOM was operationalized."

### Study 8: Service robots win customers over by feeling present — not by looking human
**Work ID:** W7224957158 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Moderation read as a main negative effect** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "people seem to warm to a robot that acts attentive but stays visibly a robot, and cool toward one that tries too hard to pass as a person"
- **Abstract says:** "robot anthropomorphism negatively moderates the relationship between social presence and satisfaction"
- **Problem:** A negative moderation means the social presence–satisfaction link is weaker when anthropomorphism is high; it does not establish that customers are less satisfied with (i.e., "cool toward") more humanlike robots overall.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say that the satisfaction benefit of social presence shrinks as the robot looks more humanlike, rather than implying customers dislike humanlike robots."

**Issue B. Anthropomorphism described as manipulated appearance** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the more human-like the robot was made to look, the weaker the link between social presence and satisfaction"
- **Abstract says:** "robot anthropomorphism negatively moderates the relationship between social presence and satisfaction"
- **Problem:** The abstract does not say anthropomorphism was experimentally manipulated or limited to visual appearance; it could be a measured perception.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "the more human-like the robot was (perceived to be)" and note the abstract doesn't specify whether this was manipulated or measured."

### Study 9: The travelers most comfortable with AI are the ones who trust its itineraries least
**Work ID:** W7224113183 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Headline misstates the technology readiness effect** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "The travelers most comfortable with AI are the ones who trust its itineraries least"
- **Abstract says:** "Technology readiness reversed the expected pattern: readier travelers scrutinized fluent output rather than relying on it, weakening the behavioral effect of trust."
- **Problem:** The finding is that readiness weakens the translation of trust into continued usage, not that tech-ready travelers hold less trust. The headline tells readers something the abstract does not report.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as "The travelers most comfortable with AI are the ones whose trust translates least into reliance on it.""

**Issue B. Self-reported usage described as actual usage** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "weakening the link between trust and actual continued usage"
- **Abstract says:** "weakening the behavioral effect of trust"
- **Problem:** Continued usage here comes from a self-report panel, as the digest's own caveat acknowledges; the body text's "actual" contradicts that caveat.
- **Suggested fix:** "Drop "actual" and say "self-reported continued usage.""

### Study 10: When AI explains its recommendations, more reasons aren't better — a moderate dose wins
**Work ID:** W7225383427 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Mediators attributed to the inverted-U experiment** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "A randomized mobile-retail experiment in China found an inverted-U pattern: trust, perceived usefulness and decision comfort rose with more explanation up to a point, then fell as information overload and what the authors call "algorithmic over-justification" took over."
- **Abstract says:** "Study 1 identifies an inverted-U response in a randomized Chinese mobile-retail experiment... Study 2 reports conditional positive indirect associations through perceived usefulness, trust in AI, and decision comfort, and conditional negative indirect associations through information overload and algorithmic over-justification."
- **Problem:** The mediators belong to Study 2's conditional indirect associations, not to Study 1's inverted-U result; combining them implies the mediating pathways were themselves shown to be curvilinear in the randomized experiment.
- **Suggested fix:** "Separate the two: report the inverted-U from Study 1, then note Study 2's conditional positive and negative indirect pathways."

**Issue B. Companion study described one-sidedly** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "A companion study showed the same messages attributed to a human salesperson rather than an AI"
- **Abstract says:** "Study S1 compares identical messages attributed to an AI assistant or a human salesperson, with separate source-neutral measurement validation."
- **Problem:** It was a comparison of AI versus human attribution, not a re-run with human attribution only; the digest's phrasing also stops short of saying what the comparison found (the caveat later implies similarity).
- **Suggested fix:** "Say the companion study compared identical messages attributed to an AI assistant versus a human salesperson."

### Study 11: What banking app reviews actually reveal: speed and security complaints hurt more than good design helps
**Work ID:** W7224875273 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Dropped nuance in sentiment moderation** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The quality–satisfaction link was asymmetric and moderated by review sentiment — under positive sentiment, negative quality issues bit harder."
- **Abstract says:** "Sentiment polarity moderates these associations: under positive sentiment, negative quality effects intensify, while the moderation of positive effects varies across dimensions."
- **Problem:** The digest reports only half the moderation result, omitting that the moderation of positive quality effects varies by dimension — which makes the asymmetry look cleaner than the abstract claims.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add: 'while the moderation of the positive dimensions varied from one quality dimension to another.'"

**Issue B. Review sentiment recast as user satisfaction** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the same defect costs more among otherwise satisfied users"
- **Abstract says:** "Under positive sentiment, negatively associated quality dimensions exert stronger adverse effects on satisfaction"
- **Problem:** The moderator is the sentiment polarity of the review text, not a measure of whether the reviewer is an 'otherwise satisfied user'; the gloss attributes a user-level trait to a text-level variable.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to 'the same defect weighs more heavily in otherwise positively worded reviews.'"

**Issue C. Bank type described as unspecified** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Worth testing whether the same asymmetry holds in your own review data across digital-only versus branch-based brands."
- **Abstract says:** "demonstrating how sentiment polarity moderates the relationship between perceived software quality dimensions and user ratings across both fully digital and hybrid digital–physical banks"
- **Problem:** The abstract states the analysis already spanned fully digital and hybrid digital–physical banks, so presenting that comparison as an open question (and the caveat that app categories 'are not specified in detail') understates what the paper reports.
- **Suggested fix:** "Note that the study already covered both digital-only and branch-based banks, and frame the angle as replicating rather than first testing that split."

### Study 12: Don't target the customers most likely to leave — target the ones your offer can actually change

### Study 13: Telling people your influencer is AI makes them like the post less — and buy less
**Work ID:** W7225100007 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline implies behavior, not intention** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Telling people your influencer is AI makes them like the post less — and buy less"
- **Abstract says:** "In Study 2, this serial process extended to lower purchase intentions."
- **Problem:** 'Buy less' reads as actual purchasing; only stated purchase intentions were measured. The body and caveats are correct, so the mismatch is confined to the headline.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to '...and say they'll buy less' or '...and lowers purchase intentions'."

**Issue B. Untested design recommendation** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "overtly stylized or non-human-presenting avatars may avoid the authenticity penalty altogether"
- **Abstract says:** "Two between-subjects online experiments with US non-followers (n = 303 and n = 316) ... tests whether disclosure that a seemingly human influencer is AI-generated lowers perceived authenticity"
- **Problem:** Only human-presenting virtual influencers were studied; the stylized-avatar alternative is an extrapolation. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Label it explicitly as a hypothesis the study does not test."

### Study 14: Plain-language data policies and outside audits both lifted willingness to adopt smart energy devices
**Work ID:** W7225315132 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Named moderators omitted** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Both richer disclosure and external assurance independently raised all four focal outcomes, including adoption intention and willingness to join a pilot, with no interaction between them; survey measures showed trust in data handling and perceived legitimacy of using household data for climate purposes as the connecting perceptions."
- **Abstract says:** "Digital literacy strengthened two of the three proposed governance‐cue relationships; institutional trust strengthened the provider‐credibility relationship, and environmental concern strengthened the legitimacy–adoption relationships."
- **Problem:** The abstract names three boundary conditions on the trust and legitimacy pathways; the digest drops all of them, presenting the mechanism as uniform across consumers.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add a sentence noting the pathways were stronger among more digitally literate, institutionally trusting and environmentally concerned respondents."

**Issue B. Overweights external assurance** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "this study suggests people are willing to make that trade, but mainly when someone independent has checked the company's homework"
- **Abstract says:** "Randomized factorial tests showed positive main effects of richer governance disclosure and external assurance on all four focal outcomes, with no significant interaction."
- **Problem:** Both cues had independent main effects of comparable standing in the abstract; 'mainly when someone independent has checked' implies external assurance is the dominant or necessary condition.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to note that clearer disclosure and independent assurance each helped on their own."
