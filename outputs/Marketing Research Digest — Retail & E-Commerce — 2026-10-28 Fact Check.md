### Study 1: Mobile coupons work harder before shoppers reach the mall than inside it
**Work ID:** W7223707755 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Unsupported claim about coupon value** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "which is also why the ones you receive on the way can be the more generous"
- **Abstract says:** "The abstract reports only that outside-mall coupon clicks are associated with a stronger purchase response for experiential-product stores, and models whether and where to issue coupons; it says nothing about the size or generosity of outside-targeted offers."
- **Problem:** Adds a specific consumer-facing claim (pre-arrival coupons are more generous) that the abstract does not test or report.
- **Suggested fix:** "Drop the generosity claim or reword to 'the first are designed to get you to come at all, rather than just to close a sale you were already going to make.'"

### Study 2: The shoppers who use cross-border platforms most are the ones who most oppose regulating them
**Work ID:** W7223071223 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline implies a usage gradient** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The shoppers who use cross-border platforms most are the ones who most oppose regulating them"
- **Abstract says:** ""direct purchase experience is associated with approximately 9–11 percentage points higher regulatory opposition""
- **Problem:** The abstract reports a comparison between consumers with and without direct purchase experience, not a dose-response relationship between intensity of use and intensity of opposition.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to 'Shoppers who have actually bought from cross-border platforms are the most likely to oppose regulating them.'"

**Issue B. 'Most organized opposition' overstates the data** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "the heaviest users of a channel form the most organized opposition to restricting it, which helps explain how a safety-framed measure can collapse in days"
- **Abstract says:** "The study measures survey-reported opposition among adults with and without direct purchase experience; it describes the 2024 initiative as "substantially revised within days amid consumer opposition" and reports associations, not collective action or organization."
- **Problem:** Converts an attitudinal association into a claim about organized political mobilization and about the mechanism by which the policy was withdrawn, neither of which the study tested.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as 'consumers with the most direct experience of a channel are the most likely to oppose restricting it, which may help explain the speed of the backlash.'"

### Study 3: Shoppers Can't Measure Air Quality — But Their Noses Decide How Long They Stay
**Work ID:** W7222049986 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal headline for predictive finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "But Their Noses Decide How Long They Stay"
- **Abstract says:** ""perceived air quality predicts dwell time, which predicts spending, whereas an objective air quality indicator predicts neither""
- **Problem:** 'Decide' asserts causation for what the abstract describes as a predictive relationship in a validation study (although Study 4 provides experimental validation of the scale, not of the dwell-time chain).
- **Suggested fix:** "Soften to 'But How the Air Feels Predicts How Long They Stay.'"

**Issue B. Country limitation not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the broader program is concentrated in a limited set of categories and countries"
- **Abstract says:** "The abstract names café and supermarket customers but does not identify any country or set of countries."
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only — the geographic scope of the studies is not stated.
- **Suggested fix:** "Limit the caveat to the categories tested (cafés and supermarkets) or mark the country point as not reported in the abstract."

### Study 5: Bank Branches Earn Their Keep as Reassurance, Not Technology Showrooms
**Work ID:** W7223672553 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal verb across mixed designs** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Staff competence and human interaction (SOCIALIZE), professional atmospherics (SURROUND) and local embeddedness (SITUATE) consistently drove loyalty and word of mouth"
- **Abstract says:** ""Across the studies, SURROUND, SOCIALIZE and SITUATE emerge as the most consequential dimensions" and "In consumer reviews, SOCIALIZE is the strongest predictor of ratings.""
- **Problem:** 'Drove' implies established causation, but the evidence combines focus groups, hypothetical scenarios and observational review data.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use 'were the dimensions most strongly associated with loyalty and word of mouth.'"

### Study 6: "Would You Like to Donate?" Works Better When the Charity Is Local
**Work ID:** W7223898707 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Design described as a survey** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Researchers surveyed 356 U.S. consumers about checkout charity campaigns"
- **Abstract says:** ""Using partial least squares structural equation modeling with data from 356 U.S. consumers" — the abstract does not state whether data came from a cross-sectional survey or a scenario-based design."
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only; the digest commits to a survey design (and a cross-sectional caveat) that the abstract does not specify.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say 'collected data from 356 U.S. consumers' and note that the abstract does not specify the data-collection design."

**Issue B. Claim that local causes are objectively less impactful** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "a cause you can picture down the road feels more impactful than one halfway around the world, even when the opposite is true"
- **Abstract says:** "The abstract reports that local charities strengthen perceived social connection and perceived donation impact; it makes no claim about actual relative impact of local versus global causes."
- **Problem:** Adds an editorial factual claim about real-world charitable impact that the study did not examine.
- **Suggested fix:** "Delete 'even when the opposite is true' or replace with 'whatever the actual need.'"

### Study 10: Political signals in a store make customers more uncomfortable than rainbow flags or religious symbols
**Work ID:** W7221815419 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Headline reverses a null result** — Severity: Major

- **As written:** "Political signals in a store make customers more uncomfortable than rainbow flags or religious symbols"
- **Abstract says:** ""Study 1 shows that political cues generate greater discomfort than environmental and lifestyle cues, although they do not differ significantly from religious or LGBTQ + cues.""
- **Problem:** The headline states as a finding precisely the comparison the study found non-significant, and the digest's own body text contradicts it. A reader scanning headlines would take away the opposite of what was found.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rewrite as 'Political signals in a store unsettle customers more than green or lifestyle cues — and stacking them makes it worse.'"

**Issue B. 'Riskiest category' overstates the comparison** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "political signaling appears to be the riskiest category"
- **Abstract says:** "Political cues generated greater discomfort than environmental and lifestyle cues only; they did not differ significantly from religious or LGBTQ + cues."
- **Problem:** Implies political cues ranked riskiest against all cue types tested, when they were statistically indistinguishable from two of them.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say 'political signaling carried more discomfort than environmental or lifestyle signalling, and was no less uncomfortable than religious or LGBTQ+ cues.'"

**Issue C. Mechanism claim not tested** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the discomfort comes less from disagreement itself than from feeling surrounded by the message"
- **Abstract says:** "The abstract reports that discomfort statistically accounts for part of the association between cue conditions and avoidance, and that perceived authenticity does not account for the multi-touchpoint effect; it does not test agreement or disagreement with the cue."
- **Problem:** Presents an untested mechanism (disagreement versus saturation) as a research conclusion.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to 'discomfort rose when the same message appeared across both staff and the store environment.'"

### Study 11: A smiley face on the packaging makes dieters think the food is more fattening
**Work ID:** W7222498555 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Packaging specified beyond abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "researchers tested how smile cues — smiling faces in food marketing and packaging — affect consumers' calorie estimates"
- **Abstract says:** "Although smile cues are widely used in food marketing, little is known about how consumers' dietary restraint shapes the calorie-related inferences they draw from such cues."
- **Problem:** The abstract refers to smile cues in food marketing generally and does not specify packaging as the stimulus; the headline builds on this by putting the smiley face "on the packaging".
- **Suggested fix:** "Say "smile cues in food marketing" and note that the specific stimuli (packaging vs. ads) are not identified in the abstract."

**Issue B. Unsupported upside for indulgence seekers** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "it may be inflating perceived calories and suppressing choice in that segment while helping with indulgence-seeking buyers"
- **Abstract says:** "smile cues increase perceived indulgence, which in turn leads to higher calorie perceptions more strongly when dietary restraint is higher or situationally activated ... consumers were less likely to choose foods with a smile cue when dietary restraint is activated than not"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports a weaker effect among low-restraint consumers, not a positive effect; nothing shows smile cues "help" with indulgence-seeking buyers.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "while having a weaker effect among shoppers who are not dieting"."

### Study 14: Push notifications work — until they don't: the point where reminders start backfiring
**Work ID:** W7223280000 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Spillover stated causally** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Notifications also lifted engagement with merchants that weren't advertised, inside and outside the promoted category — the nudge redirected attention across the whole marketplace."
- **Abstract says:** "notifications are associated with increased engagement with non-advertised merchants both within and across categories, suggesting that push notifications reshape attention across the marketplace ecosystem"
- **Problem:** The abstract explicitly frames the cross-merchant result as an association and a suggestion, while the digest asserts it as a demonstrated lift and redirection.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "were associated with increased engagement with non-advertised merchants, suggesting attention is redirected across the marketplace"."

**Issue B. Hedge dropped on high-frequency drop** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "at high frequencies engagement actually fell"
- **Abstract says:** "repeated exposure produces diminishing responsiveness and may even reduce engagement at high notification frequencies"
- **Problem:** The abstract says engagement "may even" fall at high frequencies; the digest states it as an established outcome.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add the hedge: "and at high frequencies engagement may even fall"."

### Study 15: Consumers are barely better than a coin flip at spotting a dishonest ad claim
**Work ID:** W7223392067 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Omitted boundary condition on accuracy** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "claims were judged honest 56.4% of the time, and overall accuracy at separating truth from lies was just 58.9%, with better accuracy on truthful claims than deceptive ones"
- **Abstract says:** "Accuracy varied systematically with the proportion of truthful messages and was higher for truthful than deceptive claims, consistent with the veracity effect."
- **Problem:** The digest reports the headline accuracy figure without noting that accuracy depended on the base rate of truthful messages in the stimulus set, which conditions how the 58.9% should be read.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add that accuracy varied systematically with the proportion of truthful claims presented."
