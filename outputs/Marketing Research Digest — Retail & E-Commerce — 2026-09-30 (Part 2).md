### 1. Pickup lockers don't cannibalize home delivery — they keep customers from leaving

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-28
**Work ID:** W7214672185 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00222429261496232

**The study:** Researchers used a synthetic difference-in-differences design on individual-level transaction data from an Asian pure-play e-commerce platform to measure what happens when the platform opens self-pickup stations near customers. Spending and shopping frequency rose, and home delivery orders were not cannibalized. The gain came almost entirely from reduced churn and deeper engagement among existing shoppers rather than from new customer acquisition — and the benefit shrank when stations were placed close to major offline retail alternatives.

**Why it matters:** Click-and-collect is usually justified as a logistics cost play; this suggests its real return is customer retention, and that the wrong location can hand your traffic to a competing physical store.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Retention, not acquisition, may be the right KPI for click-and-collect rollouts — and location screening should explicitly penalize sites adjacent to strong offline rivals. Worth testing in your own network before assuming the Asian platform's geometry transfers.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For shoppers, a locker down the street quietly changes how often you order online — the convenience of pickup appears to make people stick with one platform rather than shop around.

**Caveats:** Single platform in a single Asian market; quasi-experimental rather than randomized; results may not generalize to markets with different store density or delivery economics.

### 2. Putting EV chargers in the parking lot lifted German grocery revenue 3.4%

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing Research* | **Published:** 2026-09-16
**Work ID:** W7213254140 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00222437261491290

**The study:** Using a staggered difference-in-differences design across more than 300 stores of a leading German grocery chain, researchers found that adding EV charging to store parking lots increased revenue by 3.4%. The lift came from more transactions rather than bigger baskets, peaked during off-peak weekend hours, and reflected market stealing from competitors as well as market expansion. Effects were stronger in stores and neighborhoods with particular characteristics, including surface-to-parking ratio, local competition, and the mix of apartments versus houses.

**Why it matters:** It quantifies a hard-to-value amenity investment and reframes chargers as a traffic-driving and competitive-defense tool, not just a sustainability gesture.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Treat charger installation as a store-network location problem: the data suggest rollout should be prioritized where parking is ample, competition is close, and shoppers live in apartments without home charging. Test whether off-peak weekend staffing and promotions can capture the incremental trips.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Charging your car takes 30 minutes you have to spend somewhere — and this shows how retailers turn that waiting time into extra shopping trips.

**Caveats:** One grocery chain in one country; quasi-experimental design; charger placement was not randomized, so site selection may partly drive results.

### 3. Not knowing what you want can make you more likely to buy

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Research* | **Published:** 2026-09-12
**Work ID:** W7213439880 | **DOI:** 10.1093/jcr/ucag037

**The study:** Across a set of experiments, researchers separated two kinds of uncertainty: not knowing what you want (preference uncertainty) and not knowing what the product will deliver (outcome uncertainty). When outcome uncertainty is inherently high — think the flavor profile of a wine — consumers who were unsure of their own preferences reported higher purchase intent than those with clear preferences. The mechanism was optimism: with fuzzy preferences, many possible outcomes could turn out to be the perfect match, whereas a precise preference means only one can.

**Why it matters:** It inverts the standard assumption that marketers should always help consumers clarify what they want; in unpredictable categories, preference-clarifying tools may actually suppress purchase.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit whether your category has high inherent outcome variability (wine, craft beer, fragrance, specialty coffee, experiential travel). If so, test whether quizzes and preference-profiling tools help or hurt conversion — they may be closing off the optimism that drives the sale.
- **For general readers and consumers:** That pleasant feeling of browsing an unfamiliar wine list may be partly self-deception: when you're unsure what you like, your brain assumes more of the options could be great.

**Caveats:** Experimental studies measuring purchase intent rather than observed purchases; sample composition and size not specified in the abstract; effect is specific to categories where outcomes are genuinely unpredictable.

### 4. Stop storing your keepsakes — using them makes them mean more

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-09-29
**Work ID:** W7214807616 | **DOI:** 10.1002/jcpy.70042

**The study:** Across five studies, researchers tested what happens to a sentimentally valuable object when people actually use it versus store it away for protection. Using the object repeatedly increased its sentimental value, because each use makes the underlying association salient again, which leads people to feel the association is more deeply integrated into their self-concept — a cycle that further boosts the object's value.

**Why it matters:** It gives brands selling gifts, heirlooms, and commemorative products a reason to actively encourage use rather than preservation, and suggests the durability of a product's emotional hold depends on it staying in circulation.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Gift, jewelry, and memorabilia brands could test messaging and packaging that nudges toward everyday use rather than display or storage — a used keepsake may generate more attachment, more repurchase, and more word of mouth than one in a drawer.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The china you never take out and the watch you keep in its box may be losing meaning, not gaining it — the research suggests handling sentimental objects is what keeps them special.

**Caveats:** Laboratory and online experiments rather than long-run field observation; sample details not given in the abstract; short time horizons relative to the multi-year nature of real sentimental attachment.

### 5. Virtual streamers sell fun; human streamers sell function

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Behaviour* | **Published:** 2026-09-17
**Work ID:** W7213554170 | **DOI:** 10.1002/cb.70245

**The study:** In two scenario-based experiments grounded in social presence theory, researchers tested how streamer type (human versus virtual avatar) interacts with product type (utilitarian versus hedonic) in live commerce. Human streamers produced higher purchase intent for utilitarian products, while virtual streamers did better for hedonic ones, with social presence mediating the effect and brand trust strengthening the link between social presence and purchase intent.

**Why it matters:** As brands weigh cheaper, always-on virtual hosts against human influencers, this suggests the choice should follow the product category rather than the cost line.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands experimenting with AI avatars in live shopping could A/B test host type by product category — the hypothesis worth checking is that avatars underperform on practical, high-consideration items where buyers want a real person vouching.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If a cartoon host is selling you makeup and a real person is selling you a vacuum, that may not be an accident — the format is being matched to how you make each kind of decision.

**Caveats:** Two hypothetical scenario experiments measuring stated purchase intent, not real purchases; sample size and country not specified in the abstract; single mechanism tested in a fast-changing format.

### 6. The "imported goods" endcap that made imported goods sell 30% worse

**Journal:** *Marketing Intelligence & Planning* | **Published:** 2026-09-19
**Work ID:** W7213671071 | **DOI:** 10.1108/mip-12-2024-0890

**The study:** Researchers ran a 60-day field test with a local supermarket chain in Turkiye: eight imported snack items were pulled out of their normal category shelves and grouped onto a dedicated "imported products" endcap in one store, with a matched control store tracked for comparison. Unit sales of those items fell 30.28% versus the control. A follow-up online survey (189 respondents in Turkiye, 199 in England) found purchase intention was also lower in the dedicated-display scenario than in within-category shelf placement in both countries, with display format mattering more than country.

**Why it matters:** Special display fixtures are treated as a near-automatic sales lift in retail planning and are often paid for by suppliers, yet here isolating products from their category removed the comparison context shoppers apparently rely on and destroyed nearly a third of sales.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Retailers and brand managers who buy or grant endcap and themed-display space should treat it as a hypothesis to be tested store-by-store, not a guaranteed uplift — especially for products whose appeal depends on being compared against domestic alternatives on the shelf. This is one chain and one category, so the practical move is to run matched-store A/B tests before committing display budgets.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Shoppers apparently decide about imported snacks by weighing them against the familiar options right next to them; move the import to its own special stand and it loses the reference point that justified the price. It's a reminder that where something sits in a store changes how good a deal it seems.

**Caveats:** Field test covers only two stores, one chain, one country and eight imported snack items; the second study is an online survey using hypothetical scenarios and stated purchase intention, explaining about 20% of variance. No pre-registration detail reported.

### 7. When buyers and sellers get too friendly, the platform loses the transaction

**Journal:** *Asia Pacific Journal of Marketing and Logistics* | **Published:** 2026-09-24
**Work ID:** W7214190181 | **DOI:** 10.1108/apjml-04-2026-0905

**The study:** Using longitudinal transaction data from a leading Chinese freight exchange platform, researchers measured how stable each user's trading-partner network was across periods and modeled it against continued on-platform activity with two-way fixed effects. Carriers with more stable partner networks made fewer subsequent transactions on the platform; relationship dissolution was associated with fewer transactions, while forming new relationships was associated with more. The pattern did not hold robustly for shippers, suggesting the two sides of the marketplace behave differently.

**Why it matters:** Marketplaces spend heavily to create good matches, but the better the match sticks, the more the pair has reason to trade directly and bypass the platform — a structural tension for any intermediary that bills per transaction.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Platform operators should look at whether their own "successful match" metric is quietly predicting churn, and consider governance and pricing that reward repeat relationships on-platform (subscriptions, escrow, insurance, financing) rather than only per-transaction search fees. Note the effect appeared on the supply side only, so the two market sides may need different retention designs.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It explains a familiar dynamic: once you've found a good plumber, driver or supplier through an app, you stop using the app. The relationship, not the platform, becomes the infrastructure.

**Caveats:** Single platform in a single country and industry (Chinese freight); associational fixed-effects evidence rather than an experiment, and the paper itself frames results as consistent with, not proof of, the disintermediation interpretation.

### 8. Chatbots that get local culture make a foreign retailer feel like a hometown brand

**Journal:** *International Marketing Review* | **Published:** 2026-09-28
**Work ID:** W7214665435 | **DOI:** 10.1108/imr-10-2025-0500

**The study:** A mixed-methods study of a cross-border online retailer operating in one collectivist emerging market combined 15 semi-structured consumer interviews with a survey-based field study of 303 online consumers analyzed via structural equation modeling. Chatbots perceived as culturally fluent raised perceived brand localness, which fed consumer–brand identification and, in turn, value co-creation behaviors such as feedback and advocacy. Consumer ethnocentrism did not moderate the effect, suggesting even nationally-minded shoppers responded to culturally attuned bots.

**Why it matters:** As retailers deploy LLM-based service agents globally, this suggests the localization payoff goes beyond translation: cultural register in the chat window can shift how "foreign" the whole brand feels.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** International e-commerce teams could test culturally localized chatbot personas — idiom, humor, holidays, service etiquette — against literal translation and measure downstream identification and advocacy, not just resolution rate. The evidence comes from one retailer in one market and relies on self-reported responses, so treat it as a promising hypothesis for A/B testing rather than an established lift.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The chatbot that knows your local slang and holidays isn't an accident — it's a deliberate lever to make a distant company feel like it belongs where you live. Worth noticing the next time a support bot sounds unexpectedly familiar.

**Caveats:** Single cross-border retailer in one collectivist emerging market; survey-based self-report with cross-sectional structure limiting causal claims; qualitative phase N=15.

### 9. Chinese shoppers reward ESG only when they believe you mean it

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-09-27
**Work ID:** W7214549693 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105127

**The study:** Two studies with Chinese retail consumers tested how ESG messaging converts into purchase intention: a cross-sectional survey (N=226) mapping the pathways, and a two-condition between-subjects experiment (N=156) manipulating whether corporate ESG actions were framed as sincerely motivated or strategically calculated. ESG performance raised purchase intention even among skeptical consumers, but the effect ran substantially through consumer–company identification, and only sincere-motive framing activated that identification — the identification pathway accounted for 38.3% of the total effect.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the return on ESG spending depends less on the disclosure itself than on whether shoppers read the motive as genuine, which is a communications problem as much as an operations one.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Sustainability communicators can test framing that foregrounds values, history and costs borne rather than awards and metrics, and measure whether it moves identification — the mediating variable that carried most of the effect here. The experiment is small and scenario-based, so treat it as direction for message testing rather than proof.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Consumers are running a motive check on every green claim: the same recycled packaging reads as principle or as PR depending on how it's explained, and that judgment predicts whether they buy.

**Caveats:** Single country (China); small experimental sample (N=156 across two conditions, under 100 per cell); survey study is cross-sectional and cannot establish causation; outcome is stated purchase intention, not observed purchases.

### 10. Hand sanitizer stations still earn their keep: visible hygiene cues soften shoppers' dislike of crowded stores

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-09-18
**Work ID:** W7213558307 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105105

**The study:** Researchers ran a two-part study inside a single French-language survey (N=425) of post-pandemic consumers. Part one found that lingering fear of infection raised health anxiety, but only self-focused personal health anxiety predicted avoiding public service settings. Part two experimentally varied store density (high vs. low) and the presence of sanitary signals: crowding lowered shopping satisfaction, but that penalty shrank when visible hygiene cues were present, and the benefit of those cues was largest among shoppers who remained most worried about infection.

**Why it matters:** Many retailers have quietly stripped out the pandemic-era hygiene signage and sanitizer stations; this suggests those cues still function as a cheap buffer against the satisfaction hit that comes with busy stores.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Worth testing rather than assuming: run a split test keeping visible sanitary cues in a subset of high-traffic stores and track satisfaction and dwell time at peak hours. The study also suggests segmentation value, since the shoppers who respond most are those still carrying residual health anxiety.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For consumers, the finding puts a name to a vague feeling: a packed store bothers people less when there are visible signs someone is managing the risk. It also shows post-pandemic wariness is mostly about protecting yourself, not about others.

**Caveats:** Single country (French-language sample), one survey pool used for both studies, an experimental manipulation of store scenarios rather than observed behavior in real stores, and satisfaction measured as stated response; the moderation by residual fear was exploratory.

### 11. The borderless freelance marketplace isn't: four frictions that quietly kill creative work on global platforms

**Journal:** *Journal of International Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-18
**Work ID:** W7213556593 | **DOI:** 10.1177/1069031x261490582

**The study:** Using a sequential mixed-methods design, researchers conducted 40 semi-structured interviews with freelancers, clients and platform stakeholders across 17 countries, then analyzed 27,427 unsolicited online community discussions. They identified four recurring tensions — organizing, performing, belonging and learning — that systematically constrain freelancers' creative work, sharpened by culturally divergent service norms and Global North–South economic asymmetries.

**Why it matters:** Brands increasingly buy creative work through platforms like these; the study maps where the platform's own architecture, not the talent, degrades the output.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For brand and agency leaders sourcing creative work offshore: audit your briefing, revision and rating practices against these four tensions, since platform metrics that reward speed and compliance can suppress the creative risk-taking you are paying for.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A window into the reality behind the gig-economy promise — the same rating systems and 24-hour responsiveness norms that make platforms convenient for buyers create structural disadvantages for workers in lower-income countries.

**Caveats:** Qualitative and discourse-based evidence, so no causal test of how these tensions affect work quality or earnings; interview sample is modest and self-selected, and community discourse skews toward people motivated to post about problems.

### 12. Blended burgers keep surprising people: review of 79 studies finds hybrid meat wins on taste more often than expected

**Journal:** *Appetite* | **Published:** 2026-09-22
**Work ID:** W7214004504 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.appet.2026.108792

**The study:** A scoping review synthesized 79 peer-reviewed publications published through December 2025 on consumer psychology around hybrid meat products — items blending animal protein with plant or alternative protein — across retail, restaurant and experimental settings. Acceptance, liking and preference were generally high and frequently exceeded consumers' own expectations, sensory appeal was the strongest predictor of liking, and non-meat ratios ranging from 1% all the way to 80% were broadly accepted. Messaging mattered too: environmental and health framings had the greatest positive effect on adoption.

**Why it matters:** For grocers and food brands, hybrids look like a lower-resistance path to reducing meat content than full plant-based substitutes — and the evidence suggests the ceiling on how much meat you can replace is higher than most product teams assume.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Test reformulation ratios more aggressively than intuition suggests, and pair the product with environmental or health messaging rather than hiding the blend; the sensory bar, not the concept, is what determines acceptance.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Consumers who reject meat substitutes outright often like hybrid products when they actually taste them — a reminder that expectations about plant protein tend to be worse than the reality on the plate.

**Caveats:** This is a narrative scoping review, not a meta-analysis, so no pooled effect sizes; the underlying studies are heterogeneous, many use single-session taste tests rather than repeat purchasing, and long-term behavioral adaptation is explicitly flagged as unstudied.

### 13. Researchers build a 12-item test for whether your product gives people something to talk about

**Journal:** *Journal of Business Research* | **Published:** 2026-09-29
**Work ID:** W7214728277 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jbusres.2026.116564

**The study:** Across qualitative grounded-theory work and quantitative validation with diverse samples totaling N=2,672, researchers defined and measured "product social currency" — the extent to which a product's attributes and consumption experience provide conversational value and let people express themselves socially. The resulting 12-item scale predicted brand engagement, purchase intention, satisfaction and word of mouth, and was statistically distinct from brand-level social currency.

**Why it matters:** It gives product and marketing teams a diagnostic they can run pre-launch on the specific product rather than the brand, separating "people like this" from "people will tell others about this."

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Consider adding the scale to concept-testing batteries and comparing scores across a portfolio; the brand-versus-product distinction matters most for companies whose strong brand masks individually unremarkable SKUs.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Limited direct relevance for shoppers, beyond the reminder that a lot of product design is explicitly engineered to give buyers a story to tell about themselves.

**Caveats:** Scale-development work relying on self-reported survey measures and stated purchase intention rather than observed purchases or actual word of mouth; predictive validity is cross-sectional and cannot establish causation.

### 14. Why the same live-shopping pitch lands differently on Amazon than on Facebook

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing Communications* | **Published:** 2026-09-25
**Work ID:** W7214279733 | **DOI:** 10.1080/13527266.2026.2735384

**The study:** Researchers examined how consumers' pre-existing beliefs about a platform shape their response to live-streaming commerce, measuring two kinds of "fit" before participants ever saw a livestream: task-technology fit (is this a good place to shop?) and social-technology fit (is this a good place to interact with people?). Participants assigned to evaluate Amazon rated it high on shopping fit but low on social fit, while Facebook and YouTube showed the reverse pattern. Both types of fit fed engagement and, through engagement, purchase intention — but because Amazon's advantage on one dimension was offset by its deficit on the other, the total indirect effects washed out. Multi-group tests showed the underlying psychological mechanism was the same on every platform; only the starting levels of belief differed.

**Why it matters:** It suggests live commerce is not a single format that can be replicated across channels: the same broadcast has to compensate for whichever kind of "fit" the host platform lacks, adding social warmth on a retail site or shopping credibility on a social one.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands running livestreams on multiple platforms could test platform-specific formats rather than one master stream — heavier host-audience interaction and community cues on marketplace-style platforms, stronger product demonstration, pricing and purchase-path cues on social video. The study shows this is plausible in a measured sample, not that it lifts sales at scale, so treat it as a hypothesis for a channel-level A/B test.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Shoppers arrive at a livestream with expectations already formed by the logo at the top of the screen — the same host selling the same product feels like a sales pitch in one place and a hangout in another, which is worth noticing before you buy on impulse.

**Caveats:** Survey-based study with stimulus exposure and self-reported purchase intention rather than observed purchases; no sample size reported in the abstract; three specific platforms only; the headline mediation effects were partially offsetting and total indirect effects were nonsignificant.

### 15. Blind boxes inspire two kinds of shoppers — and one of them can't stop

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-12
**Work ID:** W7212380221 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jcm-08-2025-8244

**The study:** A survey of 573 blind box buyers in China tested how the "inspiration" that surprise-packaging retail creates converts into buying, using partial least squares structural equation modeling. Feeling inspired by blind boxes predicted purchase intention through two distinct routes: harmonious passion (a controlled, enjoyable enthusiasm) and obsessive passion (a compulsive pull). Sensation-seeking consumers showed a stronger link between inspiration and obsessive passion, amplifying the problematic pathway.

**Why it matters:** Surprise-and-mystery retail formats — blind boxes, loot crates, mystery drops — are among the fastest-growing merchandising tactics, and this evidence says the same design that delights most customers reliably tips a thrill-seeking subset toward compulsive buying.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands building mystery-box or drop-based assortments could examine whether their most profitable repeat buyers are the harmonious kind or the obsessive kind, and test guardrails — purchase caps, pity mechanics, transparent odds, cooling-off prompts — before regulators or media frame the category as gambling-adjacent. This is correlational evidence from one market, so it argues for internal data audits rather than an immediate redesign.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The thrill of not knowing what's in the box is the product, and for people who chase novelty that thrill can shade from fun collecting into chasing — a useful self-check for anyone with a shelf of duplicate figurines.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey of self-selected blind box buyers in a single country (China), so causation cannot be established; outcome is stated purchase intention, not observed spending; all constructs self-reported in one questionnaire.

### 16. eBay proved its own star ratings were hiding bad sellers — then rewired search to fix it

**Journal:** *Quantitative Marketing and Economics* | **Published:** 2026-08-04
**Work ID:** W3121242579 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11129-026-09313-1

**The study:** Researchers used transaction-level eBay data to build a seller quality measure based on the share of all transactions that generate positive feedback, including the many transactions where buyers say nothing at all. That measure predicted bad transaction outcomes and buyers' later purchasing even after controlling for the reputation scores shown publicly on the site. eBay then ran a field experiment inserting the hidden quality measure into its search-ranking algorithm: buyers were steered toward higher-quality sellers, subsequent purchasing rose, and conversion on the spot did not fall.

**Why it matters:** It shows that visible star ratings are systematically too coarse and too flattering to separate good sellers from bad, and that a marketplace can raise long-run buyer activity simply by reranking search on a better internal quality signal — without paying for it in short-term conversion.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Marketplace and retail media teams should ask what their ranking algorithm is actually optimizing: this experiment suggests silence from buyers is information, and that ranking on inferred quality rather than displayed ratings can lift repeat purchasing. It also flags reputational externalities — one bad seller damages trust in the whole platform, which is a case for policing quality even when it costs individual sellers volume.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For shoppers, the lesson is that a seller's public feedback score is a weak guide, partly because disappointed buyers often just walk away without leaving a review. The absence of reviews on a listing may say more than the stars that are there.

**Caveats:** Single platform (eBay) and the field experiment was run by the platform itself, so results reflect eBay's specific search architecture and buyer base; the paper does not establish how long the purchasing gains persist.

### 17. In livestream selling, more charisma is not better — smiles, looks and volume all peak in the middle

**Journal:** *Journal of Services Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-05
**Work ID:** W7172490860 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jsm-11-2025-0811

**The study:** Researchers applied computer vision and audio analysis to 2,346 livestreaming e-commerce videos, extracting nonverbal cues (facial attractiveness, smiling, voice loudness) automatically while manually coding the density of verbal product information, then linked all of it to actual sales. Verbal product information had a straightforwardly positive, linear effect on sales. The nonverbal cues followed inverted-U curves: moderate expressiveness sold best, and both restraint and overdoing it hurt. Crucially, rich verbal content amplified the curve in both directions — good scripts made nonverbal miscalibration more costly, not less.

**Why it matters:** It gives livestream retailers an objective, scalable way to audit host performance and suggests that the industry instinct to cast for maximum energy and beauty is wrong past a point.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands running livestream or shoppable video should test host briefs that pair dense product information with deliberately moderated delivery, and consider scoring past streams on these measurable cues rather than relying on gut feel about who is 'good on camera.' The interaction finding is the sharp one: investing in better scripts raises the stakes on host calibration.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Viewers can see why the hyper-enthusiastic, shouty livestream host often fails to convert — audiences appear to read excessive expressiveness as a sales tactic, while steady, informative delivery does the persuading.

**Caveats:** Observational analysis of livestream videos rather than an experiment, so cue effects are correlational; verbal cues were hand-coded while nonverbal cues were machine-extracted; the sample comes from a single livestreaming context and results may not transfer across platforms or product categories.

### 18. Recommendations feel less manipulative when they come from several AIs instead of one

**Journal:** *Journal of Macromarketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-10
**Work ID:** W7202143753 | **DOI:** 10.1177/02761467261472111

**The study:** Three experiments tested what happens when ethical product recommendations are presented as coming from multiple distinct AI agents using different evaluative criteria, rather than from a single algorithmic voice. Perceived plurality raised consumers' self-brand connection; Study 2 showed the mechanism was a heightened sense of autonomy, which in turn lifted purchase intentions. Study 3 found the effect was strongest among consumers with low product knowledge.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the architecture of a recommendation interface — how many voices appear to be advising you — can matter as much as what is recommended, and that plural framing may particularly help less-informed shoppers feel in control.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Retailers deploying AI shopping assistants could test multi-agent or multi-criteria presentation (for example, separate 'sustainability,' 'value' and 'durability' advisors) against a single recommendation engine, and watch whether the autonomy gain shows up in real conversion and returns, not just stated intent. It is also a reputational hedge as scrutiny of algorithmic nudging grows.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A practical insight for shoppers: feeling like you chose is easily engineered. If an interface presents several AI 'opinions,' that design may be increasing your comfort with the purchase more than improving the advice.

**Caveats:** Online experiments with hypothetical recommendation scenarios and purchase intentions rather than real purchases; sample details and country not specified in the abstract; no evidence yet that plural framing survives repeated exposure or that the recommendations themselves are better.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- *Displayed trust signals are weaker than structural ones.* The eBay study showing public star ratings systematically fail to separate good sellers from bad, and that reranking on an inferred quality measure lifted subsequent purchasing (Work ID W3121242579), lands on the same conclusion as the previous digest's finding that a platform's authorized-brand badge insulated ad returns while ordinary marketplace listings bled effectiveness as bad reviews accumulated (W7204166660). Both say the credibility doing the work sits in platform architecture, not in the visible score.
- *Sincerity, not disclosure, converts.* "Chinese shoppers reward ESG only when they believe you mean it" (W7214549693) extends the previous digest's suspicion thread — the retailer health endorsement that lowered perceived healthiness (W7203930195) and the "sustainable" label's hidden durability inference (W7208768688). Motive attribution is again the mediator.
- *Persuasion knowledge as the brake.* "Recommendations feel less manipulative when they come from several AIs instead of one" (W7202143753) works through the same consumer defensiveness that previously suppressed spend under vague "up to £100 off" threshold deals (W7203672036).
- *Relationships as a commercial liability.* "When buyers and sellers get too friendly, the platform loses the transaction" (W7214190181) rhymes with the previous finding that relationship and loyalty framing paradoxically licensed customer opportunism (W7172339845): closeness with the customer is not automatically an asset to the firm that brokered it.

**Advances**
- *From funnel mapping to field measurement in video commerce.* The previous digest's AR/VR meta-analysis mapped immersion and vividness onto journey stages using largely lab evidence (W7203672599). This batch pushes the same question into live commerce with harder data: computer vision and audio analysis across 2,346 livestreams linked to actual sales (W7172490860), plus platform-belief effects (W7214279733) and host-type–by–product-type interactions (W7213554170).
- *Reputation research moves from observation to platform-run experiment.* Where W7204166660 was a fixed-effects panel from one seller, W3121242579 pairs a new quality measure with an actual eBay search-ranking field experiment — a meaningful step up in causal strength on the same question.
- *Physical touchpoints as retention machinery.* The previous digest showed that how a customer migrates online predicts lifetime profitability (W7202042423); the new pickup-locker study (W7214672185) identifies the mechanism running the other direction — physical infrastructure reducing churn among existing shoppers rather than acquiring new ones.

**Counterpoints**
- *Merchandising interventions are not reliably positive.* The previous digest's merchandising thread was upbeat: mixed-body mannequins raised willingness to pay (W7203777252), AI-redesigned packs won a real-shelf take-home test (W7207745459). "The 'imported goods' endcap that made imported goods sell 30% worse" (W7213671071) is the hard counterexample — a supplier-funded display format that destroyed nearly a third of unit sales by stripping out the comparison context.
- *Helping shoppers decide can cost you the sale.* The previous digest argued for clarifying trust cues and redirecting shoppers to their own needs to close the advice-taking gap (W7212164165). "Not knowing what you want can make you more likely to buy" (W7213439880) cuts against the reflex: in categories with high inherent outcome variability, preference-clarifying tools may close off the optimism that drives purchase.

**New themes**
- Compulsive-consumption design risk in surprise retail (W7212380221) — not present in the previous batch.
- Cultural localization of AI service agents as a brand-positioning lever (W7214665435), distinct from the previous digest's labeling and packaging work.
- Post-pandemic hygiene cues as a crowding buffer (W7213558307), sentimental-object use raising attachment (W7214807616), hybrid meat acceptance (W7214004504), platform-structured creative labor (W7213556593), and a new product-level social currency scale (W7214728277).

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Three studies in this batch, using very different methods, all conclude that live shopping is not one transferable format and that the industry's casting and production instincts are miscalibrated. Computer-vision analysis of 2,346 streams linked to real sales found smiles, attractiveness and vocal volume all follow inverted-U curves, with dense product information the only reliably linear driver ("In livestream selling, more charisma is not better," W7172490860); host type should follow product type, with avatars winning on hedonic goods and humans on utilitarian ones ("Virtual streamers sell fun; human streamers sell function," W7213554170); and the same broadcast lands differently depending on whether the host platform already reads as a shopping place or a social place ("Why the same live-shopping pitch lands differently on Amazon than on Facebook," W7214279733).

**Why pitch this now:** Live and shoppable video is being scaled hard in Western markets just as cheap AI avatar hosts become viable, so brands are making casting, format and platform-portability decisions right now on the assumption that the Chinese playbook copies over intact.

**Angle:** A reported feature on live commerce's transplant problem — what actually moves product on camera versus what the industry keeps buying — built for brand, retail and commerce-media decision-makers, with a consumer-facing thread on why the shouty host is losing to the boring one.

**Potential headlines:**
- What 2,346 livestreams show about who actually sells
- The most charismatic host in live shopping is the wrong one
- Brands are scaling a live commerce format that doesn't travel

**Potential outlets:**
- **Modern Retail** — tracks live and shoppable video rollouts at retailers and DTC brands week to week; this gives the operational counter-evidence its readers are missing.
- **Marketing Brew** — its audience is actively evaluating AI avatar hosts versus creators, and the host-type-by-product-type finding is a concrete decision rule.
- **Adweek** — the casting and talent economics angle (energy and looks past a point hurt sales) sits squarely in its creator and commerce coverage.
- **Rest of World** — the evidence base is largely Chinese and Asian-platform, and the story is precisely about whether a format exports; that transplant question is its core beat.

## Story Ideas by Study

- **Pickup lockers don't cannibalize home delivery — they keep customers from leaving (Work ID W7214672185):** Click-and-collect as a churn-reduction tool rather than a logistics saving, with the warning that siting a locker near a strong physical rival hands over your traffic, for a retail trade analysis or e-commerce operations column.
- **Putting EV chargers in the parking lot lifted German grocery revenue 3.4% (Work ID W7213254140):** A hard revenue number on a sustainability amenity, with the twist that the lift comes from stealing competitors' trips in apartment-heavy neighborhoods, for a retail trade explainer or a business-desk piece on the retail side of EV infrastructure.
- **Not knowing what you want can make you more likely to buy (Work ID W7213439880):** Preference quizzes and profiling tools may be killing conversion in wine, fragrance and travel by eliminating the optimism of fuzzy preferences, for a consumer-psychology explainer in a general-interest outlet or a CX/personalization trade column.
- **Stop storing your keepsakes — using them makes them mean more (Work ID W7214807616):** The wedding china in the cupboard is losing meaning, not accruing it — a service-y behavioral story with a gifting and jewelry marketing implication, for a general-interest lifestyle or consumer-psychology section.
- **Virtual streamers sell fun; human streamers sell function (Work ID W7213554170):** Match the AI avatar to the product category, not to the budget line — avatars appear to underperform on practical, high-consideration items, for a marketing-trade news brief on AI hosts.
- **The "imported goods" endcap that made imported goods sell 30% worse (Work ID W7213671071):** Supplier-funded display space treated as automatic uplift destroyed a third of sales in a matched-store field test, for a retail/CPG trade explainer on trade-spend assumptions.
- **When buyers and sellers get too friendly, the platform loses the transaction (Work ID W7214190181):** Marketplaces' "successful match" metric may be a churn predictor, and the fix is pricing that survives disintermediation, for a platform-economics column or a business/tech analysis section.
- **Chatbots that get local culture make a foreign retailer feel like a hometown brand (Work ID W7214665435):** Cultural register in the chat window shifted perceived brand localness even among ethnocentric shoppers — a localization story for international e-commerce and CX teams, for a marketing-trade or global-business column.
- **Chinese shoppers reward ESG only when they believe you mean it (Work ID W7214549693):** Sincere-motive framing, not disclosure volume, activated the identification pathway carrying most of the effect, for a sustainability-communications trade piece or a CMO-audience column.
- **Hand sanitizer stations still earn their keep (Work ID W7213558307):** Retailers quietly removed pandemic-era hygiene cues that still buffer the satisfaction penalty of crowded stores, for a retail operations trade brief or a consumer-behavior piece on lingering pandemic habits.
- **The borderless freelance marketplace isn't (Work ID W7213556593):** Four platform-designed frictions degrade the creative work brands are buying offshore — pitchable both as a procurement warning and as a labor story, for an agency/brand-strategy column or a general-interest gig-economy feature.
- **Blended burgers keep surprising people (Work ID W7214004504):** Seventy-nine studies suggest the ceiling on replacing meat in hybrids is far higher than product teams assume, and taste beats concept, for a food-industry trade explainer or a food-and-climate section in a general-interest outlet.
- **Researchers build a 12-item test for whether your product gives people something to talk about (Work ID W7214728277):** A pre-launch diagnostic separating "people like this" from "people will tell others," useful for companies whose strong brand masks unremarkable SKUs, for a product-marketing or insights trade brief.
- **Why the same live-shopping pitch lands differently on Amazon than on Facebook (Work ID W7214279733):** Shoppers arrive with platform beliefs already formed, so a single master livestream has to compensate for whichever kind of fit the host platform lacks, for a commerce-media trade analysis.
- **Blind boxes inspire two kinds of shoppers — and one of them can't stop (Work ID W7212380221):** Sensation-seeking buyers convert inspiration into compulsive passion, raising the gambling-adjacent regulatory question before regulators ask it, for a consumer-affairs piece or a retail-trade story on mystery-box merchandising guardrails.
- **eBay proved its own star ratings were hiding bad sellers — then rewired search to fix it (Work ID W3121242579):** Buyer silence is information, and reranking on inferred quality raised repeat purchasing without costing conversion — a rare platform-run field experiment, for a marketplace/retail-media trade analysis, with a consumer sidebar on why review scores mislead.
- **In livestream selling, more charisma is not better (Work ID W7172490860):** Inverted-U curves on smiling, looks and volume, and better scripts make miscalibrated delivery more costly, not less, for a creator-economy or commerce-video trade piece.
- **Recommendations feel less manipulative when they come from several AIs instead of one (Work ID W7202143753):** Splitting an AI shopping assistant into multiple criteria-specific advisors raised felt autonomy and purchase intent, especially among low-knowledge shoppers — engineered choice, for an AI-in-retail trade column or a consumer-tech explainer on algorithmic nudging.
