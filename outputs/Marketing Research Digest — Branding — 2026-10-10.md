### 1. Copywriters who hate your brand write duller ads — and they do it on purpose

**Journal:** *International Journal of Research in Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-01
**Work ID:** W7221130306 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.ijresmar.2026.09.011

**The study:** Published in the *International Journal of Research in Marketing*, this research tests how the personal brand attitudes of professional creatives and other promotional content creators shape the work they produce. Across the studies, creators who disliked a brand produced promotional content loaded with functional, rational elements and stripped of emotional ones; creators with positive attitudes showed no comparable shift. The mechanism is deliberate self-correction: creators hold a lay belief that their negative feelings could contaminate the work, so they try to stay rational and suppress emotion. The effect held after accounting for brand characteristics, the specific reasons behind creators' attitudes, and task difficulty.

**Why it matters:** Emotional advertising generally outperforms purely functional advertising on long-term brand effects, so a creative team's private dislike of a client brand may quietly push the work toward the weaker end of that spectrum.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Agency and in-house leaders could test whether creative staffing on a brand correlates with the emotional content of the output — and whether asking creatives about their attitude to a brand before assignment, or reassuring them that emotion is appropriate, changes what they produce. The suppression here is well-intentioned, which makes it harder to spot in a review process.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The ads you see are filtered through the feelings of the people who made them. An unusually dry, spec-sheet-style campaign for a consumer brand may be a sign that the people behind it were trying very hard to be fair to a brand they do not personally like.

**Caveats:** The abstract does not specify sample sizes, countries or whether participants were working professionals versus panel participants in every study; content was created in research tasks rather than measured as live campaign performance.

### 2. Retro logos read as 'safe': old-fashioned design boosts trust through perceived stability, not warm feelings

**Journal:** *Asia Pacific Journal of Marketing and Logistics* | **Published:** 2026-10-08
**Work ID:** W7221048994 | **DOI:** 10.1108/apjml-01-2026-0072

**The study:** A four-experiment program tested why nostalgic logo designs increase brand trust, including a test using a real-world brand interface. The driver was not affective nostalgia, familiarity, processing fluency or general brand liking — all of which the researchers ruled out — but perceived visual stability, a cognitive inference that the brand is unchanging and therefore reliable. The effect appeared selectively among consumers high in uncertainty avoidance and held even for low-narrative, emotionally flat logos.

**Why it matters:** It reframes retro logo design as a risk-reduction signal rather than a sentimentality play, which changes when and for whom a heritage-style identity is worth using.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands weighing a logo refresh — especially in categories where trust is the purchase barrier, such as finance, insurance or health — could test heritage-leaning design against modernization, and segment by customers' tolerance for uncertainty rather than by age or nostalgia appeal. The finding also suggests that frequent identity updates may carry a hidden trust cost.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Consumers may be reading a slightly dated logo as evidence that a company has been around and will stick around — an inference about longevity that the design itself can fake.

**Caveats:** Experimental studies with stimuli rather than purchase outcomes; the abstract does not report sample sizes, participant sources or country, and trust was measured as a judgment rather than through observed behavior.

### 3. Collaboration fatigue is real: why young Chinese consumers are turning on co-branded launches

**Journal:** *European Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-09
**Work ID:** W7221096517 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ejm-06-2025-0496

**The study:** A two-part study examined backlash in a market saturated with brand collaborations. The first part analyzed walking interviews (n=55), in-depth interviews (n=12) and 186 netnographic artifacts from young Chinese consumers, identifying resistance built on strategic misalignment, identity and cultural disconnect, and emotional and cognitive strain. The second part built 29 real co-branding episode cases and used fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis to find the combinations that produce high consumer resistance — clustering around distorted practical value, clashing meanings and split emotional experience, with image erosion sufficient on its own in one pathway.

**Why it matters:** It identifies co-branding failure as something other than poor brand fit: pacing, release density and the gap between promotional claims and actual product experience each drive backlash independently.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brand teams running frequent collaborations could audit release cadence and partner screening as risk factors, not just creative fit — and pressure-test whether a tie-up's promotional claims survive contact with the product. The configurational results suggest no single safeguard works; resistance comes from combinations.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Shoppers are getting tired of limited-edition mashups, and the irritation is specific: the collaboration costs more, means less, and delivers a worse experience than the plain version of the product.

**Caveats:** Qualitative and configurational methods, not causal experiments; young consumers in a single country (China) and a high-frequency collaboration environment that may not generalize to Western markets; small case base (29 episodes).

### 4. Rebranding an acquisition: executives want capability proof, staff want their professional pride back

**Journal:** *Journal of Business and Industrial Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-07
**Work ID:** W7220855662 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jbim-06-2025-0517

**The study:** Researchers followed a multinational B2B life sciences company through a global rebranding of an acquired brand, using interviews, internal documents and validation workshops. Brand equity transfer required an awareness condition first — stakeholders had to register that the brand had changed before evaluating it — but buy-in depended on brand image, and what drove image differed by level. Strategic-level stakeholders responded to functional arguments (capabilities, market position, customer value), while operational-level stakeholders responded to symbolic ones (professional pride, reputation, familiar ways of working).

**Why it matters:** Most post-acquisition rebranding communication is written once and pushed to everyone; this suggests the same message will land with the C-suite and fail with the people who actually carry the brand to customers.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Companies integrating an acquired brand could split internal rebranding communication along functional and symbolic lines by audience level, and treat subsidiary and employee feedback as an input that reshapes the rollout rather than noise after the fact.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Limited direct relevance for consumers, beyond explaining why a supplier's name change can feel abrupt and half-explained: the internal selling job is often harder than the external one.

**Caveats:** Single-company qualitative case study in one industry (B2B life sciences); findings are interpretive and not generalizable or causal; no outcome measures of rebranding success.

### 5. Ethical scandals ignite boycotts; product failures mostly just annoy people

**Journal:** *Journal of Product & Brand Management* | **Published:** 2026-10-09
**Work ID:** W7221118815 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jpbm-02-2026-6784

**The study:** A two-part study paired a scenario-based experiment (N=501) contrasting an ethical brand crisis with a performance-related one against a netnographic analysis of 5,729 social media posts about real brand controversies. Ethical crises produced significantly stronger brand hate and boycott intention than performance failures, and brand hate carried more than half of the total effect of crisis type on boycott intention. Consumers high in neuroticism reacted most strongly; agreeableness made no difference. In the real online discourse, ideological incongruence was the most prominent trigger of hate, and hostility frequently spilled over onto sponsors, celebrity endorsers, sub-brands and even political parties.

**Why it matters:** It suggests crisis response cannot be one-size-fits-all: a defective product is a competence problem, but a values breach is an identity problem that recruits emotion, spreads to partners, and converts into organized boycotts.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brand and comms leaders can use this to argue for separate crisis playbooks by crisis type, and to map contagion risk across the sponsorship and endorsement portfolio before a crisis hits. Because the experiment used hypothetical scenarios, treat the magnitudes as directional and pressure-test them against the brand's own social listening data; the netnographic half shows the hostility patterns are at least visible in real discourse.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The findings put language to something shoppers already sense: we forgive brands that fail us but not brands that offend us. The analysis also shows how boycott talk escalates in register, from cool sarcasm to hot outrage, and how justifications shift from 'this will change their behavior' to pure expression of anger as intensity rises.

**Caveats:** Study 1 used hypothetical crisis scenarios and stated boycott intentions rather than observed purchase behavior; sample composition and country are not specified in the abstract. The netnographic data are observational and platform-dependent, so they describe who posts loudly rather than the average consumer.

### 6. Price promotions may generate more real sales — and less brand-switching — than standard models assume

**Journal:** *Marketing Science* | **Published:** 2026-10-08
**Work ID:** W7220913319 | **DOI:** 10.1287/mksc.2024.1280

**The study:** Marketing Science researchers reworked the econometrics of dynamic discrete-choice demand models for storable goods, showing that consumers' discount factor can be identified from the model's own built-in restrictions without the extra assumptions about storage costs that prior work relied on. Applying the approach to a published laundry detergent purchase dataset, they estimated a mean discount factor of 0.957, decisively rejecting the idea that shoppers choose myopically. Counterfactual simulations confirmed that some promotional lift comes from forward-buying, but predicted considerably higher incremental sales than a standard static choice model, which attributes most of the lift to brand-switching.

**Why it matters:** How a firm models promotion response determines whether a discount looks like pulled-forward demand and stolen share or genuine category expansion — and that changes promotional budgets and trade spend decisions.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Analytics and revenue management teams should check which demand model their promotion ROI estimates rest on; the paper implies conventional models may systematically understate true incremental sales from discounts in stockpileable categories. Worth replicating internally on a brand's own scanner or loyalty data before reallocating trade spend.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The estimated discount factor near 0.96 is evidence that ordinary shoppers are strategic: when detergent goes on sale they are, in effect, calculating future consumption and storage, not just grabbing a bargain.

**Caveats:** A single product category (laundry detergent) and a re-analysis of previously published data; the contribution is primarily econometric identification, and the empirical conclusions depend on model structure and simulation assumptions rather than a field experiment.

### 7. Not all social proof helps: testimonials that look paid for can actively reduce repurchase

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-10-02
**Work ID:** W7216479640 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105142

**The study:** A two-study project in Vietnam's online marketplace combined a Vietnamese-tuned RoBERTa sentiment analysis of social media comments with moderated regression on survey responses from 394 active online shoppers. Brand image strongly predicted repurchase intention, but the three forms of social proof behaved very differently: user-generated content amplified the brand image effect most (especially among consumers with middling brand perceptions), brand ambassadors helped only up to a point and faded once brand image was already strong, and testimonials cut both ways — boosting repurchase when seen as credible but independently triggering skepticism and weakening repurchase when they looked commercially motivated.

**Why it matters:** It challenges the common assumption that stacking more endorsements, ambassadors and reviews is uniformly positive; the wrong kind of proof on a strong brand can subtract rather than add.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Marketers can treat this as a prompt to audit their social proof mix by brand-strength segment: lean on authentic user content for mid-tier or unfamiliar brands, and question whether ambassador spend is still earning its keep on already-strong brands. Because this is survey evidence from one market, test the pattern with holdout experiments on landing pages and product detail pages.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Shoppers are better lie detectors than marketers assume. The study suggests the moment a testimonial reads as bought, it doesn't just stop working — it makes people trust the brand less than if there had been no testimonial at all.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey with stated repurchase intention rather than observed purchases, so causation cannot be established; single country (Vietnam) and a digital-marketplace context; the NLP component is exploratory and does not test the hypotheses.

### 8. The cup is the product: café packaging design shapes buying intent — differently for Starbucks and Luckin

**Journal:** *International Journal of Retail & Distribution Management* | **Published:** 2026-10-07
**Work ID:** W7220832664 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ijrdm-11-2025-0871

**The study:** Researchers surveyed customers of Starbucks (global, semi-premium) and Luckin Coffee (domestic, budget) in China and used PLS-SEM with multigroup comparison to test how three facets of cup design — aesthetic, symbolic and functional — drive purchase intention via hedonic and utilitarian value. All three design facets fed both value routes for both brands, but symbolic design and utilitarian value mattered significantly more for Starbucks customers. Gender differences appeared only at Starbucks, where women's responses ran through hedonic value and men's through utilitarian value; no such split emerged for Luckin.

**Why it matters:** It suggests disposable packaging functions as brand communication whose psychological leverage depends on the brand's positioning — status signaling pays off for premium brands and barely registers for budget ones.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Packaging and brand design teams can use this as a reason to brief cup and container design differently by price tier: symbolism and visible branding for premium positioning, function and cost efficiency for value positioning. Since the evidence is survey-based in one market, validate with in-store A/B tests or sales data before a redesign.
- **For general readers and consumers:** That branded cup you carry across the office is doing social work. The research suggests people buying a premium coffee are partly paying for what the cup says about them — something that disappears entirely when the brand is a budget one.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey measuring purchase intention, not actual sales; single country (China) and two brands only; gender findings rest on subgroup comparisons and should be treated as exploratory.

### 9. Heritage or exclusivity? The right luxury pitch depends on how hierarchical your customers are

**Journal:** *Psychology and Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-08
**Work ID:** W7220958293 | **DOI:** 10.1002/mar.70278

**The study:** Researchers ran three online experiments plus one field study testing two classic luxury brand signals — exclusivity ("few can have it") versus heritage ("made this way since 1897") — against consumers' power distance beliefs, the degree to which people accept social hierarchy as natural. Power distance was manipulated experimentally, measured as a stable individual trait, and proxied at the country level. Consumers low in power distance responded more favorably to heritage signals, while high-power-distance consumers preferred exclusivity, across click-through rates in the field, incentive-compatible choice, purchase intention and willingness to pay a premium. The effect ran through recognition benefits and then brand attachment.

**Why it matters:** Luxury houses typically run one global creative platform; this suggests the same campaign can under- or over-perform purely because of the cultural mindset of the audience, and that the fix is a signal swap rather than a new brand story.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For brands running luxury or premium communications across multiple markets, this is a concrete A/B test worth doing: hold the product and visuals constant, swap heritage framing for scarcity framing, and look at click-through and conversion by market. The field component makes the effect more credible than a lab-only result, but the size of the lift in any one brand's real media plan is still an open question.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It's a useful window into why the same expensive handbag is sold to you as "only 300 made" in one country and "a family workshop since 1897" in another — the pitch is calibrated to how much you personally care about status and rank.

**Caveats:** Three of four studies were online experiments with stated intentions or choices; the country-level power distance measure is a crude proxy for individual beliefs; the field study's platform and category are not specified in the abstract.

### 10. Modest brands outsell boastful ones — unless the brand is luxury

**Journal:** *Journal of Advertising* | **Published:** 2026-08-14
**Work ID:** W7203440056 | **DOI:** 10.1080/00913367.2026.2711046

**The study:** Across four studies, researchers compared brand messages written in a self-effacing, modest register against self-enhancing, boastful ones, tracking attitudes and actual purchasing behavior including choices involving monetary tradeoffs. Self-effacing messages produced stronger consumer attitudes and more purchasing overall. The mechanism was lower skepticism and higher brand trust. But perceived message–brand fit moderated everything: for non-luxury brands, modesty won, while for luxury brands boastful messaging matched brand identity and the gap between the two styles disappeared.

**Why it matters:** Most advertising copy defaults to superlatives; this evidence suggests that for mainstream brands, understatement is read as honesty and converts better, while luxury brands have more latitude to brag.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Worth testing in live copy: rewrite a campaign's claims in a deliberately modest register and watch trust and conversion metrics, especially for value or mass-market brands where skepticism toward superlatives is highest. The studies show the effect can occur; whether it survives a competitive media environment where rivals are shouting is the next question.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Explains the instinctive distrust many people feel toward "the best in the world" claims — and why a brand saying "we're not for everyone" can feel more credible than one promising perfection.

**Caveats:** Experimental studies; sample composition and sizes are not specified in the abstract; purchase behavior was measured within study tasks rather than in an ongoing market.

### 11. When AI writes your brand's social stance, consumers smell inauthenticity

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Affairs* | **Published:** 2026-08-21
**Work ID:** W7203997372 | **DOI:** 10.1111/joca.70072

**The study:** Five studies examined how consumers react to brand activism messages — sociopolitical statements — when they are disclosed as generative-AI-created versus human-written. AI-created activism messages produced greater negative affect and, in turn, lower decision comfort, because consumers judged them less authentic. The effect weakened among politically conservative consumers and among people with a high tendency to anthropomorphize AI.

**Why it matters:** Brands are quietly routing social and values-based communication through generative AI, and this is evidence that the one content category where AI authorship carries a real penalty may be exactly the one where brands claim to speak from conviction.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If your brand takes public positions on social issues, audit which parts of that content pipeline are AI-assisted and consider human authorship and disclosure policy for value-laden messaging specifically — the penalty appears to be about perceived authenticity, not output quality. Test whether disclosure or named human authorship restores comfort.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Shows that people apply a different standard to machine-written words depending on the subject: AI-drafted product copy is fine, AI-drafted moral conviction is not, and the discomfort shows up as a worse feeling about your own purchase decision.

**Caveats:** Five experiments, likely online panels; sample sizes and countries not specified in the abstract; relies on disclosed or manipulated AI authorship rather than consumers detecting it in the wild; measures affect and decision comfort rather than real purchases.

### 12. "Fix it, don't toss it": the right repair pitch flips depending on whether you're fast or slow fashion

**Journal:** *Journal of Advertising Research* | **Published:** 2026-09-29
**Work ID:** W7214746746 | **DOI:** 10.1080/00218499.2026.2704263

**The study:** Four experiments grounded in construal level theory tested abstract repair appeals (broad sustainability framing) against concrete ones (specific, practical repair instructions) for fast fashion versus slow fashion brands, measuring perceived environmental friendliness and purchase intention. Consumer education level moderated the result: more educated consumers preferred abstract appeals from slow fashion brands and concrete appeals from fast fashion brands, while less educated consumers showed the reverse pattern.

**Why it matters:** Repair and circularity messaging is proliferating across apparel, and this suggests a mismatched frame can blunt both the green credibility and the commercial payoff of the same initiative.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For apparel brands launching repair programs, the creative frame is not interchangeable with the brand position: a fast fashion retailer leaning on lofty sustainability abstractions may be the worst combination for its most educated shoppers. Worth testing concrete how-to repair content against values-led framing by segment before scaling.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A reminder that "repair, don't replace" messaging lands differently depending on who's saying it — the same appeal reads as practical help from one brand and as greenwashing from another.

**Caveats:** Four experiments measuring purchase intention rather than observed behavior; sample sizes, recruitment source and country not specified in the abstract; using education level as a proxy for construal is a strong assumption.

### 13. AI service failures don't just annoy customers — they corrode the brand relationship

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-10-04
**Work ID:** W7219659105 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105143

**The study:** Researchers mined 3,208 complaint records (2,824 usable) from a major Chinese consumer complaint platform using topic modeling, identifying five distinct types of AI customer service failure: functional, informational, systemic, interactional, and service recovery failure. A second study surveyed 965 Chinese consumers and analyzed the data with PLS-SEM, neural networks, fsQCA and necessary condition analysis. All five failure types predicted negative consumer-brand relationships, with informational failure (wrong or inadequate answers) and systemic failure (the system itself breaking down) consistently the most damaging across every analytic method.

**Why it matters:** Companies typically evaluate AI service on deflection rate and cost per contact; this evidence frames bot failures as a brand equity problem, with accuracy of information and system reliability mattering more than how human the bot feels.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Rebalance AI service scorecards toward informational accuracy and system uptime rather than conversational polish, and build escalation paths specifically for recovery failure — the category where a bot fails and then fails again at fixing it. The complaint-text typology is a ready-made coding frame for auditing your own contact logs.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Puts structure to a universal frustration: the worst chatbot experiences aren't the ones that sound robotic, they're the ones that confidently give you wrong information or break mid-conversation with no way out.

**Caveats:** Chinese consumers and a single national complaint platform only; the survey component is cross-sectional and self-reported, so it cannot establish causation; complaint data is inherently skewed toward dissatisfied customers.

### 14. Mystery boxes work — but only for the right kind of shopper

**Journal:** *European Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-17
**Work ID:** W7203651996 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ejm-09-2025-0775

**The study:** Across six studies, researchers tested how consumers respond to "uncertainty marketing" — offers that deliberately conceal what you're getting, like mystery boxes, surprise bundles and blind drops. They manipulated or measured two epistemic motivations (need for cognition, the enjoyment of effortful thinking; and need for cognitive closure, the desire for a quick, definite answer) alongside whether people approached the purchase with a learning goal or an outsourcing goal. When motivation and goal lined up — thinkers who wanted to figure it out, closure-seekers who wanted to hand the decision off — purchase intention, trust and perceived self-efficacy all rose, with self-efficacy carrying the effect.

**Why it matters:** Uncertainty tactics are spreading from gacha games and sneaker drops into mainstream retail, and this suggests their success is less about the gimmick itself than about whether the surrounding message tells the shopper to think or to relax and delegate.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you're running mystery or blind offers, the copy around them may matter as much as the offer: test framings that invite discovery ("figure out which one you got") against framings that promise relief from choosing ("we picked for you") on different segments, rather than assuming a single tone works. These are controlled experiments, so treat the matching principle as a hypothesis to A/B test in live campaigns, not a settled rule.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Mystery boxes feel fun to some people and faintly manipulative to others — this research suggests that split tracks a personality difference in how much you enjoy puzzling things out versus wanting the decision taken off your hands. Knowing which you are is a useful defense when a brand hides what it's selling.

**Caveats:** The abstract does not report sample sizes, participant sources or countries, and the studies appear to measure purchase intentions rather than observed purchases.

### 15. "Love it, hate it, can't quit it": ambivalence isn't a phase of social media use, it's the structure

**Journal:** *Psychology and Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-08
**Work ID:** W7220944415 | **DOI:** 10.1002/mar.70281

**The study:** Using a three-study, multi-method design, researchers examined how consumers talk about and evaluate their own social media use, including discourse about quitting. They found that positive value (connection, utility) and experienced costs (loss of control, diminished well-being) co-occur within the same accounts and do not resolve over time; instead people cycle through imperfect fixes such as boundary setting, platform switching and abstinence attempts. The authors argue net-benefit models — which assume consumers settle into either liking or disliking a platform — miss this persistent ambivalence.

**Why it matters:** Brands buy engagement on platforms that users themselves describe as compulsive, which means high engagement metrics can coexist with resentment that may eventually attach to advertisers on the platform.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Consider auditing whether your platform engagement is being earned in a context users experience as a trap: ambivalent audiences may be more receptive to brands that acknowledge the tension (time-respecting formats, off-platform owned channels) than to those that maximize dwell time. Treat this as a reframing of what engagement metrics mean, not as a measured revenue effect.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If you feel both grateful for and resentful toward your feed, that's not a failure of willpower that will eventually resolve — the research suggests the mixed feeling is a permanent feature of the product, and that quitting attempts, app switching and self-imposed limits are the normal, partial responses.

**Caveats:** The abstract gives no sample sizes, participant sources or countries for the three studies, and the evidence is largely interpretive and discourse-based rather than behavioral; no causal claims are possible.

### 16. Brand image won't save you from a scandal — satisfaction and love might

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Behaviour* | **Published:** 2026-09-29
**Work ID:** W7214968251 | **DOI:** 10.1002/cb.70239

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 700 consumers of clothing brands and used PLS-SEM to model what makes people resist negative information about a brand. Brand satisfaction predicted resistance to negative information, but brand image — the distinctive associations marketers spend heavily to build — did not do so directly. Both satisfaction and image fed brand love, which in turn strengthened resistance; Big Five personality traits had only a minimal moderating role.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the reputational armor that carries a brand through bad press is built from actual customer experience and emotional attachment, not from image advertising alone.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Worth questioning the assumption that brand-image campaigns buy crisis insurance: in this data the protective path runs through satisfaction and emotional attachment, which points to service recovery, product consistency and loyalty programs as reputation investments. It's correlational survey evidence in one category, so a natural next step is testing whether satisfaction scores actually predict which customers stay after a real incident.
- **For general readers and consumers:** When your favorite clothing brand gets hit with bad headlines, whether you shrug it off probably depends less on its advertising than on whether it has personally treated you well — which is also a decent cue for noticing when loyalty is doing your thinking for you.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey, so causation can't be established; self-reported resistance rather than observed behavior after an actual negative event; a single industry (clothing and fashion); the country of the sample isn't stated in the abstract.

### 17. Which content tasks to hand to AI, and which to keep: a workflow map from one real marketing team

**Journal:** *Marketing Intelligence & Planning* | **Published:** 2026-09-29
**Work ID:** W7214780511 | **DOI:** 10.1108/mip-03-2025-0271

**The study:** An exploratory case study followed how a small education company integrated generative AI into its social media content production, mapping seven activities from evidence synthesis and thematic classification through script generation, refinement, media production and evaluation. Data came from practitioner interviews and workflow analysis. AI delivered efficiency gains in research synthesis, ideation and language polish, while credibility verification, brand alignment and final sign-off stayed with humans — producing a hybrid model in which AI handles low-risk tasks and people own high-risk ones.

**Why it matters:** Most GenAI marketing guidance is either hype or abstraction; this is a task-by-task account from inside a working team of where automation held up and where it didn't.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Use it as a template for your own audit: classify each step of your content pipeline by risk (factual claims, brand voice, legal and copyright exposure) and decide deliberately which are automatable, rather than letting adoption happen tool by tool. One SME's experience is a starting hypothesis, not a benchmark — your category's risk profile may put more steps in the human column.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Much of the social content you scroll past is now part machine-written, and this offers a glimpse of where the human still intervenes — usually at fact-checking and tone, not at drafting.

**Caveats:** Single-firm, single-sector case study (an education SME) with no control condition and no quantified efficiency or performance outcomes; findings are practitioner-reported and not generalizable.

### 18. Decadent imagery only goes viral for luxury brands — for everyone else it backfires

**Journal:** *Marketing Letters* | **Published:** 2026-09-09
**Work ID:** W7212008823 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11002-026-09838-1

**The study:** Researchers analyzed 98,081 brand images posted on social media and paired that dataset with controlled experiments testing how willing people were to share visually indulgent content (rich desserts, sensual imagery). For low-end mainstream brands, the more indulgent the image, the less people wanted to share it — because sharing it signaled something unflattering about themselves. As brand luxury rose, that reputational worry faded, and for high-end luxury brands indulgent visuals actually boosted sharing by raising emotional arousal.

**Why it matters:** Indulgence is one of the most common levers in food, beauty and fashion creative, and this suggests the same creative asset can help or hurt reach depending entirely on where the brand sits on the luxury ladder.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Mass-market brands leaning on "food porn" style content should test whether indulgence is suppressing shares rather than driving them; the mechanism identified here is the sharer's own reputation, so creative that gives people cover (humor, occasion framing, social permission) is worth A/B testing against straight indulgence.
- **For general readers and consumers:** What people repost is less about what they like and more about what it says about them — consumers quietly self-censor indulgent content when the brand attached to it doesn't flatter them.

**Caveats:** Sharing intention in the experiments is stated intention rather than observed resharing; the large image dataset is correlational, and the experimental portion's sample details are limited in the abstract.

### 19. After a brand takes a political stand, angry defectors don't just leave — they want to punish it

**Journal:** *The Journal of Marketing Theory and Practice* | **Published:** 2026-08-23
**Work ID:** W7204024551 | **DOI:** 10.1080/10696679.2026.2717180

**The study:** Across three studies, researchers examined what happens competitively after ideologically misaligned brand activism. Study 1a analyzed tweets responding to high-profile Nike activism and found more than half detailed switching to a competitor. Study 1b, based on consumers' actual switching experiences, found conservatives placed greater importance on supporting a direct rival, driven by retaliatory motives. A follow-up experiment showed that for consumers open to switching, ad appeals explicitly positioning the brand as the rival outperformed discount appeals among conservatives.

**Why it matters:** It reframes a competitor's activism backlash as an acquisition window, and suggests the winning message is rivalry rather than price.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Competitive-response playbooks: when a rival's stance triggers backlash, brands can test "rival appeals" against standard discounting for the segment already considering a switch — though the evidence here is from one high-profile case plus an experiment, not a scaled campaign.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Boycotts are rarely just about walking away. The research suggests defecting consumers want their replacement purchase to count as a rebuke, which is why a competitor often benefits more than the category.

**Caveats:** Single focal case (Nike) and US political ideology; experimental ad test uses stated responses rather than real purchases; retaliation findings rest partly on self-reported past behavior.

### 20. Consumers forgive AI-designed products — if they believe the brand is driven by passion, not profit

**Journal:** *Journal of Brand Management* | **Published:** 2026-08-13
**Work ID:** W7202384182 | **DOI:** 10.1057/s41262-026-00451-1

**The study:** Two online-panel experiments tested reactions to products described as AI-designed versus human-designed, using wedding jewelry and craft beer as stimuli. AI-designed products were evaluated more favorably — on perceived quality, purchase intention and willingness to pay a premium — when the brand was framed as passion-driven rather than profit-driven. Perceived authenticity of the brand extension explained the effect, with the indirect path stronger for passion-driven brands.

**Why it matters:** As brands decide whether to disclose AI involvement in design, this suggests disclosure isn't inherently damaging — what matters is whether consumers already read the brand's motives as sincere.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Before publicizing AI in the design process, brands should audit how consumers perceive their underlying motive; the findings imply craft- and mission-positioned brands may have more room to use AI openly than efficiency-positioned ones, which is a testable proposition in live product launches.
- **For general readers and consumers:** When shoppers judge an AI-made product, they're really judging why they think the company used AI — to make something better, or to cut costs.

**Caveats:** Online panel; hypothetical product scenarios rather than real purchases; only two categories (wedding jewelry, craft beer); sample size not reported in the abstract.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Several studies in this batch converge on the same consumer skill: inferring a brand's motive and discounting the message accordingly. Modest, self-effacing claims beat boastful ones because they lower skepticism and raise trust ("Modest brands outsell boastful ones," W7203440056); testimonials that read as commercially motivated don't merely fail but actively depress repurchase ("Not all social proof helps," W7216479640); AI-authored activism reads as inauthentic and leaves buyers less comfortable with their own decisions ("When AI writes your brand's social stance," W7203997372); and AI-designed products are forgiven only when the brand is already seen as passion-driven rather than profit-driven ("Consumers forgive AI-designed products," W7202384182). Even the creative-side study fits — copywriters who dislike a brand strip emotion out of the work precisely to avoid contaminating it ("Copywriters who hate your brand write duller ads," W7221130306).

**Why pitch this now:** Generative AI has made persuasive content effectively free at the same moment that disclosure regimes — AI transparency rules and crackdowns on undisclosed endorsements and fabricated reviews — are forcing brands to say out loud how their content was made and paid for, which turns motive from a hidden variable into a published one.

**Angle:** A feature on the economics of sincerity: as production costs collapse and disclosure becomes mandatory, the scarce asset is a believable reason for the brand to be speaking at all — written for CMOs and brand strategists, with enough consumer-psychology texture to carry a general-interest read.

**Potential headlines:**
- Understatement outsells superlatives, and paid-looking praise costs more than it earns
- The harder a brand tries to persuade you, the less you believe it
- Brands are about to publish how their content gets made, and not all of them will survive it

**Potential outlets:**
- **Marketing Week** — its readers argue constantly about brand trust and effectiveness metrics; evidence that modest copy beats superlatives is a direct provocation to house creative orthodoxy.
- **Digiday** — the testimonial and ambassador findings land squarely in its coverage of the paid-creator economy and where endorsement spend stops working.
- **MIT Sloan Management Review** — fits its remit on AI governance: which content categories a company should keep in human hands, and why values-led messaging is the costly exception.
- **The Atlantic** — runs consumer-psychology and AI-authenticity essays for general readers; the "we can smell the motive" thread works as cultural reporting, not just trade news.
