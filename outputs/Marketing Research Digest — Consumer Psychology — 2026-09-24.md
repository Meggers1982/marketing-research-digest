### 1. "Ready at 6:30" beats "ready in 30 minutes" — but only for short waits

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Research* | **Published:** 2026-09-15
**Work ID:** W7213274443 | **DOI:** 10.1093/jcr/ucag039

**The study:** Across eight experiments with 16,604 participants, researchers compared logically identical ways of describing a time period: a duration ("in 30 minutes") versus start-and-end clock times or calendar dates ("at 6:30 PM"). Short periods felt longer when expressed as a duration, while the pattern flipped for longer periods, which felt longer when expressed as start-and-end points. The authors attribute this to duration frames triggering compressed, diminishing-sensitivity judgments, whereas clock and calendar frames anchor time in structured categories.

**Why it matters:** Wait times, delivery windows and service estimates are communicated constantly, and this work suggests the wording alone — with no change in the actual wait — shifts how long it feels and whether consumers pay to skip it.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** An easy, near-zero-cost A/B test for any business that quotes waits: restaurants, delivery apps, call centers and repair services could try switching short estimates to clock times and long ones to durations, and watch abandonment, upgrade purchases and satisfaction. The flip point matters — a blanket rule would backfire on longer horizons.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A reminder that your sense of how long something will take is being nudged by phrasing. If "in 30 minutes" makes you reach for the paid express option, reframing it in your head as "done by 6:30" may take the sting out.

**Caveats:** Experiments rather than field deployments; the abstract does not specify samples or countries, and many such studies rely on online panels, so effects on real waiting behavior at scale remain to be shown.

### 2. Ask for $25 toward a $1,000 goal: round-number math makes people give

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-18
**Work ID:** W7213625881 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00222429261492622

**The study:** Seven studies — including field experiments, secondary campaign data and lab experiments — tested how the relationship between a fundraising campaign's overall goal and its suggested donation amount affects giving. People were more likely to contribute when the goal divided cleanly by the requested amount (for example, $25 into $1,000) than when it did not. The mechanism was perceived clarity: easy division made donors see exactly how their gift moved the total, which boosted motivation to help reach the collective goal.

**Why it matters:** Nonprofits and crowdfunding platforms set goal amounts and suggested donation tiers independently; this shows the arithmetic relationship between the two numbers is itself a lever on conversion.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Fundraisers and platforms can test pairing goal and ask amounts that divide evenly, and the same logic may extend to loyalty points, savings targets and step or fitness challenges where a total and an increment are shown together. Worth checking whether the effect survives once donors see multiple suggested tiers at once.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Your generosity may be partly a math reflex: when you can instantly see that your $25 is one-fortieth of the goal, giving feels more meaningful than when the numbers don't line up neatly.

**Caveats:** Mix of field, archival and lab studies strengthens the case, but the abstract gives no sample sizes, countries or effect magnitudes; single domain (charitable giving).

### 3. Infinite scroll sells — and then gets returned

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing Research* | **Published:** 2026-09-23
**Work ID:** W7214126722 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00222437261494550

**The study:** Five studies using real websites compared infinite-scrolling and paginated product listings under free-return policies. Shoppers browsing infinite-scroll layouts were more likely to return what they bought. The reason: endless scrolling reduces the sense of navigational control, which blocks "choice closure" — the feeling that the decision is settled — leaving buyers second-guessing afterward. Adding navigational aids such as sequence numbers, giving affirmative feedback, or making return costs salient reduced the effect.

**Why it matters:** Returns are a major drag on e-commerce margins, and this points to an interface design choice — usually optimized for engagement and browsing time — as a hidden driver of them.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Retailers should look at return rates split by layout and device, and test cheap fixes such as item counters, progress indicators or post-purchase confirmation messaging before assuming the problem is product fit or sizing. The tension is real: the layout that maximizes browsing may be the one that maximizes reverse logistics costs.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If you often feel unsure about an online purchase after clicking buy, the endless feed may be to blame — you never reached the end, so the decision never felt finished. Pausing to review a short shortlist before checkout can restore that sense of closure.

**Caveats:** Return intentions were the outcome in at least part of the work rather than observed returns; effects established in study settings using real websites, not a deployed retailer field test; free-return context only.

### 4. More photos, fewer bookings: why extra visuals hurt experience sales

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing* | **Published:** 2026-09-01
**Work ID:** W7213493969 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretai.2026.09.002

**The study:** Five studies, including a real newsletter campaign, a field test at a zoo using a trailer video, and a choice study of hotel options, found that consumers were less likely to buy an experiential product — vacations, dining, attractions — when it was promoted with many images rather than few. The effect disappeared when the extensive image set was visually heterogeneous, suggesting redundancy rather than volume is the problem. In an actual choice task, participants preferred the hotel option shown with fewer images.

**Why it matters:** Travel, hospitality and leisure marketers have been adding ever more photos and video to listings on the assumption that more information helps; this suggests a large, similar-looking gallery can suppress demand.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Test trimming image galleries on experiential listings, or curating for variety rather than volume — and measure conversion, not just time on page. Worth checking whether the same logic applies to user-generated photo floods on review and booking platforms.
- **For general readers and consumers:** When a hotel or tour listing drowns you in near-identical pictures, your enthusiasm can quietly deflate — a useful thing to notice before concluding a place "just didn't look appealing."

**Caveats:** Experiential products only; mix of field and lab work but sample sizes and countries not specified in the abstract; effect may depend on the specific images used.

### 5. Virtual influencers get more traction when they're pictured hanging out with real people

**Journal:** *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* | **Published:** 2026-08-10
**Work ID:** W4318015176 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11747-026-01185-x

**The study:** Combining automated image and text analysis of thousands of social media posts with controlled experiments, researchers tested whether computer-generated influencers perform better when shown in photos interacting socially with humans. Posts showing genuine social ties — not just a human in frame — drew more engagement and choice, because the interaction made the virtual figure seem more human and therefore more trustworthy. The boost was strongest for less humanlike avatars, and disappeared when the companion was merely present, was another virtual entity, or when the poster was a real human influencer.

**Why it matters:** Brands are shifting budget from human to virtual influencers; this gives a concrete, testable content rule for making CGI personas credible rather than uncanny.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Content teams running virtual personas can test staging genuine-looking social interactions with real people, especially for stylized or cartoonish avatars — and note that the same tactic offers no lift for human influencers, so it is not a universal engagement hack.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It reveals something about how trust is built: we extend credibility to a digital character mainly when we see other humans apparently treating it as one of them.

**Caveats:** Observational social media analysis cannot fully rule out confounds in what brands choose to post; experimental components likely used online panels; platform and category scope not specified in the abstract.

### 6. Tearjerker or revenge flick? Culture predicts which mixed emotions sell

**Journal:** *Psychology and Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-17
**Work ID:** W7213511827 | **DOI:** 10.1002/mar.70269

**The study:** Researchers tested whether the emotional cocktails audiences seek out track cultural orientation, combining eight years of box office revenue data (2015–2022) with six experiments and an internal meta-analysis. They found that collectivist-oriented consumers process affection-plus-sadness blends more fluently and choose content signaling them, while individualist-oriented consumers do the same for pride-plus-anger blends. The earlier assumption that mixed emotional appeals simply work better on collectivists did not hold; what mattered was whether the specific emotional mix matched self-transcendence versus self-enhancement values.

**Why it matters:** Streaming services, publishers and advertisers routinely tag content by emotion; this suggests those tags should be matched to viewers' cultural orientation rather than applied universally.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** A test worth running: instead of one emotional creative per market, build parallel versions of the same story beat — a moving reunion versus a satisfying comeuppance — and see whether matching to audience cultural orientation lifts click-through on recommendations and trailers. The box office component gives real-market support, but the mechanism evidence comes from experiments, so treat personalization gains as a hypothesis to validate in your own recommender.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Why one friend cues up a good cry while another wants to watch the villain get what's coming: the study suggests these aren't just personality quirks but reflect deeper cultural values about self-transcendence versus self-enhancement.

**Caveats:** Experimental components rely on choices among content options rather than paid viewing; cultural orientation is measured at the individual level, which can flatten within-country variation; box office analysis is correlational.

### 7. Consumers will pay more for low-carbon milk — but a big chunk of them dislike 'organic'

**Journal:** *Food Quality and Preference* | **Published:** 2026-09-18
**Work ID:** W7213546298 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.foodqual.2026.106117

**The study:** A choice experiment fielded through an online survey across four US regions asked regular grocery milk buyers to choose among gallons varying on organic production, greenhouse gas emissions reduction, nutrient runoff reduction and price. Preferences split sharply: a higher-socioeconomic, environmentally engaged segment showed substantially higher willingness to pay for both organic and emissions reductions, while a lower-socioeconomic, less environmentally engaged segment actually derived negative utility from the organic label. Education was the main driver of the organic premium; environmental attitudes drove the premium for emissions reductions.

**Why it matters:** As US dairy pledges Net Zero by 2050 and passes on higher production costs, the finding says climate claims and organic claims are not interchangeable — and that organic labeling can actively repel part of the market.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you're building a sustainability story for a staple category, test emissions and runoff claims separately from organic certification; they appeal to different buyers through different mechanisms, and bundling them may cost you volume among price-sensitive shoppers who read 'organic' as a negative signal.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Green labels on everyday groceries aren't read the same way by everyone — for some shoppers 'organic' signals value for money lost, not virtue gained, which helps explain why sustainable products stall outside affluent zip codes.

**Caveats:** Hypothetical choice experiment with stated preferences rather than observed purchases; online survey sample; single country and single category (milk); willingness-to-pay estimates from choice experiments typically overstate real market behavior.

### 8. Sticky seats beat beer lists: what actually drives the choice of pub

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing Management* | **Published:** 2026-09-22
**Work ID:** W7172190947 | **DOI:** 10.1080/0267257x.2026.2733461

**The study:** Using Q-methodology — a mixed-methods technique for mapping how people rank competing priorities — researchers examined the trade-offs consumers make between product, physical and service features when choosing a pub, framed through foraging theory. Two psychographic segments emerged: Traditional Pint Loyalists and Inclusive Drinkers. Cleanliness and physical environment factors surfaced as decisive alongside drink range, and the analysis points to the growing weight of women, families and non-beer drinkers in a market still segmented by dated demographic stereotypes.

**Why it matters:** Hospitality operators tend to compete on drinks range and price; this suggests the physical environment and inclusivity of the space are doing more segmentation work than the product lineup.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Worth auditing whether your venue's investment is going where choice is actually made — a cheap fix to seating, cleanliness and atmosphere may move more traffic than another tap line. The segments here are exploratory, so treat them as hypotheses to validate with your own footfall and loyalty data before rebuilding a targeting strategy.
- **For general readers and consumers:** People tell themselves they pick a pub for the beer, but the study suggests they're really foraging for a comfortable environment — and the archetypal pub regular is increasingly not a man with a pint.

**Caveats:** Q-methodology uses small purposive samples and yields subjective viewpoint patterns, not population estimates; single country (UK) and single category; stated preferences rather than observed venue choice.

### 9. Feeling local makes people more creative — and pride is the reason

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-09
**Work ID:** W7202051364 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00222429261479623

**The study:** Across a field study, six controlled experiments, secondary data from a large crowdsourcing platform, and manager interviews, researchers tested how making a person's local versus global identity salient affects creative output. Consumers primed toward a local identity produced more creative ideas than those primed toward a global identity, and group-based pride was the mechanism: boosting pride externally raised creativity among globally primed participants, while dampening pride lowered it among locally primed ones.

**Why it matters:** Companies running crowdsourcing contests, co-creation programs, and ideation platforms can shift the quality of submissions simply by changing the cultural framing of the brief.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Test whether local framing — hometown references, regional imagery, community language — lifts the creativity of ideas submitted to your co-creation or contest platform, and whether pride-evoking copy does the same work for globally framed campaigns. The multi-method evidence including field and platform data is stronger than lab-only work, but replication in your own category is still warranted.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Where you feel you belong may quietly change how inventive you are. Reminders of your hometown or region appear to spark pride, and pride seems to unlock more original thinking than reminders of being a global citizen.

**Caveats:** Mechanism evidence comes mainly from controlled experiments; the abstract does not specify sample sizes, countries, or the crowdsourcing platform, so generalizability across cultures and categories is unverified.

### 10. Naming a government research institute as co-developer makes shoppers warmer to lab-grown and insect protein

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-09-19
**Work ID:** W7213674865 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105122

**The study:** Four experiments with Chinese consumers tested whether labeling an alternative-protein product as co-developed with an official agricultural research institute changed acceptance. The cue generally raised acceptance of plant-based, cultured-meat, and insect-based products by reducing perceived risk; the effect was strongest among food-neophobic consumers and disappeared entirely when trust in the named institution was low.

**Why it matters:** Alternative protein brands struggle with unfamiliarity at shelf; an institutional co-development credential is a cheap packaging-level lever that may do more than taste or sustainability claims for the most hesitant shoppers.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Consider testing institutional co-development credentials on-pack and in e-commerce product pages for unfamiliar food innovations — but audit trust in the named institution first, since the study found a low-trust endorser delivers nothing. Note these are experiments, not shelf tests.
- **For general readers and consumers:** When faced with food you find strange, you may be reacting less to the food than to who vouches for it. The finding shows how much a trusted third-party name can quietly lower your sense of risk.

**Caveats:** All four studies used Chinese consumers, where trust in state agricultural institutions may differ sharply from other markets; experiments used product evaluations rather than real purchases, and sample sizes are not reported in the abstract.

### 11. Older celebrities get punished for sponsored posts; younger ones get rewarded

**Journal:** *Psychology and Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-17
**Work ID:** W7213468681 | **DOI:** 10.1002/mar.70268

**The study:** Researchers analyzed 22,619 Instagram-style posts from 286 celebrities, ran sentiment analysis on 67,778 follower comments, and conducted a 2×2 experiment (N=290). Older celebrities drew lower engagement and less positive emotion on sponsored posts relative to their own non-sponsored content, while younger celebrities saw the reverse — higher engagement and warmer sentiment on sponsored content. The experiment traced this to a chain of reduced perceived interest fit and weaker social identification.

**Why it matters:** Brands paying premiums for established, older celebrity partners may be buying reach that converts poorly into engagement precisely when the content is commercial.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit engagement on sponsored versus organic posts by talent age before renewing influencer contracts, and test whether framing older talent's paid content around genuine personal interest closes the fit gap. The observational data is large, but causality rests on a single modest experiment.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Audiences appear to hold an unspoken stereotype about who is 'supposed' to be selling things online — and react coolly when an older famous face does the pitching.

**Caveats:** The experiment (N=290) is small relative to its 2×2 design; platform and country not specified in the abstract; observational engagement data cannot rule out differences in follower composition, category, or posting style across celebrity ages.

### 12. Emotional AI in customer service only works on customers who want a relationship with your brand

**Journal:** *Asia Pacific Journal of Marketing and Logistics* | **Published:** 2026-08-28
**Work ID:** W7204588967 | **DOI:** 10.1108/apjml-12-2025-2751

**The study:** Three between-subjects experiments with a combined 677 participants tested how emotionally expressive AI customer service agents affect satisfaction and continuance intention. For consumers with communal brand relationship norms — those who see the brand as a relationship partner — high emotional expressiveness raised satisfaction; for exchange-oriented consumers who just want the transaction done, it made no difference. The effect ran through expectation confirmation and only emerged when consumers attributed experiential 'mind' to the AI.

**Why it matters:** Service teams are rolling out warmer, more emotive chatbots as a blanket upgrade; the evidence suggests the benefit is concentrated in one customer segment and absent in another.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Rather than standardizing chatbot warmth, test segmented emotional calibration — expressive tone for relationship-oriented customers, efficient and neutral for transactional ones — and watch whether framing the AI as capable of feeling amplifies or backfires.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If a chatbot's sympathetic language leaves you cold, that may say less about the bot than about what you want from the brand: some people want a friend, most just want the problem solved.

**Caveats:** Experimental scenarios rather than real service encounters; stated satisfaction and intentions rather than observed retention; sample recruitment and country not specified in the abstract.

### 13. Put a face in the first five seconds and fewer people watch your ad

**Journal:** *Marketing Letters* | **Published:** 2026-09-14
**Work ID:** W7212428236 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11002-026-09839-0

**The study:** Researchers analyzed 3,498 skippable digital video ads running across 685 channels and 23 product categories, measuring how much of the pre-skip portion of each ad contained human faces. Ads with less facial presence before the skip button appeared earned more qualified video views. The effect was driven by utilitarian products; for hedonic products, facial presence had no significant relationship with views.

**Why it matters:** The opening seconds of a skippable ad are the most expensive real estate in digital video, and conventional creative wisdom that "faces stop the scroll" may be backwards in that window, at least for practical, information-driven products.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Media and creative teams can treat the pre-skip window as its own testable unit: run A/B variants of the same spot with and without a human face in the first five seconds, split by whether the product is functional or pleasure-driven. This is correlational field data rather than a controlled test, so it argues for experimentation, not a rule.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It hints at what actually holds our attention when we hover over the skip button: for something we're evaluating on function, an on-screen person may feel like a delay before the information, while product or visual imagery gets to the point faster.

**Caveats:** Observational data from one platform environment, not a randomized experiment; view counts are a proxy for engagement and can be shaped by media buying, targeting and channel factors not fully controlled; causality cannot be established.

### 14. The busier people feel, the more they want the games of their childhood

**Journal:** *European Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-17
**Work ID:** W7203605101 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ejm-10-2024-0887

**The study:** Across five studies — one preregistered quasi-field study and four controlled experiments — researchers manipulated or measured perceived busyness and then tested preferences for retro versus modern video games. Feeling busy increased preference for retro games, and the mechanism was emotional instability: busyness made people feel emotionally unsettled, and nostalgic games served as a stabilizer. The effect disappeared among participants who felt powerful, for whom the compensatory motive was muted.

**Why it matters:** It identifies a specific, timeable emotional state — busyness — as a driver of nostalgic consumption, suggesting nostalgia marketing works best when aimed at moments of overload rather than broadcast year-round.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Game publishers and any brand with heritage products could test nostalgia-forward messaging timed to high-pressure periods — tax season, quarter close, exam weeks, holiday crunch — and placed in productivity-adjacent contexts, while emphasizing calm and comfort rather than novelty. These are lab and quasi-field results, so the timing hypothesis needs a live campaign test.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For readers, it reframes the pull of old games, old music and old shows: reaching for the familiar when life speeds up isn't avoidance so much as a low-cost way of steadying yourself.

**Caveats:** Mostly controlled experiments with manipulated mindsets rather than observed purchases; preference measures rather than actual play or spending; single category (video games).

### 15. Rivalry ads split the crowd by gender: women in the stands react differently than men

**Journal:** *European Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-21
**Work ID:** W7203929993 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ejm-05-2023-0367

**The study:** Two experiments with sports consumers (N=406 and N=327) tested reactions to sponsors that explicitly invoked a city rivalry in their activation, varying the fan's own team allegiance, level of team identification, and gender. Women with moderate to strong team identification responded positively when their own team's sponsor referenced the rivalry, and negatively when the rival team's sponsor did; for men the pattern reversed. The authors conclude rivalry leveraging is a double-edged sword with genuinely ambivalent outcomes.

**Why it matters:** Rivalry-based sponsorship activation is a staple of sports marketing, and this suggests it can quietly alienate a large segment of the very fan base it is meant to excite.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Sponsors planning rivalry creative could pretest by gender and identification strength before launch, and consider whether the upside among one segment offsets backlash in another — especially as women's share of sports audiences grows. Panel experiments around a single rivalry indicate a risk to check, not a universal gender rule.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It shows how tribal marketing works on fans: the same needling ad can read as fun banter or as a personal slight depending on who you are and how much the team means to you.

**Caveats:** Scenario-based experiments rather than real sponsorship exposure; one sponsorship case in one country, in a region where the rivalry extends beyond sport; self-reported responses rather than purchase behavior.

### 16. Your AI rollout will live or die with the middle manager

**Journal:** *Journal of Service Research* | **Published:** 2026-07-28
**Work ID:** W7171543795 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10946705261464323

**The study:** Researchers used a theories-in-use approach with 51 frontline managers and top executives charged with implementing AI systems in customer-facing operations, mapping their mental models. A manager's personal AI readiness shaped how the system was actually integrated — full automation, augmentation of staff, or human–AI collaboration — and those choices had long-lasting consequences for managers, employees and customers. Two enablers of readiness emerged (AI strategic and task alignment) plus three management strategies: championing AI engagement, building AI literacy, and activating AI empowerment.

**Why it matters:** It locates the main bottleneck in frontline AI investment not in the technology or in staff resistance but in the readiness of the managers translating strategy into practice.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Service leaders can audit manager-level AI readiness before deployment and check whether the tool is aligned to specific frontline tasks, rather than assuming a top-down mandate will be implemented as designed. Qualitative findings here generate hypotheses for firms to measure, not benchmarks.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It explains why customer experience with the same chatbot or AI-assisted service can vary so much between branches or teams of the same company: local managers are making very different calls about what the machine is allowed to do.

**Caveats:** Qualitative interview study with no quantitative outcome data; 51 informants; findings reflect managers' accounts and mental models rather than observed implementation results.

### 17. Ask shoppers to type their opinion and you get emotion; ask them to talk and you get analysis

**Journal:** *Food Quality and Preference* | **Published:** 2026-09-19
**Work ID:** W7213621009 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.foodqual.2026.106115

**The study:** Participants tasted three chocolates and three plant-based drinks and described them in open-ended responses, either written or spoken, with the text analyzed using LIWC2015. Liking scores for chocolate were consistent across modalities, but the language was not: written descriptions carried more emotional and sensory-perceptual words and more complex vocabulary, while spoken ones were longer and heavier on cognitive and function words. Chocolate drew more affective language overall; plant-based drinks drew more analytical, perceptual vocabulary.

**Why it matters:** How you collect open-ended consumer feedback can change the emotional signal you extract from it, which matters for any team mining verbatims, interviews or voice data for insight.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Insight teams can check whether the emotional tone in their verbatim data is a property of the product or an artifact of the collection method — and consider running written and spoken modes in parallel when emotion is the thing being measured.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It also says something about the plant-based category: people describe dairy alternatives in the language of scrutiny and analysis, while chocolate gets the language of feeling.

**Caveats:** Exploratory design; two categories and six products only; sample size and country not specified in the abstract; dictionary-based text analysis, not validated emotion measurement.

### 18. The wider your product line, the less your advertising works — and the more word-of-mouth does

**Journal:** *Journal of Interactive Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-06
**Work ID:** W7210276982 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10949968261488312

**The study:** Researchers analyzed sales, advertising and online word-of-mouth data from China's automobile market to see how the breadth of a carmaker's product line changes what drives sales. As a brand's line-up broadened, the sales impact of its own advertising fell while the impact of consumer online word-of-mouth rose. Advertising and word-of-mouth generally acted as substitutes, but that substitution weakened as product lines widened, and the shift was explained by "mismatch cost" — the difficulty of pairing the right buyer with the right model.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the marketing budget mix should shift with portfolio strategy: broad, complex line-ups may be better served by cultivating user-generated content and review ecosystems than by buying more paid media.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit whether media spend is being allocated as though every product in the portfolio responds the same way; where the line-up is broad and choices are hard to match, test reallocating budget toward review seeding, owner content and community platforms and measure incremental sales rather than reach.
- **For general readers and consumers:** When a brand sells twenty variants of the same thing, ads can't tell you which one fits you — which is exactly why shoppers lean on other buyers' reviews, and why those reviews carry more weight in crowded categories.

**Caveats:** Observational market data from a single country and a single category (Chinese autos); correlational moderation rather than an experiment, so causal interpretation depends on the modeling assumptions.

### 19. Why ambitious people don't want AI to do it for them

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Behaviour* | **Published:** 2026-08-28
**Work ID:** W7204561075 | **DOI:** 10.1002/cb.70234

**The study:** Across a field study and four experiments, researchers tested how consumers' pursuit of an "ideal self" affects their appetite for AI products that take over tasks. People with stronger ideal-self pursuit showed lower preference and lower willingness to pay for substitutional AI — AI that replaces effort in domains tied to self-improvement — an effect driven by self-improvement motivation and amplified when people felt the product threatened their sense of self. Crucially, when the same AI was positioned as supporting rather than replacing the user's effort, ideal-self pursuit flipped to increase preference.

**Why it matters:** It identifies a positioning lever that costs nothing to change: framing AI as a coach or assistant rather than a substitute can convert the very customers most likely to reject automation.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Test "AI that helps you get better" copy against "AI that does it for you" in categories tied to skill and self-development — fitness, learning, cooking, creative tools — and watch willingness-to-pay, not just click-through; the efficiency pitch may be actively repelling your most motivated segment.
- **For general readers and consumers:** There's a reason handing your workout plan, your writing or your cooking to an algorithm feels vaguely like cheating: for tasks tied to who we want to become, the effort is part of the point.

**Caveats:** Mostly experimental with stated preference and willingness-to-pay measures rather than observed purchases; one field study included but its scale and setting are not specified in the abstract.

### 20. Make people suffer a little: painful charity events boost perceived authenticity of cause marketing

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Behaviour* | **Published:** 2026-09-02
**Work ID:** W7204997257 | **DOI:** 10.1002/cb.70236

**The study:** Two experiments tested how the stated motive behind a cause-related marketing program interacts with how much effort it demands of consumers. A public-serving motive paired with an effortful event (a marathon) produced the highest perceived authenticity and support compared with self-serving motives or low-effort events; authenticity and perceived meaningfulness sequentially explained effects on brand word-of-mouth, event word-of-mouth and donations. The effect was strongest among participants who felt a personal calling toward the cause (childhood starvation).

**Why it matters:** It suggests brands running cause campaigns may get more credibility from asking consumers to do something hard than from making participation frictionless.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Where cause programs have been optimized for ease — round-up-at-checkout, one-tap donations — consider testing an effortful participation tier alongside it, and be explicit about public-serving rather than brand-serving motives in the messaging.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The "martyrdom effect" in action: we judge a charity effort as more sincere, and give more, when it costs us sweat rather than just a click.

**Caveats:** Two scenario-based experiments measuring intentions and stated supporting behaviors rather than actual donations; single cause issue; sample details and country not specified in the abstract.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Several studies in this batch converge on the same uncomfortable finding: the maximalist, frictionless digital playbook -- endless feeds, bigger galleries, faster asks, faces up front -- is suppressing the very conversions it was built to drive, while deliberate friction and finishability restore commitment. "Infinite scroll sells — and then gets returned" (W7214126722) ties layout choice to return rates via blocked choice closure; "More photos, fewer bookings" (W7213493969) shows redundant image volume depressing experiential sales; "Put a face in the first five seconds and fewer people watch your ad" (W7212428236) finds the standard attention-grabbing opener backfiring for utilitarian products; and on the other side of the ledger, "Make people suffer a little" (W7204997257) and "Why ambitious people don't want AI to do it for them" (W7204561075) show that effort demanded of the consumer raises perceived authenticity and willingness to pay. "Ready at 6:30" beats "ready in 30 minutes" (W7213274443) and the round-number donation study (W7213625881) round it out: what moves people is a sense of a bounded, legible task, not a smoother one.

**Why pitch this now:** Retailers are under sustained margin pressure from returns and reverse logistics, and 2026's AI-assisted shopping and recommendation interfaces are being designed on the same engagement-maximizing assumptions these studies undercut -- which makes "engagement versus completion" a live budgeting fight inside marketing and e-commerce teams right now.

**Angle:** A reported feature on the quiet unbundling of engagement from conversion -- how design and creative conventions optimized for time-on-page and scroll depth are now measurably working against sales, returns, and credibility -- written for CMOs, e-commerce leads, and design teams, with a consumer-facing throughline about why shopping online increasingly feels unfinished.

**Potential headlines:**
- Endless scroll, bigger galleries, more returns
- Making shopping harder is starting to look like a growth strategy
- The design choices quietly padding your return rate
  
**Potential outlets:**
- **Retail Dive** -- returns economics and interface design are core beats, and the infinite-scroll finding maps directly onto reverse-logistics cost stories they already track.
- **Digiday** -- the pre-skip creative finding and the engagement-versus-conversion tension speak straight to platform and media-buying audiences.
- **Marketing Week** -- has an appetite for evidence-led challenges to received creative wisdom, and the "less is more" thread cuts across creative, media, and CX.
- **Fast Company** -- the design and UX framing (why interfaces that never end leave buyers second-guessing) fits its business-of-design coverage for a broader readership.
