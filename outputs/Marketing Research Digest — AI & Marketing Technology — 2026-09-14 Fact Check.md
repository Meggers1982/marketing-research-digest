### Study 2: The people most likely to be rejected are the most likely to walk away from an algorithm
**Work ID:** W7204140072 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Design detail not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Across a series of experiments, researchers examined how consumers applying for loans, insurance, and similar benefits respond to being judged by an algorithm versus a human."
- **Abstract says:** "This research explores how consumers react to this practice, with the goal of understanding what leads some consumers to react more positively or negatively to being evaluated by an algorithm than others."
- **Problem:** The abstract never states that the evidence comes from experiments, nor how many studies were run. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Soften to 'In a series of studies' or note explicitly that the abstract does not describe the method."

**Issue B. Headline conflates perceived and actual applicant strength** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The people most likely to be rejected are the most likely to walk away from an algorithm"
- **Abstract says:** "applicant strength (i.e., how strong an applicant believes their case is based on the information they have about their standing)"
- **Problem:** The construct is belief about one's own case, not objective likelihood of rejection; the headline implies the latter.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to 'People who think their case is weak are the most likely to walk away from an algorithm.'"

**Issue C. Extension to employers** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "with fairness and access implications for lenders, insurers and employers"
- **Abstract says:** "consumers applying for access to valued services, opportunities, or benefits (e.g., loans, insurance)"
- **Problem:** Employment screening is not among the contexts named in the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "Limit the list to lenders, insurers and benefit providers, or flag employment as a plausible extension."

### Study 3: Customers who start talking about the past are about to leave you
**Work ID:** W7204117449 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Generalization beyond B2B data** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "It holds for million-dollar contracts and probably for more than that."
- **Abstract says:** "in a customer's collective communication prior to an RFP decision, a 1% increase in past focus raises the odds of a multimillion-dollar RFP defection by 8.4%"
- **Problem:** The research is confined to B2B customer language and RFP defection; extending the pattern to personal relationships is speculation the abstract does not support.
- **Suggested fix:** "Drop the extension or explicitly label it as speculation outside the study's scope."

### Study 4: The most damaging bad review is the one that says 'they could have just...'
**Work ID:** W7203674935 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Compensation effect overstated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "offering compensation neutralized the damage and could even produce incremental profit"
- **Abstract says:** "Providing compensation in response to negative reviews containing UCEs mitigates their detrimental effect on sales and can even generate incremental profits for firms."
- **Problem:** 'Mitigates' means reduces, not eliminates; 'neutralized' implies the damage was fully offset.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to 'offering compensation reduced the damage'."

**Issue B. Unsupported advice to readers** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "and as a reader, that's the review worth trusting"
- **Abstract says:** "UCEs signal the argument quality of negative reviews, which in turn deters prospective customers from making purchase decisions"
- **Problem:** The study shows such reviews are perceived as higher-quality arguments and deter purchase; it does not test whether they are more accurate or more trustworthy.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as 'that's the review other shoppers take most seriously'."

### Study 5: Explaining a disappointing refund works better when a bot does it
**Work ID:** W7204096810 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Conditional AI advantage stated as a rule** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Explaining a disappointing refund works better when a bot does it"
- **Abstract says:** "explanations provided by AI service agents can be more impactful than those delivered by human service agents"
- **Problem:** The abstract frames the AI advantage as possible under certain contingencies ('can be'), not as a general superiority; the headline and the 'let the chatbot do the explaining' line read as an established rule.
- **Suggested fix:** "Hedge the headline, e.g. 'Explaining a disappointing refund can work better when a bot does it'."

**Issue B. 'Experiments' not specified** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Across three main and three supplementary experiments spanning multiple service industries"
- **Abstract says:** "Across three main studies and three supplementary studies spanning multiple service industries"
- **Problem:** The abstract says 'studies'; the design is not identified as experimental.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use 'studies' unless the experimental design can be confirmed."

### Study 10: In luxury retail, AI doesn't sell by itself — it only works if it signals exclusivity
**Work ID:** W7212369437 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline narrows four channels to one** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "In luxury retail, AI doesn't sell by itself — it only works if it signals exclusivity"
- **Abstract says:** "their effects operate through these four signaling dimensions ... exclusivity, authenticity, innovation, and responsiveness"

### Study 11: Giving ad creatives AI isn't enough — teaching them to prompt is what moved the needle
**Work ID:** W7204938023 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Untrained-AI comparison not stated in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "simply handing people the tool produced no measurable idea-volume gain"
- **Abstract says:** "Participants who used GenAI after training generated significantly more ideas than those in the other conditions, although perceived creativity did not differ."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports only that the trained group beat the other two conditions; it does not report a direct comparison between the unguided GenAI group and the no-GenAI group, so 'no measurable gain' from access alone is an inference.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to 'only the trained group significantly out-produced the others; the abstract does not report whether unguided AI use beat no AI at all.'"

### Study 12: Chatbots that remember you sell better — until customers think about what else they remember
**Work ID:** W7212274308 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Measured perception described as a message to users** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Merely telling people they were generally empowered over privacy did not help; a concrete data-control mechanism did"
- **Abstract says:** "In Study 2, baseline perception of privacy empowerment was insufficient to mitigate privacy concerns associated with LTM; however, within a memory‐enabled condition, introducing an explicit user data‐control mechanism in Study 3 increased perceived privacy empowerment..."
- **Problem:** Study 2 assessed consumers' pre-existing (baseline) sense of privacy empowerment; it did not test 'telling people' they were empowered. Presenting it as a communications manipulation misstates the design and could lead managers to think reassurance messaging was tested and failed.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to 'consumers' general baseline sense of privacy empowerment was not enough to offset the concerns; an explicit data-control mechanism was.'"

**Issue B. Headline implies sales outcomes** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Chatbots that remember you sell better"
- **Abstract says:** "LTM significantly increased satisfaction and continuance intention, while its effect on word‐of‐mouth (WoM) intention was primarily indirect."
- **Problem:** The measured outcomes were satisfaction, continuance intention and WoM intention in scenario experiments — no sales or purchase measure.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe the headline around satisfaction and intention to keep using the service rather than selling."

### Study 17: AI nudges and social proof track with compulsive shopping — but chatbots pointed the other way
**Work ID:** W7207605884 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Scarcity cue not among the studied design features** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "scarcity and social-proof nudges look most implicated"
- **Abstract says:** "Artificial intelligence–driven persuasive design, spanning personalisation, social influence cues, nudging, hedonic features and conversational tools... Social influence and nudging showed the strongest associations with addictive behaviour"
- **Problem:** The abstract's design dimensions are personalisation, social influence cues, nudging, hedonic features and conversational tools; scarcity is not one of them, so attributing the finding to scarcity cues (and the 'ten people are viewing this' example) adds a mechanic the study did not isolate.
- **Suggested fix:** "Refer to 'social influence cues and nudging' rather than scarcity, or label the scarcity example explicitly as illustrative."

### Study 19: Chatbots that use your name feel more sincere — until they use it too much
**Work ID:** W7204492734 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Inverted-U and 'manipulative' reading not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the evidence suggests an inverted-U where a few uses signal sincerity and too many read as manipulative"
- **Abstract says:** "we identify a few boundary conditions for the positive effects of name‐based personalization, namely chatbot performance and perceived overuse of name‐based personalization."
- **Problem:** The abstract names perceived overuse as a boundary condition but does not report a curvilinear (inverted-U) dose-response function or that overuse is read as manipulative.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say the benefit disappears when consumers perceive the name is overused, without asserting an inverted-U curve or a specific attribution of manipulation."

### Study 20: 40 million images from the biggest open-source AI image platform show a sharp tilt toward NSFW content and real people's likenesses
**Work ID:** W4416014100 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Likeness models labelled as LoRAs and 'explicitly built'** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "a substantial number of personalization adapters (LoRAs) explicitly built to mimic identifiable real individuals"
- **Abstract says:** "we find a disproportionate rise in not-safe-for-work (NSFW) content and a significant number of models intended to mimic real individuals."
- **Problem:** The abstract says 'models intended to mimic real individuals' — it does not specify that these are LoRA adapters or that intent was explicitly declared.
- **Suggested fix:** "Write 'a significant number of models intended to mimic real individuals', noting LoRA only as the general personalization method the paper discusses."

**Issue B. Unsupported comparative harm claim** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "the AI image tools most people never see are the ones doing the most damage"
- **Abstract says:** "This study presents an exploratory sociotechnical analysis of CivitAI... they also facilitate harmful practices, including the generation of non-consensual deepfakes and the amplification of misogynistic or hypersexualized content."
- **Problem:** The study is a descriptive analysis of one platform and makes no comparison of harm levels across open and closed AI image ecosystems, so 'doing the most damage' is an editorial leap.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as 'open model repositories are a largely unmonitored source of non-consensual and hypersexualized imagery', without ranking harm against other ecosystems."

**Issue C. 'Quantifies how easily' overstates the analysis** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the study quantifies how easily anyone's face can be turned into a shareable model"
- **Abstract says:** "Drawing on a dataset of more than 40 million user-generated images and over 230,000 models, we find... a significant number of models intended to mimic real individuals."
- **Problem:** The paper counts how many likeness-mimicking models exist; it does not measure the ease or effort of creating one.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say the study documents how many models mimicking real individuals are circulating, rather than how easy they are to make."
