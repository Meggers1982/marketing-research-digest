### 1. When the bank gave loan officers an AI copilot, approvals fell — and so did customer satisfaction

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing Research* | **Published:** 2026-08-21
**Work ID:** W7203942746 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00222437261483524

**The study:** Researchers studied a bank that rolled out a machine-learning tool to advise frontline employees handling mortgage applications, a nonroutine judgment task, using a quasi-experimental design comparing AI-assisted and unassisted staff. AI-assisted employees became measurably more conservative: mortgage approvals dropped, defaults dropped, and customer satisfaction with those employees fell. Textual analysis, a staff survey, and a preregistered experiment traced the internal cost to perceived rigidity — AI-generated recommendations read as inflexible and closed to alternatives, which lowered employees' satisfaction with the guidance and, through that, their sense of job autonomy, regardless of how helpful they judged the advice to be.

**Why it matters:** It is rare field evidence that AI assistance on judgment-heavy frontline tasks can simultaneously shrink the customer base, dent satisfaction, and demoralize staff — even while improving a risk metric like defaults. The lesson is that AI deployment is a task-fit decision, not a blanket productivity upgrade.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit where AI advice is being injected into nonroutine, discretionary work and instrument both sides of the ledger: not just risk and efficiency gains but approval rates, customer satisfaction with staff, and employee autonomy. Consider testing advice framed as options rather than a single rigid recommendation, and watch for the possibility that your AI is quietly making your frontline risk-averse.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If your mortgage or loan application is being screened with algorithmic help, the answer may be more cautious than a human would have given on their own — a reminder that 'computer says no' can reflect the tool's conservatism as much as your finances.

**Caveats:** Single bank in one country and one category (mortgages); the quasi-experimental design limits causal certainty relative to a randomized rollout, though it is supported by a preregistered experiment; short observation horizon for downstream effects.

### 2. The people most likely to be rejected are the most likely to walk away from an algorithm

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Research* | **Published:** 2026-08-23
**Work ID:** W7204140072 | **DOI:** 10.1093/jcr/ucag033

**The study:** Across a series of experiments, researchers examined how consumers applying for loans, insurance, and similar benefits respond to being judged by an algorithm versus a human. The key variable was 'applicant strength' — how strong people believed their own case was. Applicants who saw themselves as weak were substantially more deterred by algorithmic evaluation than strong applicants, because they valued human evaluators' perceived flexibility and leniency as a shot at a favorable outcome. The result was lower willingness to apply at all among weaker applicants.

**Why it matters:** Automating evaluation does not just change who gets approved, it changes who bothers to apply — potentially shrinking and skewing the applicant pool before any model runs, with fairness and access implications for lenders, insurers and employers.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you automate application screening, measure application volume and mix, not just decision quality: self-selection out of the funnel is invisible in approval statistics. Worth testing hybrid framings — algorithmic screening with a stated human review or appeal step — to see whether it keeps marginal applicants in the pipeline.
- **For general readers and consumers:** There is a belief that a human will bend the rules for you. That intuition shapes real decisions about whether to even apply for credit, insurance or benefits — and it may be costing people opportunities they would have gotten.

**Caveats:** Experimental studies with hypothetical application scenarios and stated willingness to apply rather than observed behavior; abstract does not report sample sizes or populations, likely online panels.

### 3. Customers who start talking about the past are about to leave you

**Journal:** *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* | **Published:** 2026-08-24
**Work ID:** W7204117449 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11747-026-01192-y

**The study:** Using written reviews, spoken call transcripts, and pre-RFP communications from business customers, researchers tested five families of language features — sentiment, temporality, certainty, concreteness, and personal pronouns — for their ability to flag deteriorating customer experience and predict defection in multimillion-dollar request-for-proposal decisions. Sentiment and temporality were the consistent winners across all three studies, adding predictive power beyond standard measures. In the communications preceding an RFP decision, a 1% increase in past-focused language raised the odds of defection by 8.4%.

**Why it matters:** Most B2B churn-warning systems rely on satisfaction surveys and usage data; this shows that the words in existing call transcripts and emails carry incremental, actionable signal about who is about to put the contract out to bid.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Run sentiment and past-versus-future orientation over your existing account communications as a churn early-warning layer, and use it to triage retention effort toward accounts whose language has drifted backward-looking. The authors supply prompts and code, so this is testable on your own transcripts before any vendor spend.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A quiet tell in how people talk: when someone starts dwelling on what used to happen rather than what will happen, the relationship is already ending. It holds for million-dollar contracts and probably for more than that.

**Caveats:** Observational language data, so associations rather than proven causation; specific to B2B RFP contexts and the firms studied; predictive accuracy may not transfer across industries or languages.

### 4. The most damaging bad review is the one that says 'they could have just...'

**Journal:** *Journal of Service Research* | **Published:** 2026-08-18
**Work ID:** W7203674935 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10946705261464327

**The study:** Researchers fine-tuned a BERT language model to detect 'upward counterfactual expressions' — a reviewer imagining how things could have gone better — in 21,187 negative reviews of 943 US hotels and 12,889 negative reviews of 300 UK restaurants on Tripadvisor, and paired the field data with an online experiment. Reviews containing these expressions measurably reduced sales, apparently because they signal a well-argued complaint that prospective customers take seriously. Firm responses mattered: offering compensation neutralized the damage and could even produce incremental profit, empathetic apologies made no difference, and offering explanations made things worse.

**Why it matters:** It gives review-response teams a triage rule based on the structure of the complaint rather than its star rating, and it suggests that the standard defensive explanation is actively counterproductive on the most persuasive negative reviews.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Classify inbound negative reviews by whether the writer spells out a better counterfactual, and route those to compensation offers rather than explanations; test whether your current 'here's why this happened' template is amplifying the reviews that hurt most.
- **For general readers and consumers:** When writing a complaint, saying what the business should have done instead is far more persuasive to other shoppers than venting — and as a reader, that's the review worth trusting.

**Caveats:** Two hospitality categories on one platform in two countries; field results are observational, with causal support coming from a single online experiment; profitability claims for compensation depend on modeling assumptions.

### 5. Explaining a disappointing refund works better when a bot does it

**Journal:** *Journal of Service Research* | **Published:** 2026-08-24
**Work ID:** W7204096810 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10946705261460013

**The study:** Across three main and three supplementary experiments spanning multiple service industries, researchers tested whether explaining a partially favorable service-recovery decision helps or hurts customer reactions, and whether the identity of the explainer matters. Explanations generally raised satisfaction and choice by making agents seem more competent and warm, but the details mattered: rationales beat excuses and justifications, specific explanations beat general ones, and explanations delivered by AI agents were sometimes more impactful than the same explanations from human agents.

**Why it matters:** Most firms default to keeping recovery decisions vague and to reserving sensitive conversations for humans; this suggests the opposite may sometimes be right — explain specifically, and let the chatbot do the explaining.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Rewrite partial-refund and denied-claim messaging to give a specific rationale rather than a generic apology or excuse, and A/B test AI-delivered versus human-delivered versions — the counterintuitive result is that automation may be an asset when a decision needs justifying.
- **For general readers and consumers:** When a company only partly fixes a problem, what makes customers accept it is a concrete reason, not sympathy. Ask for the reasoning rather than the apology.

**Caveats:** Experimental studies, likely using scenarios and online panels; abstract does not report sample sizes or countries; stated satisfaction and choice rather than observed spending.

### 6. A robot upgrade feels less special — unless the upgrade comes at someone else's expense

**Journal:** *European Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-18
**Work ID:** W7203703034 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ejm-03-2025-0198

**The study:** In one qualitative study and four experiments set in hospitality, airline and restaurant contexts, researchers compared how customers felt about preferential treatment delivered by a human versus a service robot. Human agents generated more gratitude and a stronger sense of status, especially when the perk was earned or functional, because customers credited humans with moral agency and genuine intent. But when the perk visibly disadvantaged other customers, the robot version produced more favorable emotional responses — its lack of moral agency spared customers the guilt.

**Why it matters:** Loyalty perks are meant to signal recognition, and this suggests the channel of delivery can strip that meaning out — while also pointing to a specific case where automation is the better messenger.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Segment your loyalty delivery by intent: route routine, transactional benefits to automated channels, keep human delivery for perks meant to confer status, and consider automating the perks that visibly bump other customers — like priority boarding or queue jumps — to defuse guilt. Worth testing in-market before restructuring service design.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Being upgraded by a person feels like being chosen; being upgraded by a kiosk feels like a rule being applied. And when your perk costs someone else their place in line, a machine handing it over lets you off the hook emotionally.

**Caveats:** Scenario-based experiments with self-reported emotional responses rather than observed loyalty behavior; sample sizes and countries not reported in the abstract; findings may not generalize across robot designs and levels of anthropomorphism, as the authors note.

### 7. Before you launch that AI-written email, check whether the data can actually predict it

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing Research* | **Published:** 2026-08-03
**Work ID:** W7172334002 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00222437261476639

**The study:** Marketing scientists built a causal prediction framework that uses pretrained large language models to encode the text of previously deployed marketing content, learn how content features relate causally to outcomes, and then screen new AI-generated content with a rejection-sampling step that discards drafts too dissimilar from anything in the historical record. They tested it on a large email marketing dataset of 3.3 million observations across 34 campaigns, and it improved out-of-sample prediction and real-world deployment performance relative to standard approaches. The framework also sets an explicit threshold: content close enough to past campaigns can be judged from data, while genuinely novel content still requires live experimentation.

**Why it matters:** Generative AI has made producing marketing content nearly free, which shifts the bottleneck to choosing which version to send — and this gives managers a principled way to rank AI drafts instead of guessing or A/B testing everything.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** The practical lesson is a triage rule: build a model on your own campaign history, score AI drafts against it, and reserve costly experiments for the genuinely novel ideas the model flags as out-of-distribution. Worth piloting on a single high-volume channel like email before generalizing, since performance depends on having a deep, consistent archive of past campaigns and outcomes.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The marketing emails landing in your inbox are increasingly written by machines and pre-screened by other machines predicting whether you'll click — a quiet shift in who decides what messages reach you.

**Caveats:** Single channel (email) and a single firm's campaign archive; gains may not transfer to other media, categories or brands with thinner historical data.

### 8. Should your AI shopping assistant act like a professional or a friend? It depends what you're selling

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Behaviour* | **Published:** 2026-09-03
**Work ID:** W7208752452 | **DOI:** 10.1002/cb.70243

**The study:** Three online experiments (N=263, 293 and 367) tested how the social role a generative AI recommender adopts affects whether consumers accept its advice. An "assistant" persona drove higher adoption intentions for search goods — products whose attributes can be verified before purchase — while a "friend" persona worked better for experience goods. The mechanism differed by role: assistants built cognitive trust, friends built affective trust. A third study found loss-framed messages strengthened the assistant/search-good pairing, while gain framing strengthened the friend/experience-good pairing.

**Why it matters:** The same conversational AI can present itself in different voices at essentially zero cost, so persona becomes a tunable variable rather than a fixed design decision — and the study suggests the optimal setting flips by product category.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Treat the AI's persona as an experiment, not a brand guideline: test a crisp, factual assistant voice on spec-driven categories and a warmer, peer-like voice on taste-driven ones, pairing each with the matching message frame. These are stated intentions in online experiments, so the real test is whether persona switching moves conversion in live traffic.
- **For general readers and consumers:** When a chatbot starts talking to you like a friend rather than a clerk, that warmth may be a deliberate design lever aimed at the kind of trust that doesn't rest on evidence — useful to notice when you're being sold something you can't verify in advance.

**Caveats:** Online experiments measuring adoption intentions rather than actual purchases; hypothetical recommendation scenarios; sample details and country not fully specified in the abstract.

### 9. Virtual influencers sell more when they talk about how a product is made, not how fun it is

**Journal:** *European Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-21
**Work ID:** W7203949248 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ejm-01-2025-0039

**The study:** Three experiments compared two scripting strategies for virtual influencers: describing the product's "production experience" (how it's made, sourced, engineered) versus the "enjoyment experience" of using it. The production-experience framing produced significantly higher purchase intentions overall, working through perceived quality and perceived authenticity. The advantage was concentrated on utilitarian platforms and for virtual influencers understood to be AI-controlled; on hedonic platforms enjoyment framing did better, and when consumers believed a human was behind the avatar the gap between the two strategies largely disappeared.

**Why it matters:** Virtual influencers are cheap, controllable and increasingly common, but brands have largely copied human-influencer scripts built on personal enjoyment — which this suggests is the wrong register for a character that cannot actually taste, wear or feel anything.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit your virtual influencer copy for claims the avatar cannot credibly make. Testing product-process storytelling against lifestyle-enjoyment storytelling, and varying it by platform, is a low-cost experiment with a clear hypothesis; disclosure of whether a human operates the avatar may itself change what scripts work.
- **For general readers and consumers:** There's an intuitive logic here consumers already apply: a computer-generated character raving about how delicious something tastes rings hollow, while the same character explaining manufacturing detail does not. It's a small window into how quickly people adjust their credibility standards to the speaker.

**Caveats:** Experimental studies using hypothetical purchase intentions rather than real purchases; sample sizes, recruitment source and country not reported in the abstract; single influencer format.

### 10. In luxury retail, AI doesn't sell by itself — it only works if it signals exclusivity

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-09-12
**Work ID:** W7212369437 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105111

**The study:** A multi-method study of luxury fashion retail combined survey-based structural equation modeling (n=362), exploratory segmentation of 101 consumers, and sentiment analysis of 193 online reviews to ask how shoppers interpret AI-enabled marketing. AI applications had no statistically significant direct effect on brand performance; the effects ran entirely through four perceptual channels — exclusivity, authenticity, innovation and responsiveness. Review sentiment was broadly positive but flagged recurring concerns about excessive automation and reduced human contact.

**Why it matters:** Luxury brands investing in AI personalization on efficiency grounds may be measuring the wrong outcome: in this category the technology appears to pay off only insofar as customers read it as a statement about the brand.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Question the ROI case for AI in premium categories that rests on cost or conversion alone, and consider measuring whether deployments move perceptions of exclusivity and authenticity. The review data hints at a tipping point where more automation starts subtracting from brand meaning — worth monitoring rather than assuming more is better.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Shoppers in high-end stores appear to read a brand's technology choices as part of its identity, not just its service — an automated chat window can say as much about a label as its packaging.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey design cannot establish causation; modest samples for the segmentation (n=101) and review analysis (n=193); one category (luxury fashion); country not specified.

### 11. Giving ad creatives AI isn't enough — teaching them to prompt is what moved the needle

**Journal:** *Journal of Advertising Research* | **Published:** 2026-09-01
**Work ID:** W7204938023 | **DOI:** 10.1080/00218499.2026.2702092

**The study:** Researchers randomly assigned 98 advertising professionals to one of three conditions: brainstorm without generative AI, use generative AI with no guidance, or use it after receiving prompt-writing training. Only the trained group produced significantly more ideas than the others; simply handing people the tool produced no measurable idea-volume gain. Judged creativity of the ideas did not differ across conditions, and a content analysis of the prompts themselves revealed how collaboration styles varied.

**Why it matters:** Agencies are rolling out generative AI on the assumption that access alone boosts creative output, but this experiment suggests the return comes from training in how to prompt, not from the license itself.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Test whether your own AI rollout is budgeting for skills as well as seats: run an internal A/B with and without prompt coaching and measure idea volume and quality separately, since this study found quantity rose while perceived creativity did not.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The 'AI makes you creative' promise looks conditional — in this test, untrained users got essentially nothing, a useful reality check for anyone told a chatbot will unlock their imagination.

**Caveats:** Small sample (98 professionals, roughly 33 per condition); a controlled ideation task rather than live campaign work; short time horizon; creativity assessed as perceived creativity.

### 12. Chatbots that remember you sell better — until customers think about what else they remember

**Journal:** *Psychology and Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-11
**Work ID:** W7212274308 | **DOI:** 10.1002/mar.70267

**The study:** Across three scenario-based online experiments set in e-commerce customer service, researchers manipulated whether a chatbot had long-term memory of past interactions, and in the third study whether users were given explicit control over their data. Memory raised satisfaction and intention to keep using the service, mainly by making the bot seem more competent, but those gains were partly cancelled out by heightened privacy concerns and by perceptions that the bot was uncomfortably human-like. Merely telling people they were generally empowered over privacy did not help; a concrete data-control mechanism did, reducing privacy concerns and improving outcomes.

**Why it matters:** Memory is the headline feature in the current wave of AI assistants, and this suggests the feature pays off only when paired with visible, specific controls over what the system retains.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you are shipping a memory-enabled assistant, treat the 'forget this' or memory-management panel as a conversion feature rather than a compliance checkbox — and watch whether overly human framing of memory backfires.
- **For general readers and consumers:** That pleasant feeling when a bot recalls your last order has a flip side: the same continuity that feels like good service is also a data trail, and people in these studies reacted better when they could see and control it.

**Caveats:** Scenario-based online experiments with hypothetical service encounters, not real purchases; stated intentions rather than observed behavior; single category (e-commerce customer service).

### 13. The friendlier the chatbot, the angrier customers get when it fumbles the conversation

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Behaviour* | **Published:** 2026-09-04
**Work ID:** W7208749448 | **DOI:** 10.1002/cb.70240

**The study:** Three pretests (N=30, 120 and 240) and two scenario-based experiments (N=346 and N=778) had consumers evaluate service interactions with chatbots designed to be either highly human-like or minimally so, across failures of process (a clumsy, inappropriate interaction) versus outcome (a bad result). Human-like chatbots triggered noticeably more customer aggression after process failures, because they raise expectations of socially competent interaction that are then sharply violated. For recovery, a straightforward apology beat having the bot acknowledge that it was, after all, just an AI — especially for the most human-like bots.

**Why it matters:** Brands routinely add names, avatars and chatty personalities to service bots without recognizing that this raises the social stakes of every misstep, and the common 'I'm only an AI' disclaimer appears to be the weaker repair move.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit your bot's recovery scripts: this evidence suggests replacing excuse-style AI disclaimers with plain apologies, and considering dialing back anthropomorphism in high-friction flows where conversational stumbles are likely.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If you have ever found yourself unreasonably furious at a cheerful bot, there is a mechanism behind it — the more human a system acts, the more you expect basic social competence, and the more betrayed you feel when it fails.

**Caveats:** Scenario-based vignettes rather than real service encounters; stated reactions rather than observed behavior; no information on real-world escalation or churn.

### 14. A dash of nostalgia makes customers more willing to be served by AI

**Journal:** *Journal of Service Research* | **Published:** 2026-08-11
**Work ID:** W7202159278 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10946705261475714

**The study:** Drawing on compensatory control theory, researchers tested whether embedding nostalgic cues in AI-driven services increases customer acceptance, using both field and online experiments. Nostalgic cues appeared to reduce the uncertainty of interacting with AI and restore a sense of control over the AI agent, which in turn raised willingness to use the service. The effect depended on how the AI was positioned (its role) and on whether the interaction posed a threat to the customer's identity.

**Why it matters:** Low customer acceptance is the practical bottleneck for AI service rollouts, and this points to a cheap design lever — retro framing, language or imagery — rather than expensive capability upgrades.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Worth A/B testing nostalgic framing in AI touchpoints — interface copy, onboarding, agent persona — but note the effect is conditional on AI role positioning, so it is unlikely to be a universal fix across every use case.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Nostalgia is doing quiet psychological work in modern tech design: reminders of the past restore a feeling of control, which is exactly what unfamiliar automated systems take away.

**Caveats:** Abstract reports limited design detail — sample sizes, country and field setting are not specified; effects are moderated, so generalizability across AI service types is unclear.

### 15. Virtual influencers are novel but not believable — and the novelty is wearing off

**Journal:** *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* | **Published:** 2026-07-28
**Work ID:** W7171554746 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11747-026-01186-w

**The study:** Researchers meta-analyzed 210 independent experimental studies and 643 effect sizes comparing virtual (CGI/AI) influencers with human influencers. Virtual influencers reliably generate more novelty but less credibility than humans, and the analysis shows the balance shifting over time away from novelty and toward credibility as the driver of persuasion. Effectiveness hinges on anthropomorphism: highly human-like virtual influencers do better with AI integration, futuristic positioning and rational, near-term messaging, while less human-like ones do better with emotional, distant framing.

**Why it matters:** Brands spending on synthetic spokespeople are buying an asset whose main advantage — being new and eye-catching — depreciates, while its main liability, low credibility, does not.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit whether your virtual influencer program is still coasting on novelty. The meta-analysis suggests that as audiences habituate, credibility-building design choices — degree of human likeness, message framing, category fit — become the lever worth testing, and that the right creative treatment differs sharply between highly human-like and stylized avatars.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For consumers, this quantifies an instinct many already have: a computer-generated influencer is fun to look at but harder to trust, and the fun fades faster than the doubt.

**Caveats:** A synthesis of mostly experimental studies, many of which likely used online panels and hypothetical exposure rather than real purchases or long-run brand outcomes; effects are averages across heterogeneous designs and platforms.

### 16. Swearing costs influencers engagement — and costs female influencers more

**Journal:** *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* | **Published:** 2026-07-24
**Work ID:** W7170627554 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11747-026-01183-z

**The study:** Researchers analyzed videos posted by influencers on a major video-sharing platform, using machine-learning tools to extract auditory, visual and verbal features, including profanity. Profanity in a post was associated with lower user engagement, with a much larger penalty in content categories where swearing is not normatively expected. The penalty was steeper when the influencer was female, when the post was sponsored, and when the surrounding content was otherwise polite; a track record of swearing softened the effect, but in low-profanity categories consistent swearing actually amplified the backlash.

**Why it matters:** Language style is a measurable, controllable variable in creator contracts and briefs, and this shows it interacts with category norms, sponsorship disclosure and — uncomfortably — the creator's gender.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands running creator campaigns could test language guidelines by category rather than applying a blanket policy: the data suggest profanity is far more costly in categories where it is off-norm and in sponsored posts specifically, meaning the same creator voice that works organically may backfire in paid content.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Audiences apparently punish women for swearing more than men — a double standard visible in real engagement data rather than in attitude surveys, and a reminder of how tightly viewers police tone against their expectations for a given creator.

**Caveats:** Observational platform data, so associations rather than proven causation; a single video platform; engagement metrics are a proxy for persuasion or sales, and the paper does not identify the platform or categories in the abstract.

### 17. AI nudges and social proof track with compulsive shopping — but chatbots pointed the other way

**Journal:** *International Journal of Consumer Studies* | **Published:** 2026-09-01
**Work ID:** W7207605884 | **DOI:** 10.1111/ijcs.70249

**The study:** A survey of 847 female online shoppers in India examined links between AI-driven persuasive design features and impulse-compulsive buying, testing addictive shopping behavior as a mediator. Social influence cues and nudging showed the strongest associations with addictive shopping, which in turn was associated with impulse-compulsive buying. Conversational AI stood out as the exception, showing a negative association with both addictive behavior and compulsive buying — consistent with chat introducing friction and reflection. Digital financial literacy buffered the effect only among psychologically vulnerable shoppers.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the specific persuasive mechanic matters for consumer harm: scarcity and social-proof nudges look most implicated, while conversational interfaces may actually slow people down.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For teams building AI commerce features, this is an early signal to disaggregate 'personalization' into its component mechanics before regulators do — and a hypothesis worth testing internally that conversational layers may reduce regret-driven purchases rather than maximize them.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If you find yourself buying things you did not plan to, the culprit may be less the recommendation engine than the 'ten people are viewing this' banner — and talking to a chatbot before buying may be the thing that breaks the spell.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional self-report survey, so causation cannot be established; single country (India) and a female-only sample; self-reported compulsive buying rather than observed transactions; findings rest on a moderated-mediation path model.

### 18. AI rewrote health messages in teens' own slang — the nudge worked, the personalization barely added anything

**Journal:** *Appetite* | **Published:** 2026-09-03
**Work ID:** W7114890653 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.appet.2026.108773

**The study:** A preregistered two-week randomized controlled trial with 587 adolescents (average age 13) in the UK and Ireland tested whether large language model text style transfer — rewriting health messages to match each teen's individual communication style — boosted plant-based lunch choices. Teens were assigned to no messages, generic messages, linguistically tailored messages, or a crossover arm. Daily messages of any kind lifted plant-based lunch choices from 44% to 59% over the two weeks, while the passive control stayed flat at 45–46%; the AI-tailored version produced no extra behavioral gain, though in week two it scored higher on perceived relevance, personalization and liking of the source.

**Why it matters:** It is one of the first real trials of LLM-based style personalization in persuasion, and the headline result is sobering for anyone selling AI tailoring: message frequency and consistency did the heavy lifting, not the algorithmic voice-matching.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** A caution for brands investing in generative personalization at the copy level: this trial suggests style-matching may buy you warmth and perceived relevance rather than immediate behavior change, and the liking effects only emerged after repeated exposure — so any test should run long enough to separate novelty from accumulated rapport, and should measure behavior, not just attitudes.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For parents and teens: simply being reminded daily nudged a meaningful share of adolescents toward plant-based lunches — repetition mattered more than whether a message sounded like it was written by someone their own age.

**Caveats:** Single trial in two countries with adolescents only; the tailoring benefits on relevance and liking came from exploratory (not preregistered) analyses; two-week horizon; lunch choice context may not generalize to other behaviors or to commercial persuasion.

### 19. Chatbots that use your name feel more sincere — until they use it too much

**Journal:** *Psychology and Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-27
**Work ID:** W7204492734 | **DOI:** 10.1002/mar.70251

**The study:** Across five experiments, researchers tested whether chatbots that address consumers by name produce better brand outcomes. Name-based personalization raised perceived chatbot sincerity and, in turn, brand sincerity, leading to more favorable brand attitudes and higher purchase intentions, with perceived anthropomorphism carrying the effect. Two boundary conditions broke it: when the chatbot performed poorly, and when consumers felt the name was being repeated too often.

**Why it matters:** Name-dropping is the cheapest personalization lever in any conversational interface, and this maps out both the psychological mechanism and the two ways it backfires.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Worth A/B testing name frequency in live chat and support bots: the evidence suggests an inverted-U where a few uses signal sincerity and too many read as manipulative — and that a bot which cannot actually solve the problem gets no credit for being friendly. Fix competence before adding warmth cues.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Why that chatbot keeps saying your name — and why it starts to feel creepy the fifth time. The effect works by making software seem human, which is exactly why it collapses when the software fails you.

**Caveats:** Five lab/online experiments with hypothetical brand scenarios and stated purchase intentions rather than observed behavior; sample details and country not specified in the abstract; no field validation at scale.

### 20. 40 million images from the biggest open-source AI image platform show a sharp tilt toward NSFW content and real people's likenesses

**Journal:** *Big Data & Society* | **Published:** 2026-07-28
**Work ID:** W4416014100 | **DOI:** 10.1177/20539517261467361

**The study:** Researchers conducted a sociotechnical analysis of CivitAI, the most active platform for sharing open-source text-to-image models, using a dataset of more than 40 million user-generated images and over 230,000 models. They document a disproportionate rise in not-safe-for-work content and a substantial number of personalization adapters (LoRAs) explicitly built to mimic identifiable real individuals, with internet subcultures visibly shaping which tools get built and shared.

**Why it matters:** This is rare large-scale empirical evidence of what open generative-image ecosystems actually produce when personalization is frictionless — a brand-safety and likeness-rights problem that sits outside the walled gardens most marketers monitor.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brand and legal teams should treat open-model repositories as an unmonitored exposure surface: celebrity endorsers, founders and brand mascots can be replicated as downloadable adapters with no consent mechanism. Worth asking what your talent contracts and monitoring vendors actually cover, and watching how platform policy and content moderation evolve here.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For readers: the AI image tools most people never see are the ones doing the most damage — the study quantifies how easily anyone's face can be turned into a shareable model, and why consent is the missing piece in the design.

**Caveats:** Exploratory, descriptive analysis of a single platform; no causal claims; classification of NSFW content and likeness-mimicking models depends on platform metadata; content warning — the paper covers gender-based digital violence.

### 21. When the algorithm remembers everything you've browsed, it can either curb waste or trap you in impulse loops

**Journal:** *International Journal of Consumer Studies* | **Published:** 2026-08-31
**Work ID:** W7204874545 | **DOI:** 10.1111/ijcs.70254

**The study:** Through 30 semi-structured interviews with Singapore-based e-commerce shoppers plus a practitioner-reflexive account from a researcher with over a decade of multi-platform retail experience, this study names a mechanism it calls "AI-augmented memory" — the way algorithms record, retrieve and resurface your past behavior during decision-making. The analysis finds a dual pathway: the same mechanism can reduce trial-and-error buying and support deliberate restraint, or amplify impulse-purchase loops and erode self-control, with need-based norms, social proof, age and reliance on recommendations shaping which path activates.

**Why it matters:** It reframes recommendation systems as a memory technology with a moral valence, and offers a design typology platforms and regulators could use to push personalization toward restraint rather than repeat impulse.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** A useful provocation for personalization teams: the resurfacing features that drive short-term conversion (abandoned-cart nudges, "you viewed this") may be the same ones that generate regret and returns. Consider testing restraint-oriented variants — reminding shoppers what they already own, or that they abandoned an item deliberately — as a retention play.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Shoppers describing their own experience of being followed around by their browsing history — sometimes it saves them from buying the wrong thing twice, sometimes it lures them back into buying things they'd decided against.

**Caveats:** Qualitative, exploratory study with only 30 interviewees in a single country (Singapore); no experimental or behavioral data; includes the researcher's own practitioner reflection, which introduces a subjective element; findings are theory-building, not generalizable estimates.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** A cluster of studies in this batch converges on the idea that making AI more human-like is not a free upgrade — it has a ceiling, and sometimes the machine's obvious machine-ness is the asset. Name-dropping chatbots raise perceived sincerity only up to a point before reading as manipulative (W7204492734), highly human-like bots provoke sharply more customer aggression when they fumble a conversation (W7208749448), and memory-enabled assistants gain competence credit but lose it again to privacy worry and "uncomfortably human" discomfort (W7212274308). Running the other way, the same batch finds machines outperforming people precisely because they lack social presence: AI agents were sometimes more persuasive than humans when explaining a disappointing refund (W7204096810), robot-delivered perks felt better than human-delivered ones when the perk visibly cost another customer their place (W7203703034), virtual influencers scored higher on production-process claims than on enjoyment claims they can't credibly make (W7203949248), and conversational AI was the one persuasive mechanic negatively associated with compulsive buying (W7207605884). The meta-analytic evidence that virtual influencers' novelty advantage is decaying while their credibility deficit isn't (W7171554746) puts a clock on the whole strategy of humanizing synthetic agents.

**Why pitch this now:** Memory, persona and voice are the headline features of the current assistant wave and of agentic commerce rollouts, and transparency rules requiring disclosure that a user is talking to a machine are arriving at the same moment — so brands are being pushed to be more openly artificial exactly when their design instinct is to be more convincingly human.

**Angle:** A longer feature on "the anthropomorphism ceiling" — where the evidence now says warmth in AI interfaces stops paying, where coldness actively outperforms, and how service and product teams should be tuning humanness by task rather than maximizing it by default. Written for CMOs, service-design and product leads, with a consumer-facing version for readers who have wondered why a cheerful bot makes them angrier than a bad menu tree.

**Potential headlines:**
- The evidence says your chatbot is too friendly for its own good
- Sometimes customers prefer the machine — especially when the news is bad
- Every human touch you add to AI raises what customers expect when it fails

**Potential outlets:**
- **MIT Sloan Management Review** — takes research-grounded arguments about designing AI systems around task fit, which is exactly the "tune humanness by use case" claim here.
- **Marketing Week** — its CMO readership is actively budgeting for conversational and assistant-led customer service, and this reframes a live spending decision.
- **Fast Company** — strong fit for design-and-behavior features on how interface choices shape emotional response, with the guilt-free-upgrade and angry-at-the-bot findings as color.
- **The Atlantic** — the consumer-psychology version: why we punish machines that act human for failing at being human, grounded in the aggression and name-repetition studies.
