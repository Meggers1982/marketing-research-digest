### 1. What 1,144 real support calls reveal: unhappy customers say it outright, happy ones only sound it

**Journal:** *Journal of Interactive Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-29
**Work ID:** W7224826657 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10949968261506232

**The study:** Researchers analyzed 1,144 real-world customer service phone calls, each labeled as dissatisfied, neutral, or satisfied, and ran them through three kinds of deep learning models: text-only (transcripts), audio-only (vocal delivery), and multimodal models that combine both. Dissatisfied calls were detected from transcripts alone about as accurately as from the full multimodal model, because unhappy customers explicitly state the problem and what they want fixed. Neutral and satisfied calls were much harder to read from words alone and depended on vocal cues, where cross-attention models that fuse voice and text outperformed text-only approaches.

**Why it matters:** Firms sit on hours of call audio but rely on delayed, low-response-rate post-call surveys; this shows satisfaction can be scored live, and tells companies exactly where cheap transcript analytics suffice and where costlier voice modeling earns its keep.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** A test worth running: route transcript-only sentiment scoring at complaint detection, and reserve voice-based models for the ambiguous middle, where most calls actually sit. The practical question for contact-center leaders is whether real-time flagging changes agent behavior or escalation outcomes, which this analysis does not yet establish.
- **For general readers and consumers:** When you are annoyed, you spell it out; when you are content, you merely sound it. That asymmetry means companies can hear your displeasure easily but may systematically miss what is quietly going right, and that your tone of voice is increasingly machine-readable data.

**Caveats:** Observational analysis of calls from a limited set of service interactions; satisfaction labels are coded rather than drawn from customers' own ratings in every case; predictive accuracy does not show that acting on real-time scores improves outcomes; single-language/single-context data limits generalizability.

### 2. When an AI handles a B2B complaint, buyers judge the warmth, not just the bot

**Journal:** *Industrial Marketing Management* | **Published:** 2026-11-01
**Work ID:** W7221491089 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.indmarman.2026.10.003

**The study:** Three experiments tested how industrial customers respond to AI versus human agents handling a service failure. Study 1 varied agent type and found customers read relational cues such as warmth, which signal how hard the agent is trying to fix the problem; Study 2 crossed agent type with displayed emotional competence (high vs low) and found that an agent recognizing the customer's emotions raised recovery satisfaction (though not complaint intentions) by increasing the customer's sense of control; Study 3 varied failure severity for AI agents and found sustained disruptions cut satisfaction and raised complaint intentions, partly through perceived unfairness of the recovery.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the AI-versus-human framing is the wrong design question in B2B recovery: whether the agent appears warm, acknowledges emotion, and is seen as fair matters more than what it is.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For firms rolling out AI complaint handling, the testable lever is emotional acknowledgment scripting and visible fairness in remedies, not hiding the bot. Note these are controlled experiments, so the next step is validating in live support queues with real accounts at stake.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Even business buyers, supposedly the most rational customers in the economy, soften when the agent on the other end seems to notice they are frustrated. And prolonged outages feel unjust in a way brief glitches do not, which is why the clock matters as much as the apology.

**Caveats:** Experimental scenarios with stated intentions rather than observed complaint behavior or retention; sample composition and size not specified in the abstract; B2B service failure context only.

### 3. In the ER, both the patient and the provider are the vulnerable party

**Journal:** *Journal of Service Research* | **Published:** 2026-10-20
**Work ID:** W7223154744 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10946705261474258

**The study:** Using a mixed-method design combining hospital data analysis with netnography (analysis of online patient and clinician discourse), researchers examined what they call service interaction vulnerability in emergency departments. They document how exhausted providers facing long shifts, high volumes, and difficult behaviors interact with patients made vulnerable by illness, dependency, and stigmatized presentations such as intimate partner violence, substance use, or suicidal ideation. The resulting framework shows how these encounters can either amplify or defuse vulnerability on both sides, shaping whether the encounter becomes a service failure.

**Why it matters:** It reframes service failure in high-stakes settings as a two-sided dynamic, meaning fixes aimed only at patient experience scores will miss the employee-side exhaustion that drives many bad encounters.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For service leaders in health care and other high-stakes frontlines, the actionable idea is designing recovery and training around dual vulnerability, including staffing and emotional-load management, rather than treating difficult encounters as individual staff failings. This is theory-building fieldwork, so specific interventions still need testing.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It helps explain why an emergency room visit can feel dehumanizing even when the clinical care is competent, and why certain patients, especially those arriving with stigmatized conditions, get treated differently before anyone has made a clinical judgment.

**Caveats:** Qualitative and exploratory rather than causal; netnographic data skews toward people motivated to post online; appears limited to one health system/country context; no intervention tested.

### 4. In virtual stores, flash beats substance — but only for the first few minutes

**Journal:** *International Journal of Retail & Distribution Management* | **Published:** 2026-11-02
**Work ID:** W7225124455 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ijrdm-09-2025-0684

**The study:** Two experiments tested how the design of metaverse retail environments drives shopping behavior, using a stimulus-organism-response model that broke virtual scenes into six features: visual appeal, navigability, informativeness, novelty, embodied co-presence (the sense of being there with others) and trialability (the ability to try products virtually). All six lifted purchase intention, pleasure and loyalty, and the effect ran entirely through a two-stage "customer inspiration" process — first being inspired by the scene, then being inspired to act. Crucially, how long shoppers stayed immersed changed which features mattered: informativeness, co-presence and trialability only gained traction with longer exposure.

**Why it matters:** It gives virtual-store designers a sequencing rule rather than a wish list — lead with visual spectacle and novelty to hook people, then deploy information, social presence and virtual try-on to convert sustained attention into sales.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands building virtual or 3D commerce experiences could test a time-phased design: measure dwell time and swap in deeper informational and social features once shoppers pass a threshold. These were controlled experiments, so treat the sequencing as a hypothesis to validate against your own platform analytics before committing budget.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For shoppers, this is a useful piece of self-knowledge: what grabs you in a virtual store in the first thirty seconds — the look, the newness — is not what actually informs a good purchase decision. The useful stuff only registers once you slow down.

**Caveats:** Experimental study with limited sample and participant details reported in the abstract; measures purchase intention rather than actual purchases; metaverse retail remains a niche context with uncertain real-world scale.

### 5. Trust isn't the universal driver of digital banking loyalty that banks assume

**Journal:** *Asia Pacific Journal of Marketing and Logistics* | **Published:** 2026-11-03
**Work ID:** W7225168433 | **DOI:** 10.1108/apjml-05-2026-1307

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 940 existing mobile banking users in Malaysia and Indonesia and analyzed the data three ways — PLS structural equation modeling, multi-group comparison between countries, and fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis that looks for combinations of conditions rather than average effects. Performance expectancy — simply believing the app gets the job done — was the strongest predictor of continued use and appeared in all six successful configurations. Trust, by contrast, showed no reliable direct or mediating effect in the pooled model, though it was a core ingredient in three specific configurations. Platform quality was strongly tied to trust, more so in Indonesia, while the performance-to-continued-use link was stronger in Malaysia.

**Why it matters:** Banks routinely pour marketing spend into trust and security messaging, but this suggests trust functions as a conditional ingredient in certain user segments rather than a blanket lever — while perceived usefulness is the stable foundation everywhere.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Financial services marketers might question whether trust campaigns are earning their keep across the whole base, and instead segment: identify the user configurations where trust is genuinely decisive and target it there, while defending core performance and reliability for everyone. Also worth noting that the same app strategy performed differently in two neighboring markets.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Most people keep using a banking app because it works, not because they have warm feelings about the institution. The emotional relationship banks try to cultivate matters less than whether a transfer goes through on the first try.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey, so causal direction cannot be established; self-reported intention to keep using rather than observed usage data; limited to two Southeast Asian markets and existing users only, which excludes anyone who already abandoned the channel.

### 6. Why "green" transit customers care more about how services connect than about the environment

**Journal:** *Journal of Service Theory and Practice* | **Published:** 2026-11-10
**Work ID:** W7225757846 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jstp-05-2025-0202

**The study:** A German mobility provider's customers were studied in two stages: 20 in-depth interviews analyzed thematically, followed by an online survey of 278 users tested with structural equation modeling. The research took a customer-dominant perspective — asking how sustainability fits into people's actual daily routines rather than how they rate the provider. What predicted using sustainable mobility services, and in turn satisfaction and word of mouth, was the perceived interconnectivity between the provider's services, the value those services added to customers' own sustainability goals, their multimodal behavior and how central the provider was to their travel life.

**Why it matters:** It suggests sustainability-marketing for services succeeds less through environmental appeals and more through seamless integration into customers' everyday logistics — being the hub that makes low-carbon travel practical.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Mobility, energy and other sustainability-oriented service brands could test shifting messaging and product investment from green claims toward connectivity: can customers chain trips across modes without friction, and does the brand sit at the center of that chain? The evidence is correlational and single-provider, so treat it as a direction for experimentation.
- **For general readers and consumers:** People don't adopt greener travel because they've been persuaded to care; they adopt it when the pieces fit together in their week. Convenience, not conviction, is doing most of the work.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey of 278 users with no causal test; a single provider in a single country; scales were developed by the same authors from their own interview data; respondents are existing customers, who skew favorable.

### 7. A VR headset after the trip beats a phone screen at turning visitors into recommenders

**Journal:** *Journal of Destination Marketing & Management* | **Published:** 2026-10-28
**Work ID:** W7224128805 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jdmm.2026.101179

**The study:** Researchers ran a two-stage study with actual heritage museum visitors. On site, they measured visitors' physiological arousal during a VR experience alongside self-reported reactions; one week later, the same participants were randomly shown follow-up communication about the museum either in VR or on a smartphone screen, and their intention to recommend the site was measured. Post-visit VR content produced significantly stronger recommendation intentions than the smartphone version, with the feeling of "presence" explaining the effect. Notably, the more memorable the original on-site VR experience had been, the less the post-visit immersion added — the two sources of impact partly substituted for each other.

**Why it matters:** It is rare evidence that immersive technology pays off not just at the moment of use but downstream in the customer journey, and that post-experience follow-up media format — not just content — moves advocacy.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Experience and destination marketers typically deploy VR as a one-off on-site attraction; this suggests testing immersive formats in the post-visit remarketing window, where most brands default to email and mobile. The trade-off finding is the practical catch: if the on-site experience was already memorable, extra immersion later may add little, so the test worth running is which customers actually need the heavy-format follow-up.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It is a glimpse at how memory and marketing interact: a week after an outing, how vividly you recall it shapes how susceptible you are to content designed to make you recommend the place to others. The more forgettable the visit, the more the slick follow-up does the persuading.

**Caveats:** Single museum and single country context; recommendation intention rather than observed recommendations or visits; one-week horizon; sample size per condition not stated in the abstract, so effects may rest on modest cells.

### 8. What actually happens when B2B suppliers sell through virtual reality

**Journal:** *Journal of Business and Industrial Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-26
**Work ID:** W7223701714 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jbim-02-2026-0160

**The study:** Researchers tracked five dyadic case studies pairing VR providers with their business customers, following each collaboration across three phases: conceptualization, development, and in-use. They identified specific mechanisms — immersive demonstrations, iterative prototyping, real-time feedback, and analytics-driven simulations — that changed how the two sides shared resources and coordinated work. The core observation is that VR compresses feedback loops and blurs the line between a supplier producing value and the customer co-creating it.

**Why it matters:** B2B firms investing in VR usually justify it as a sales demo tool; this suggests its bigger payoff is as a shared development environment that shortens specification cycles and generates behavioral data on how customers actually use a proposed solution.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For industrial and B2B brand leaders, the testable idea is measuring what customers do inside the VR session — where they look, what they manipulate — and feeding that into product decisions, rather than treating VR as a presentation layer. Equally, the cases point to governance as the make-or-break factor: clear objectives, task ownership, and information flow, not headset quality.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Limited direct relevance to everyday consumers, though it hints that the products and workplaces around them are increasingly designed and safety-tested in virtual environments before anything physical is built.

**Caveats:** Only five case dyads, qualitative and non-generalizable by the authors' own admission; no quantitative outcomes; self-selected firms already committed to VR, so success stories may dominate.

### 9. From unhappy customer to "hater" to anarchist: researchers map how consumers radicalize against brands

**Journal:** *The Journal of Marketing Theory and Practice* | **Published:** 2026-10-29
**Work ID:** W7224485554 | **DOI:** 10.1080/10696679.2026.2754216

**The study:** Drawing on theories of political radicalization, the authors ran two empirical studies of how dissatisfied consumers escalate. Study I used exploratory analyses to identify the stages through which dissatisfaction hardens into hatred and then into anti-brand activism; Study II tested that staged process and found multiple routes to the final stage, which can involve violence or other destructive acts aimed at the firm, its employees or parties merely associated with it. Notably, a firm's public stance on social issues emerged as a radicalization trigger alongside product failures and badly handled complaints.

**Why it matters:** It reframes extreme anti-brand behavior as a process with identifiable stages rather than a random outburst, which means firms have windows to intervene before a complaint becomes a campaign.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit where your escalation process breaks down: the research suggests mishandled complaints and divisive public positioning are accelerants, not side issues. Worth testing whether early, high-touch recovery for the loudest detractors — and clearer internal rules on when and how the brand takes social stances — slows escalation, while treating these findings as a diagnostic model rather than a validated playbook.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For consumers, the work is a mirror on how ordinary annoyance — a faulty product, a call center runaround, a CEO's political comment — can snowball into identity-level hostility toward a company, and how online communities of fellow "haters" reinforce each step.

**Caveats:** The empirical designs and sample details are thinly described in the abstract; stage models of radicalization inferred from self-reports cannot establish that any individual consumer will progress through them, and destructive behavior is rare, so base rates matter.

### 10. Send your executives to sit in the waiting room: ethnography inside a medical clinic as a CX diagnostic

**Journal:** *Qualitative Market Research An International Journal* | **Published:** 2026-09-16
**Work ID:** W7213312638 | **DOI:** 10.1108/qmr-01-2026-0021

**The study:** Researchers applied design ethnography — structured observation and participation inside the business itself — at a family medicine clinic, documenting the lived patient experience and feeding it back into the organization. The fieldwork surfaced both best practices and concrete service failure points, which the authors then integrated with service design and customer experience management literature into a conceptual framework and a five-step guide for redesigning services.

**Why it matters:** Most CX programs run on survey scores and dashboards; this shows how immersive observation can catch friction that metrics never register, and gives managers a repeatable procedure for doing it.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Consider piloting an observational immersion alongside your NPS or CSAT program in one location and comparing what each method surfaces — the paper's five-step guide offers a structure, though evidence here comes from a single clinic rather than a tested, scaled method.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Anyone who has waited in a clinic knows the experience is shaped by small, invisible things — signage, handoffs, how staff talk across a counter. This research is about getting the people who design those systems to actually live through them.

**Caveats:** Single-site qualitative study at one family medicine clinic; no quantitative outcomes, no control comparison, and the contribution is largely methodological, so generalization to other service settings is untested.

### 11. Green credentials buy forgiveness, not a second booking

**Journal:** *Review of Marketing Science* | **Published:** 2026-11-02
**Work ID:** W7225114666 | **DOI:** 10.1515/roms-2026-0070

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 804 digitally engaged sharing-economy consumers about how fairly they felt a service failure had been handled, and used partial least squares structural equation modeling to test whether the firm's "green brand equity" changed the picture. When a firm with strong green credentials handled a failure fairly, customers rewarded it with citizenship behaviors — defending it, recommending it, giving feedback. But that goodwill did not extend to actually using the service again. The authors argue green brands raise normative expectations, so a failure disconfirms them even when the recovery is fair.

**Why it matters:** Sustainability positioning appears to earn moral credit that shows up in advocacy metrics while leaving repeat purchase untouched — a gap that can make a green brand's post-failure dashboards look healthier than its revenue.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For brands leaning on sustainability as a trust asset, this is a prompt to separate advocacy metrics from repurchase in post-recovery measurement rather than assuming one predicts the other. Because the evidence is survey-based, the useful next step is to check the pattern against actual rebooking data after real service failures.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It suggests something about how we judge companies we think of as virtuous: we may publicly give them the benefit of the doubt while quietly taking our business elsewhere — and we hold them to a higher bar than we hold ordinary firms.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey, so causation cannot be established; self-reported intentions rather than observed rebooking; single context (sharing-economy services) and a single consumer pool; results come from a structural model rather than an experiment or transaction data.

### 12. Cafés sell aspiration, but customers bond with brands that match who they already are

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing Analytics* | **Published:** 2026-10-27
**Work ID:** W7223831545 | **DOI:** 10.1057/s41270-026-00553-4

**The study:** A survey of 351 café customers in China tested how service innovation, store environment, staff behavior and three kinds of identity fit feed satisfaction, brand trust, brand love and ultimately loyalty. Lifestyle congruence and brand identification mattered; "ideal self-congruence" — the brand matching who customers wish they were — did not register a significant effect. Social media marketing activity strengthened the link between satisfaction and loyalty.

**Why it matters:** In a category built on aspirational imagery, the data points toward actual-self fit and everyday operational quality as the loyalty drivers, with social media acting as an amplifier of satisfaction rather than a substitute for it.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Worth testing whether aspirational brand positioning is doing less work than assumed in experience-led categories, and whether creative built around customers' real routines outperforms idealized lifestyle imagery. Treat this as a hypothesis to A/B test, not a settled result.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The café you keep returning to is probably the one that fits the life you actually live, not the one in the life you imagine for yourself.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey in a single country and a single category (N=351), so no causal claim; self-reported loyalty rather than observed repeat visits; findings are a set of paths in a structural model, and the headline result is a non-significant effect, which is weak evidence of absence.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**

- **Machines read complaints better than they read contentment.** "What 1,144 real support calls reveal" (W7224826657) finds dissatisfied callers are detectable from transcripts alone because they state the problem outright, while satisfied and neutral calls need vocal modeling. That is the same blind spot documented last run in "Speed is a luxury killer" (W7220919414), where an LLM pass could not distinguish deliberate slowness from service failure, and it reinforces the broader pattern that automated readings of feedback capture operational content and miss the relational layer.
- **Agent identity matters less than relational conduct.** "When an AI handles a B2B complaint, buyers judge the warmth, not just the bot" (W7221491089) finds emotional acknowledgment, perceived control and fairness outweigh whether the agent is human — consistent with last run's "Customers find humanoid service robots creepy — except when the service is embarrassing" (W7220660112), which also located the real variable in context rather than in the machine-versus-human framing.
- **Execution beats positioning, again, in two more categories.** "Trust isn't the universal driver of digital banking loyalty that banks assume" (W7225168433) and "Cafés sell aspiration, but customers bond with brands that match who they already are" (W7223831545) extend the pattern set by "Faith-aligned features won't save a glitchy app" (W7216405737), "Gluten-free shoppers aren't asking for more choice" (W7222470326) and "For small-business clients, getting the order right beats having the slickest logistics tech" (W7215042510): the sentimental or symbolic layer underperforms basic usefulness.
- **The staff side of the encounter keeps showing up as the hidden input.** "In the ER, both the patient and the provider are the vulnerable party" (W7223154744) places exhausted providers inside the failure mechanism itself, which is the health-care analogue of last run's "Rude customers make staff less caring" (W7220440663), "Asking Service Staff to Sell" (W7221728768) and "Not all workplace stress is bad" (W7221898122).

**Advances**

- **Escalation research moves from diffusion to a staged process.** "From unhappy customer to 'hater' to anarchist" (W7224485554) models how dissatisfaction hardens into anti-brand activism, advancing beyond the previous digest's work on who spreads damage and how far — "When your hashtag gets hijacked" (W7206212528), "How close the disaster feels decides whether tourists boycott a destination" (W7221524554) and the 141,000-complaint escalation study (W7204606942) — and names brand social stances as an accelerant alongside product failures.
- **Service vulnerability becomes two-sided.** The prior run built an exclusion thread entirely on the customer side (W7204606942, plus earlier self-checkout and dignity work); W7223154744 makes provider exhaustion a co-equal term in the same encounter.
- **Sentiment measurement gets a cost rule.** W7224826657 does not just flag model limits; it tells firms where cheap transcript analytics suffice and where voice modeling earns its cost — a practical step past the methodological warning in W7220919414.

**Counterpoints**

- **Observation versus the text corpus.** Last run consolidated review and complaint corpora as the default customer-experience instrument (W7222527276, W7216405737, W7220919414, W7204606942). "Send your executives to sit in the waiting room" (W7213312638) argues the opposite direction: immersive, in-person ethnography catches friction that no dashboard or review pile registers.
- **Immersion is not anthropomorphism.** The previous batch's consistent penalty for humanlike interfaces (W7220660112 and predecessors) does not transfer to immersive media without a human face: "A VR headset after the trip beats a phone screen at turning visitors into recommenders" (W7224128805), "In virtual stores, flash beats substance" (W7225124455) and "What actually happens when B2B suppliers sell through virtual reality" (W7223701714) all find positive effects. The penalty appears to attach to simulated humanity, not simulated environments.
- **Advocacy is not loyalty.** "Green credentials buy forgiveness, not a second booking" (W7225114666) separates post-recovery citizenship behavior from repurchase — a caution for last run's recovery work, including "Fixing a Bad Meal? Compensate the Friend Too" (W7221159140), which rested on satisfaction and felt status as the outcome measures.

**New themes**

- **Immersive commerce as a cluster.** W7225124455, W7224128805 and W7223701714 together open a virtual-environment thread with no equivalent in the previous digest, including sequencing rules for virtual store design and VR as a shared B2B development environment rather than a demo tool.
- **Sustainability as logistics rather than persuasion.** "Why 'green' transit customers care more about how services connect than about the environment" (W7225757846) is new territory for this topic.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Five studies in this batch, from five different categories, find that the values and identity layer brands invest in is not doing the loyalty work they assume. Trust showed no reliable direct effect on continued mobile banking use while plain usefulness did ("Trust isn't the universal driver of digital banking loyalty that banks assume," W7225168433); aspirational self-image failed to predict café loyalty while actual-life fit succeeded (W7223831545); green credentials bought advocacy but not a second booking (W7225114666); sustainable mobility uptake ran on service interconnectivity rather than environmental conviction (W7225757846); and a brand's public stance on social issues emerged as a trigger for consumer radicalization alongside product failures (W7224485554).

**Why pitch this now:** Brands are in the middle of a visible retreat from purpose and ESG messaging under political and budget pressure, and this is a rare body of evidence about what that layer was and was not buying in the first place — including the finding that it can generate flattering advocacy metrics while repeat purchase flatlines.

**Angle:** A reported feature on the gap between the metrics values-led positioning moves and the ones it doesn't, framed for brand and CX leaders deciding what survives the next budget round, with a consumer-facing version on why we praise the virtuous companies we quietly stop using.

**Potential headlines:**
- Five studies, five categories, one finding: customers reward usefulness and merely applaud values
- The customers defending your brand loudest may be the ones who never come back
- What a company's principles are actually buying it, and what they aren't

**Potential outlets:**
- **Marketing Week** — its readership is already arguing about purpose-marketing effectiveness, and this gives that argument measured outcomes rather than opinion.
- **Harvard Business Review** — the advocacy-versus-repurchase split is a measurement problem for executives who report brand health to boards.
- **Marketing Brew** — well suited to the sharp, counterintuitive framing of brand values underperforming basic utility across categories.
- **Fast Company** — regularly covers sustainability claims and brand activism, and the radicalization-trigger finding gives the story a genuine risk dimension.
