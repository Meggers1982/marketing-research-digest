### Study 3: For disabled consumers in the metaverse, brand love does something unusual: it supports self-acceptance
**Work ID:** W7221050035 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline states an aim as a finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "For disabled consumers in the metaverse, brand love does something unusual: it supports self-acceptance"
- **Abstract says:** "This study explores the perceptions and experiences of people with disabilities in the Metaverse and examines how brand love can enhance acceptance of disability and fulfill the need for uniqueness among this population segment."
- **Problem:** The abstract frames brand love and disability acceptance as what the study examines; its findings section reports only that immersive interaction and inclusivity shape well-being and emotional brand connections. The headline asserts a confirmed relationship the abstract does not explicitly report.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rephrase to reflect what the abstract reports, e.g. "A study asks whether brand love in the metaverse supports disability acceptance — and finds immersion and inclusive representation central.""

### Study 6: Why a beloved brand buys you an extra hour in the queue
**Work ID:** W7222807396 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Invented wait-time quantities** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Why a beloved brand buys you an extra hour in the queue"
- **Abstract says:** "Study 1 shows that film narratives and destination brand love positively affect willingness to wait longer... Study 2 shows that film narratives directly increase willingness to wait longer"

### Study 7: In online private label, a credible influencer does what a brand name can't
**Work ID:** W7225259429 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Mediators described as drivers** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Influencer reputation, consumer confidence, and bandwagon effects all drove favorable attitudes"
- **Abstract says:** "Social media influencer reputation, consumer confidence and bandwagon positively affect consumer attitude towards OPLBs... Across both studies, consumer confidence and bandwagon effect mediate the effects of social media influencer reputation and heuristic cues on consumer attitude"
- **Problem:** The abstract positions consumer confidence and bandwagon as receiver-side mechanisms that carry the effect of influencer reputation and heuristic cues, not as parallel independent drivers; the digest flattens the model.
- **Suggested fix:** "Note that influencer reputation and heuristic cues work through consumer confidence and bandwagon effects."

**Issue B. Headline comparison not tested** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "In online private label, a credible influencer does what a brand name can't"
- **Abstract says:** "This study aims to examine the digital signals and mechanisms involved in shaping consumer attitude towards online private label brands (OPLBs) in an emerging digital market under conditions of heightened uncertainty."
- **Problem:** No condition in either study contrasts influencer credibility against an established brand name, so the comparative claim is an inference rather than a result.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe around what was tested, e.g. "For unknown own-label brands online, influencer reputation and popularity cues shaped attitudes.""

### Study 8: Startups improvise their way to a brand — and then quietly lose the plot
**Work ID:** W7214118345 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Conditional risk stated as certainty** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the ad hoc positioning decisions that were a survival strength in year one become an incoherent brand by year five"
- **Abstract says:** "coherence drift is most likely when these practices accumulate without stable reference points or strategic anchoring as the venture evolves"
- **Problem:** The abstract makes drift conditional on the absence of strategic anchoring and gives no time horizon; the digest presents it as a default outcome on a five-year clock.
- **Suggested fix:** "Restate conditionally, e.g. "without a stable strategic anchor, early ad hoc positioning choices can accumulate into an incoherent brand as the firm scales.""

### Study 9: Why small brands kept their activist stances in 2025 while big ones backed away
**Work ID:** W7222731158 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Background premise presented as finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "It offers a diagnosis of why corporate activism collapsed under pressure — it was institutional calculation, not embedded belief"
- **Abstract says:** "The 2025 corporate retreat from diversity, equity, and inclusion commitments showed how fragile institutional activism can be... a pattern that suggests their activism functioned as strategic calculation rather than a genuine commitment to values."
- **Problem:** The characterization of large-corporation activism as strategic calculation is the paper's motivating observation, not something the SME interviews tested; no corporate firms were studied.
- **Suggested fix:** "Attribute it as the paper's starting premise, e.g. "The authors start from the view that corporate activism was strategic calculation, and ask what makes SME activism different.""

### Study 10: Teaching employees the brand story backfires on women when they're angry at work
**Work ID:** W7221327304 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Measured perception framed as an intervention** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Teaching employees the brand story backfires on women when they're angry at work"
- **Abstract says:** "In two-wave survey data from 304 South Korean employees... higher PBK was associated with a flatter anger-NWOM slope among male employees, but a steeper anger-NWOM slope and a more positive contentment-BCB slope among female employees."
- **Problem:** Perceived brand knowledge was measured in a survey, not manipulated through training, and the results are associations between slopes. The headline implies a training programme causes women to badmouth more, which the design cannot support.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe to the measured construct and association, e.g. "Employees who feel they know the brand story: quieter anger among men, louder among women.""

### Study 11: Brand loyalty and buying fakes aren't opposites — the same shopper can do both
**Work ID:** W7223684627 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Direction of conditional social-influence effect** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Social influence had no direct effect but mattered conditionally — it pushed people toward counterfeits mainly when the perceived practical benefits of fakes were weak."
- **Abstract says:** "Social influence does not operate as a direct net predictor, but the configurational results show that it matters conditionally, particularly when perceived counterfeit benefits are weak."
- **Problem:** The abstract says only that social influence "matters" in configurations where counterfeit benefits are weak; it does not specify that it pushed consumers toward counterfeit purchase. The digest supplies a direction (and the gloss "practical" benefits) the abstract does not.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to: "Social influence had no direct effect but showed up in the configurational analysis as mattering mainly when perceived counterfeit benefits were weak.""

### Study 16: Brands that match their own colors get fewer likes: what 15,299 Instagram posts reveal
**Work ID:** W7224579614 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Moderator flattened** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the strength of these patterns varied with how committed the brand was to social media"
- **Abstract says:** "Brands with higher commitment levels benefited more in likes but experienced a weaker positive association between logo presence and comments."
- **Problem:** The abstract specifies the direction of the commitment moderation for each outcome (more likes benefit, weaker logo–comment link); the digest's generic "varied with" loses that detail and could be read as a uniform strengthening.
- **Suggested fix:** "State the specifics: higher-commitment brands gained more in likes but showed a weaker positive logo–comment association."

**Issue B. Causal phrasing in headline** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Brands that match their own colors get fewer likes"
- **Abstract says:** "Controlling for confounders, color similarity was negatively associated with both metrics, whereas logo presence positively predicted comments."
- **Problem:** "Get fewer likes" implies causation from observational platform data; the abstract reports an association only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as "Posts that match a brand's own colors draw fewer likes" or "linked to fewer likes"."

### Study 17: Admitting an ad is AI-made backfires — but only for brands built on authenticity
**Work ID:** W7225395232 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Headline conflates two separate findings** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Admitting an ad is AI-made backfires — but only for brands built on authenticity"
- **Abstract says:** "Disclosure boosts engagement for genuine ads but reduces it for AI content. AI ads enhance purchase intentions for low-authenticity brands but damage high-authenticity ones."
- **Problem:** Disclosure reduced engagement for AI content generally (Study 1); brand authenticity moderated the effect of AI imagery on purchase intentions (Study 2), not the effect of disclosure. The headline wrongly presents brand authenticity as the boundary condition on disclosure, which would mislead a marketer deciding whether to label AI creative.
- **Suggested fix:** "Split the claims, e.g. "Disclosing AI imagery depresses engagement — and AI creative only helps low-authenticity brands"."

**Issue B. Lab result stated as current market behavior** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Consumers are already penalizing ads that admit to being machine-made — a quiet sign that "made by a human" is becoming a product attribute."
- **Abstract says:** "The experiments used static images in controlled online settings. Future research should ... validate the effects with real-world behavioral metrics, such as click-through and conversion rates."
- **Problem:** "Already penalizing" asserts real-world behavior, while the evidence is stated engagement and purchase intention in controlled online experiments — a limitation the authors themselves flag.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "In these experiments, participants reacted less favorably to ads disclosed as AI-made"."

### Study 20: Scarcity can sour a collab: limited-edition drops weakened the emotional pull of co-branded drinks
**Work ID:** W7224853290 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Qualitative suggestion stated as established pattern** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "interviews suggested scarcity flipped from meaningful exclusivity into purchase friction and frustration"
- **Abstract says:** "Qualitative insights further suggest that scarcity may shift from meaningful exclusivity to purchase friction and emotional disruption."
- **Problem:** "Flipped" states as fact what the abstract offers as a tentative interpretive insight ("may shift").
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "interviews suggested scarcity may shift from meaningful exclusivity into purchase friction and emotional disruption"."

**Issue B. Prevalence claim in consumer angle** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "frustration, not fandom, is the common response"
- **Abstract says:** "Qualitative insights further suggest that scarcity may shift from meaningful exclusivity to purchase friction and emotional disruption."
- **Problem:** Neither the 573-person survey (which tested scarcity as a moderator) nor 22 interviews support a claim about what the "common response" to sold-out collabs is; this generalizes a tentative qualitative theme into a population-level frequency.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "some buyers described frustration rather than excitement when a collab sold out"."
