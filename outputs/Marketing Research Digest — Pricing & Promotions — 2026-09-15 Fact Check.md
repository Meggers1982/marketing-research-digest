### Study 1: When groceries got expensive, shoppers didn't just trade down — they cut fruits and vegetables too
**Work ID:** W7204997399 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Brand switching framed as displacing basket reduction** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Brand switching is the default consumer response to inflation, not basket reduction"
- **Abstract says:** "the most commonly reported food purchasing strategy was switching to cheaper brands (34% to 43%)... respondents in all countries reported reductions in purchasing of non-core foods, such as desserts and non-alcoholic beverages, but also of core foods, such as fruits and vegetables"
- **Problem:** The abstract shows respondents reported both brand switching and reductions in purchasing; brand switching was simply the most frequently reported strategy, not an alternative to cutting the basket. The digest sets the two in opposition.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to: 'Switching to cheaper brands was the most commonly reported response, though respondents also reported cutting purchases of both non-core and core foods.'"

**Issue B. Private label / value-tier inference** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "shows that private label and value-tier growth in an inflation cycle comes with a nutritional cost"
- **Abstract says:** "the most commonly reported food purchasing strategy was switching to cheaper brands (34% to 43%)"
- **Problem:** The survey asked about switching to cheaper brands; it did not measure private label or value-tier sales growth. 'Shows' overstates an inference.
- **Suggested fix:** "Soften to 'suggests that the shift toward cheaper brands coincides with...' and drop the specific private-label/value-tier claim."

**Issue C. 'Which lever shoppers pull first'** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "exactly which lever shoppers pull first under price pressure"
- **Abstract says:** "Respondents reported whether food had become more expensive for them in the last year and whether they altered ten food purchasing strategies"
- **Problem:** The survey measured which strategies were most commonly reported, not any temporal ordering of responses.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to 'which response shoppers most commonly report'."

### Study 2: Let them start today: immediate activation makes subscribers stick around longer
**Work ID:** W7170377972 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Churn and CLV presented as measured outcomes** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Activation timing is a cheap, purely operational design lever that appears to move churn and customer lifetime value"
- **Abstract says:** "immediate subscription activation reliably increases long-term subscription likelihood relative to delayed activation... Managers should prioritize immediate access where possible... to reduce churn and increase customer lifetime value."
- **Problem:** The measured outcome was long-term subscription likelihood/commitment; churn and CLV are the authors' practical recommendation, not reported results.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say the lever moves long-term subscription likelihood, with the authors suggesting churn and CLV implications."

### Study 4: Employee discounts can backfire: keeping promises to staff beats cutting their price
**Work ID:** W7170087457 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Ambiguous sample attribution** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Three studies, including two experiments with a total of 738 respondents"

### Study 5: Pay-what-you-want works in stages — and only the last stage responds to social pressure
**Work ID:** W7212055380 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Untested contrast between visibility and anchoring** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "social visibility, not price anchoring, is what moves the final number"
- **Abstract says:** "Study 2 demonstrates that willingness-to-pay is jointly shaped by organisational objective and external reference price... Study 3 finds that descriptive norms increase actual payments when payments are publicly visible."
- **Problem:** Reference prices were tested on willingness-to-pay, not on actual payments, so the abstract provides no basis for ruling out price anchoring at the payment stage.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rewrite as 'visible descriptive norms were what moved actual payments, while reference prices shaped stated willingness to pay'."

**Issue B. Overgeneralized consumer takeaway** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The same person who would happily underpay in private pays closer to the norm when the amount is public."
- **Abstract says:** "Study 3 finds that descriptive norms increase actual payments when payments are publicly visible."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports a group-level effect of norms under visibility, not a within-person private-versus-public payment pattern.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say 'on average, people paid more when a descriptive norm was shown and payments were visible to others'."

### Study 6: 32,000 people later: paying with cash really does hurt more than tapping a card
**Work ID:** W7203715795 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Direction of moderator not in abstract** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "The effect was moderated by individual habits: people who mostly use cards felt the difference most."
- **Abstract says:** "the mode-of-payment effect on pain of paying (and thereby on outcome variables) is moderated by individual differences, particularly the relative frequency of using cash versus card"
- **Problem:** The abstract names the moderator but not which group shows the stronger effect; the digest asserts a specific direction and repeats it in the consumer angle ('the more card-dependent you already are, the bigger the jolt'). Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "State only that the effect varied with how often people habitually use cash versus card, and drop the directional claim (or source it to the full text)."

**Issue B. Frictionless checkout characterization** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the frictionless checkout arms race (one-click, stored cards, wallets, BNPL) is effectively a spending-increase program"
- **Abstract says:** "we replicate the finding that paying with cash is more psychologically painful than paying with card... these direct effects of payment method on outcome variables are mediated by pain of paying"
- **Problem:** The replications compared cash versus card; one-click checkout, wallets and BNPL were not tested, so calling them a spending-increase program extrapolates.
- **Suggested fix:** "Keep the conditional framing but label it explicitly as untested extrapolation beyond the cash/card comparison."

### Study 7: Financial education's payoff is narrower than we thought — it mostly teaches people to compare prices
**Work ID:** W7212374919 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Knowledge conflated with education programs** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Financial education's payoff is narrower than we thought — it mostly teaches people to compare prices"
- **Abstract says:** "This study re-examines the relationship between financial knowledge and financial behavior using three-wave panel data... Changes in financial knowledge are associated with changes in only five behaviors"
- **Problem:** The study measured individuals' financial knowledge over time, not any educational intervention. Attributing the result to 'financial education' (and, in the story angle, to corporate financial-wellness programs) implies an intervention effect the data cannot supply.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change the headline to reference financial knowledge, and flag the education-program implication as speculation."

**Issue B. Causal phrasing for panel data** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "researchers tested whether financial knowledge actually causes better financial behavior across 24 specific behaviors"
- **Abstract says:** "We estimate the relationship between financial knowledge and 24 individual financial behaviors... The results depend strongly on model specification."
- **Problem:** The abstract describes associations under alternative model specifications, not a causal test; the digest's own caveat acknowledges this, but the body text asserts causation.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use 'tested how robustly financial knowledge predicts later behavior'."

### Study 8: When the stock market overvalues a company, marketing budgets are safer
**Work ID:** W7212389830 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Equity issuance moderator misread** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "prepare the long-term-value argument before an underpricing episode or an equity issuance makes short-term earnings management tempting"
- **Abstract says:** "Equity issuance weakens this relationship, whereas firm growth strengthens it."
- **Problem:** The abstract says equity issuance weakens the mispricing–myopia relationship; it does not say issuance itself makes earnings management more tempting.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say that equity issuance mutes the link between mispricing and myopic cuts, rather than implying it increases them."

**Issue B. 'Puts a number on' with no number** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "It puts a number on something CMOs suspect intuitively"
- **Abstract says:** "we find an asymmetric effect where overpricing decreases the likelihood of myopic marketing management more than underpricing increases it"
- **Problem:** No magnitude is reported in the abstract or the digest, so the claim to have quantified the relationship is unsupported as presented.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to 'It documents a pattern CMOs suspect intuitively'."

### Study 9: "Best before March 3" beats "expires in 2 days": how date wording changes what shoppers put in the cart

**Issue A. Researcher nationality not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Chinese researchers ran three experiments"
- **Abstract says:** "Across three experiments using purchase tasks, information-viewing measures, and mouse-tracking, the findings show that the date frame increases consumer acceptance of near-expiry food"
- **Problem:** The abstract gives no author or participant country. Unverifiable from abstract only, and it sits oddly with the digest's own caveat that country context is limited.

**Issue B. 'Earlier and longer' attention claim** — Severity: Minor

- **Abstract says:** "under the date frame, price enters the decision process earlier than expiration time, whereas under the delay frame no significant difference is observed in the onset timing of the two attributes"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports onset timing (earlier) and attention allocation generally; it does not state that price was viewed for longer under the date frame.
- **Suggested fix:** "Limit to 'price entered the decision process earlier under the date frame'."

### Study 10: Default nudges work on everyone — which is exactly why they won't close the health gap

### Study 13: Fast food logos on kids' jerseys — for sponsorship worth under 1% of the club's budget
**Work ID:** W7211920431 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Scope of the 1% figure** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "In most clubs surveyed, the sponsorship covered about 1% of the budget or less — the club is giving away more visibility than it is getting in cash."
- **Abstract says:** "The most common benefit received from food-related sponsors included direct funding (ON: 21% of clubs; QC: 25%) ... Among clubs who received these benefits, their value was small relative to club's total revenue (i.e. ≤1% for funding; ≤1.5% for in-kind benefits) in most cases."
- **Problem:** The ≤1% figure applies only to the minority of clubs that reported receiving direct funding (roughly a fifth to a quarter), not to most clubs surveyed. The headline repeats the same over-generalization.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "Among the clubs that did receive direct funding, the money was worth 1% or less of revenue in most cases" and adjust the headline accordingly (e.g., "...for funding worth under 1% of revenue where it was reported")."

### Study 15: Half of shoppers paid more for greener packaging — when it sat on the regular shelf
**Work ID:** W7203899003 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Unsupported price-gap characterization** — Severity: Minor

- **Abstract says:** "Across all stores, 53% of consumers chose tomatoes in an environmentally friendly tray despite the higher price and potential trade-offs."

### Study 20: Italians will pay about €1.31 extra for a health claim on olive oil — and the label-readers won't
**Work ID:** W7170171784 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Stated WTP presented as behavior in headline** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Italians will pay about €1.31 extra for a health claim on olive oil — and the label-readers won't"
- **Abstract says:** "The findings reveal that the average stated WTP premium is €1.31. ... Price orientation and, counterintuitively, attention to label information are negatively associated with willingness to pay."
- **Problem:** "Will pay" implies actual purchase behavior when the figure is a stated survey premium (the body and caveats get this right), and "won't" overstates a negative association as a zero premium.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "Italians say they'd pay about €1.31 extra for a health claim on olive oil — and label-readers say they'd pay less.""

### Study 21: A premium price drowns out your positioning story
**Work ID:** W7203839485 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Attenuation described as elimination** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Crucially, that fit effect appeared under standard pricing but faded under premium pricing, where price itself dominated evaluation."
- **Abstract says:** "The interaction emerges under standard pricing but attenuates under premium pricing, where price becomes a dominant evaluative signal."
- **Problem:** "Faded" and the headline's "drowns out" imply the positioning effect disappears, whereas the abstract reports only attenuation of the interaction.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "weakened" or "attenuated" rather than "faded"/"drowns out" — e.g., "the fit effect weakened under premium pricing, where price became a dominant evaluative signal.""

**Issue B. Dropped nuance on conventional farming** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Attitudes depended on fit with the salient health goal: when hygiene was the focal concern, vertical farming was rated more favorably, mediated by perceived appropriateness."
- **Abstract says:** "When hygiene is salient, vertical farming is evaluated more favorably, mediated by perceived appropriateness; the nutrient-oriented advantage of conventional farming is less uniform across crops."
- **Problem:** The mirror-image half of the finding — that conventional farming's nutrient-orientation advantage was not consistent across crops — is omitted, making the fit story look cleaner than the abstract reports.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add a clause noting that the reverse advantage for conventional farming under a nutrient-focused goal was less consistent across crops."

**Issue C. Generalization from online panel to shoppers** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Shoppers apparently use price as a shortcut that crowds out other information: once something costs noticeably more, the reasons given for buying it matter less to the decision."
- **Abstract says:** "Three between-subjects scenario-based experiments were conducted with US MTurk consumers... The studies rely on US online samples and stated-attitude measures."
- **Problem:** Extends stated attitudes from US MTurk scenario experiments to "shoppers" and to "the decision," i.e., actual purchase behavior.
- **Suggested fix:** "Qualify as "In these scenario studies, participants' attitudes shifted less with positioning once the price was premium" and note attitudes rather than purchases."
