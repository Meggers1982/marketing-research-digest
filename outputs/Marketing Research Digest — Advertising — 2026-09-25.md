### 1. Coke's AI Christmas ad drew 7,800 comments — and the anger wasn't mostly about the visuals

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-08-24
**Work ID:** W7204147563 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105058

**The study:** Researchers scraped 7,822 YouTube and Reddit comments responding to Coca-Cola's generative-AI remake of its "Holidays Are Coming" holiday campaign and ran a large language model–assisted, human-validated thematic analysis of the discourse. Backlash sorted into three layers: content judgments (visual realism, execution flaws, whether the look fit the brand), psychological reactions (loss of authenticity, creativity and human connection), and socio-ethical attributions (assumptions about corporate greed, displaced artists and animators, and broader ethics). The authors argue the deepest hostility came not from the ads looking bad, but from what using GenAI signaled about the brand's values.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that fixing the visual quality of AI-generated ads will not neutralize consumer backlash, because much of the objection is moral and symbolic rather than aesthetic.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands piloting GenAI creative could use the three-layer breakdown as a pre-launch risk audit — separately stress-testing execution quality, authenticity perceptions and the labor/ethics inference — rather than assuming better rendering solves the problem. Worth noting this is one campaign from one iconic brand in a heavily nostalgic category, so the intensity of reaction may not generalize.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For consumers, the study is a portrait of how online audiences reason about AI: people read a company's production choices as a statement about who the company is and who it is willing to replace.

**Caveats:** Single campaign, single brand, single category; self-selected commenters on YouTube and Reddit skew toward those motivated to complain; qualitative thematic coding rather than measured effects on attitudes or sales; LLM-assisted coding with researcher validation.

### 2. Advertising before people download your app keeps them using it afterward

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing* | **Published:** 2026-09-01
**Work ID:** W7212164169 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretai.2026.08.007

**The study:** Across eleven studies totaling N=11,272 — including behavioral data from a Dutch retailer's mobile app, a critical incident study and controlled experiments — researchers tested whether ads seen before installation affect usage after installation. Consumers exposed to pre-adoption advertising showed higher sustained app usage, which the authors attribute to reduced post-adoption cognitive dissonance. The effect was stronger for functional (rather than emotional) ad appeals, for consumers with a high need for control, and in less competitive markets.

**Why it matters:** Retail app budgets are usually split between acquisition ads and re-engagement ads treated as separate jobs; this suggests acquisition creative is quietly doing retention work, changing how the two should be measured and valued.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Media teams could test attributing downstream retention — not just installs — to pre-install campaigns, and compare functional feature-led creative against emotional brand creative on post-install usage. The field component comes from one Dutch retailer, so the size of the effect is likely category- and market-specific.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It offers a small window into why people abandon apps: when what you find inside matches what the ad promised, you are less likely to feel you made a mistake and delete it.

**Caveats:** Field behavioral data from a single retailer in one country; the remaining studies are experiments including online panels; boundary conditions tested largely in controlled settings; unclear long-run time horizon for usage measures.

### 3. Green claims work better for brands that aren't already green — and the evidence you cite matters differently for each

**Journal:** *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* | **Published:** 2026-08-10
**Work ID:** W7202096496 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11747-026-01189-7

**The study:** A meta-analysis of 156 studies covering 146,961 participants examined how green marketing communication affects consumer evaluations and behavior. Overall the effect is positive, but it is weaker for brands already positioned as green than for conventional brands. For green brands, emotional, abstract messaging delivered on packaging and backed by lower levels of evidence performed better, and new green brands benefited most; for conventional brands, rational messaging, paid advertising, stronger supporting evidence and brand familiarity all improved results.

**Why it matters:** It gives a generalizable explanation for years of contradictory green-advertising results: the same environmental claim is read as informative from a conventional brand and as redundant or self-promotional from a brand already known for sustainability.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Sustainability communications teams should question the default assumption that more proof and more data always help — the evidence suggests heavily certified, rational claims pay off for mainstream brands but can underperform for brands whose green identity is already taken for granted. Consider auditing whether your category positions you as the "conventional" or the "green" sender before choosing message style.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For shoppers, this shows environmental claims are judged partly by who is making them: a green boast from an oil company reads as news, the same boast from an eco-brand reads as noise.

**Caveats:** Meta-analysis aggregates heterogeneous primary studies, many of which are lab or online experiments with hypothetical products rather than real purchases; effect sizes reflect the literature's biases, including possible publication bias.

### 4. Search ads help the national chains; social ads help the local ones

**Journal:** *Marketing Intelligence & Planning* | **Published:** 2026-09-23
**Work ID:** W7214082546 | **DOI:** 10.1108/mip-03-2025-0185

**The study:** Using anonymized GPS location data on physical store visits combined with advertising expenditure data across 180 US counties, researchers built "competitor spillover" metrics — how often a brand's customers also visit a rival — for national and local limited-service restaurant chains. They introduced "advertising localism," the share of advertising placed within the county where it is displayed, and analyzed its effects by platform. Local advertising delivered through intent-driven search engines aggregated the market toward national chains, while local advertising on social media dispersed traffic toward local chains.

**Why it matters:** It implies the choice between search and social is not just a performance-efficiency decision but a structural one that shifts who captures the neighborhood's foot traffic — relevant to both challenger brands and antitrust regulators watching ad concentration.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Independent and regional operators may get more geographic defense per dollar from social platforms than from bidding against national chains on search; large chains might read the same result as evidence that local search spend consolidates their advantage. Test platform mix against measured store visits, not just clicks.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Where you end up eating lunch may be quietly shaped by which platform the surrounding restaurants advertise on — search tends to funnel you toward the familiar national name, social toward the place down the street.

**Caveats:** Observational GPS and spend data with OLS models, so causal claims are limited; single country, single category (limited-service restaurants); location panel data has known coverage and matching biases.

### 5. Pairing a virtual influencer with a human one works — but only if the ad keeps its distance

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-09-16
**Work ID:** W7213402036 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105113

**The study:** Four experiments with a combined N=1,293 tested ads featuring a human influencer alone versus a human influencer alongside a virtual influencer. Co-endorsement produced higher purchase intentions when the ad emphasized distal sensory cues (sight and sound) but lower intentions when it emphasized proximal cues (touch, taste, smell). Perceived novelty drove the co-endorsement advantage; perceived authenticity drove the human-only advantage. Future-oriented framing strengthened the co-endorsement effect, while past-oriented framing favored the human-only ad.

**Why it matters:** It gives a concrete rule of thumb for where synthetic endorsers can be added without damaging credibility: virtual influencers can sell what you look at, not what you touch or taste.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands experimenting with virtual influencers could run split tests by sensory category — fashion and tech visuals versus food, fragrance and skincare — and by time framing, rather than treating a virtual endorser as a uniformly modern upgrade. These are controlled experiments measuring intentions, so treat the pattern as a hypothesis to validate in live campaigns.
- **For general readers and consumers:** There's a quiet logic to the discomfort people feel seeing a CGI character recommend a meal: audiences appear willing to accept a synthetic figure as novel, but not as someone who has actually experienced the product.

**Caveats:** Experimental, hypothetical ads and stated purchase intentions rather than real purchases; likely online panel participants; country and product categories limited; mechanism measures are self-reported.

### 6. Eight brands switched off their branded search ads. Sales didn't budge.

**Journal:** *Journal of Business Research* | **Published:** 2026-08-27
**Work ID:** W7204491303 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jbusres.2026.116485

**The study:** Researchers ran nine field experiments with eight small direct-to-consumer brands that sell exclusively online, so every sale was captured in the dataset. When the brands stopped bidding on branded search terms (their own name), there was no significant effect on sales for any of the eight. Stopping generic search advertising cut website sessions for two of three brands tested, but again did not significantly move sales. A pre-registered online experiment replicated the null effect of branded search on click intentions.

**Why it matters:** Branded search is one of the largest line items in many digital budgets precisely because it looks efficient on a cost-per-click basis, yet these experiments suggest much of that spend is buying clicks the brand would have received for free.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** A prompt to run your own geo- or time-based holdout test on branded search before the next budget cycle: the authors argue spend is better reallocated to generic terms or to SEO that wins first-page placement. Note these were eight small DTC brands, so the size of any waste at a large multi-brand advertiser remains an open question.
- **For general readers and consumers:** When you type a brand's name into a search engine and click the top paid result, the brand is often paying for a visit you were already making — a small window into why the ads you see are not always the ads that changed your mind.

**Caveats:** Eight small DTC online-only brands; findings may not generalize to large retailers, offline sales or competitive categories where rivals bid on your brand name; the supporting online experiment measured click intentions rather than actual clicks.

### 7. "Buy now," "Just do it": meta-analysis finds bossy ad copy backfires — most of all for charities

**Journal:** *Journal of Advertising* | **Published:** 2026-09-24
**Work ID:** W7214238772 | **DOI:** 10.1080/00913367.2026.2725644

**The study:** A meta-analysis pooled 142 samples testing assertive language in advertising and used meta-analytic structural equation modeling to trace the causal chain. Assertive wording substantially raised perceived threats to freedom (d = 0.72) and state reactance (d = 0.39), and had a small but significant negative effect on persuasion outcomes (d = -0.08), with the persuasion effect serially mediated by threat then reactance. Nonprofit advertising provoked stronger reactance than for-profit advertising.

**Why it matters:** Commanding copy is a default in direct response and in public health and charity messaging, but the accumulated evidence says it reliably irritates audiences and, on net, slightly reduces persuasion — with the worst penalty in exactly the category that uses it most.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Worth A/B testing imperative versus non-assertive phrasing in calls to action, especially for cause and nonprofit campaigns. The average persuasion penalty is small, so the story is less "never use commands" than "know that you are paying a reactance tax and check whether the attention gain covers it."
- **For general readers and consumers:** There's a name for that flicker of resistance you feel when an ad tells you what to do: psychological reactance. The research shows it's near-universal, and that pushy donation appeals trigger it more than pushy product ads.

**Caveats:** Synthesizes mostly lab and online experiments rather than field campaigns; effect on persuasion is small in absolute terms; underlying studies vary widely in context and measures.

### 8. What actually makes mobile ads work: usefulness wins, irritation kills, peer pressure barely registers

**Journal:** *Journal of Business Research* | **Published:** 2026-08-26
**Work ID:** W7204247420 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jbusres.2026.116482

**The study:** A meta-analysis of 211 empirical studies tested which factors drive consumers' perceived value of mobile advertising and how that value feeds attitudes, intentions and behavior. Perceived usefulness emerged as the strongest positive driver and irritation as the strongest negative one, while subjective norms — what other people think — had minimal influence. Relationships were stronger for customized, permission-based, location-aware and in-app formats, and among younger samples and in developed markets.

**Why it matters:** It settles a long-running set of contradictory findings with an unglamorous conclusion: mobile ad performance rests mainly on being genuinely useful and not annoying, and the format choices that amplify that are personalization, permission and in-app placement.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** A checklist for auditing mobile campaigns — prioritize customized content over generic blasts, make permission the default, and treat irritation metrics (skips, mutes, complaints) as a leading indicator rather than noise. Bear in mind most underlying studies measure perceptions and intentions, not sales.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Consumers rarely value a phone ad because everyone else likes the brand; they value it when it tells them something they can use, and they punish it when it interrupts. Privacy concern matters, but sheer annoyance matters more.

**Caveats:** Built on primary studies that are largely cross-sectional surveys measuring intentions rather than observed purchases; effect sizes vary by country, age and study quality; publication bias in the underlying literature is a risk.

### 9. The cameo gamble: a second celebrity only helps if they're as famous as your first one

**Journal:** *International Journal of Advertising* | **Published:** 2026-09-10
**Work ID:** W7212116218 | **DOI:** 10.1080/02650487.2026.2722506

**The study:** Researchers tested "celebrity cameo endorsement" — a brief appearance by a second known figure alongside a brand's long-standing endorser, as with John Malkovich appearing with George Clooney for Nespresso — across two experiments plus a Google Trends analysis. Cameos by celebrities with comparable or higher media visibility than the primary endorser raised perceived ad novelty and lifted search interest, purchase intention and actual purchase behavior. Cameos by lower-profile celebrities reduced novelty and undermined campaign effectiveness.

**Why it matters:** Brands with fatigued long-term endorsers often add a second face to refresh a campaign; this suggests the refresh works only when the newcomer's star power matches or exceeds the incumbent's, and otherwise actively hurts.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you're extending a long-running endorser campaign, the casting screen isn't just fit or affordability — it's relative media visibility against your existing star. Test cameo executions against a control before committing budget, since the experimental evidence shows what can happen rather than guaranteeing a lift.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Why that surprise second celebrity in an ad can feel either delightful or slightly cheap — audiences appear to read the pairing as a signal about how much the brand is investing.

**Caveats:** Two experiments (sample sizes and recruitment not specified in the abstract) supplemented by correlational Google Trends data; purchase behavior measured within the study context; single-tactic focus with limited detail on categories tested.

### 10. Pet influencers: viewers want the cat, not the owner — and definitely not the ad

**Journal:** *Asia Pacific Journal of Marketing and Logistics* | **Published:** 2026-09-23
**Work ID:** W7214117679 | **DOI:** 10.1108/apjml-12-2025-2667

**The study:** Combining data mining and content analysis, researchers coded 244 short videos from the six most influential cat influencers on the Chinese platform Xiaohongshu, measuring advertising inclusion, owner self-disclosure, anthropomorphism, video length and engagement. Ads depressed every engagement metric. Higher owner self-disclosure also correlated with lower engagement, as did videos running over one minute. Anthropomorphism worked selectively: depicting pet–owner communication boosted saves but not likes or comments.

**Why it matters:** The pet economy is one of the fastest-growing influencer niches, and this indicates the engagement playbook borrowed from human influencers — personal storytelling, building parasocial ties with the creator — actively backfires when the star is an animal.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you're briefing pet creators, push for pet-centric, sub-60-second executions and be realistic about the engagement cost of visible sponsorship; treat likes, comments and saves as distinct outcomes rather than a single engagement number. Correlational platform data, so the sponsorship penalty may partly reflect which posts get sponsored.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Audiences follow animal accounts for the animal. The moment the human steps into frame with their own story, attention drops — a clue about why parasocial connection works differently in this corner of social media.

**Caveats:** Observational, not experimental — no causal claims; a single platform, single country and single species (cat accounts); only six creators and 244 videos; engagement metrics, not sales.

### 11. Older consumers don't reject AI-made ads — they reject unrealistic ones

**Journal:** *Journal of Services Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-23
**Work ID:** W7214074450 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jsm-01-2026-0071

**The study:** Researchers conducted 25 semi-structured interviews with Australian consumers aged 65 and over about how AI disclosure affects their trust in advertising, analyzed with reflexive thematic analysis. Trust turned out to be contextually calibrated rather than driven by disclosure itself: in utilitarian service contexts disclosure created a transparency–skepticism tension, raising demands for credible human presence, while in hedonic contexts disclosure mattered less unless AI-generated depictions looked unrealistic. "Relatability" — whether people saw themselves and plausible reality in the content — acted as the filter on credibility.

**Why it matters:** As AI disclosure labels spread through regulation and platform policy, this suggests the label alone neither destroys nor protects trust; what matters is whether the AI-generated content looks believable and includes recognizable human signals.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Plan disclosure by context rather than as a blanket policy, and pair it with realistic representation and visible human elements in functional, high-stakes service categories. This is exploratory qualitative work, so treat it as hypothesis generation for quantitative testing.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A useful window into how one of the fastest-growing consumer segments reads AI in advertising: they're not reflexively hostile, but implausibly perfect people in ads set off alarms.

**Caveats:** Qualitative, 25 interviews, no causal or generalizable estimates; single country (Australia) and a single age group; based on stated interpretations rather than observed ad responses or purchases.

### 12. When family firms miss their targets, they cut ads and spend on CSR instead

**Journal:** *Journal of Business Research* | **Published:** 2026-09-11
**Work ID:** W7212229180 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jbusres.2026.116531

**The study:** Using a panel of Indian listed firms from 2006 to 2019, researchers examined how family ownership shapes two signaling strategies, advertising and corporate social responsibility investment. Family ownership was positively associated with both. But when performance fell below aspiration levels, family owners shifted the mix — raising CSR spending and cutting advertising.

**Why it matters:** It identifies a predictable governance-driven pattern in where marketing budgets go under pressure, useful for agencies, media sellers and marketing leaders forecasting spend in emerging markets.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If your client or employer is a family-controlled firm in an emerging market, expect the advertising line to be the first casualty of a bad year while CSR is protected — a reminder to build the case for advertising's long-term brand effects before the downturn arrives, not during it.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Limited direct relevance for consumers, beyond explaining why some struggling companies go quiet on advertising while getting louder about their social commitments.

**Caveats:** Archival panel data, single country (India) and a listed-firm sample; associations rather than experimental causation; data ends in 2019 and predates the current digital-spend mix.

### 13. Podcast hosts: hog the mic for good news, share it for bad

**Journal:** *International Journal of Research in Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-01
**Work ID:** W7201848118 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.ijresmar.2026.07.005

**The study:** Researchers examined what makes conversational audio engaging, combining two controlled experiments (transcript-based stimuli in Study 1, AI-generated voice stimuli in Study 2) with an econometric analysis of 401 real podcast episodes covering more than 42,000 speaker turns and roughly 209 hours of audio. They found that "discourse concentration" — how much talk is dominated by one or two voices versus spread evenly — interacts with content valence: positive content draws more engagement when one voice dominates, while negative content engages more when the conversation is fragmented across speakers. Empathy toward the speakers mediated the effect.

**Why it matters:** Podcasts reach over 100 million monthly US listeners and generate more than $2 billion in advertising revenue a year, yet production advice on conversational structure has been largely intuition; this gives producers and media buyers a measurable lever tied to real episode performance.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Advertisers evaluating podcast and livestream inventory could start scoring shows on speaker balance as well as downloads — and test whether host-read spots land differently in dominant-host versus round-table formats. The lab studies show the mechanism can work; the 401-episode analysis suggests it shows up in the wild, but it is one sample of shows.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It explains something listeners may have felt but not named: a single confident voice is satisfying when the story is upbeat, but when the subject turns grim, hearing several people trade reactions feels more human and keeps you listening.

**Caveats:** Two of the three studies use artificial stimuli (transcripts and synthetic voices) with lab participants; the field component is observational and correlational, covering a single set of 401 podcast episodes, so causal direction in the real-world data is inferred rather than tested.

### 14. "De-influencing" hurts brands more than praise helps them — and does nothing for the influencer

**Journal:** *European Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-11
**Work ID:** W7202169140 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ejm-02-2025-0156

**The study:** Across three between-subjects experiments (total n=460, using online panels and student samples), plus a pilot using secondary TikTok data and a fourth supplementary study, researchers compared influencers who trashed a product with influencers who endorsed it. De-influencing produced more negative brand attitudes and lower intent to engage with the post, and its effect on attitudes was stronger than the effect of positive endorsement. Negative affect explained the drop in engagement, and although audiences rated de-influencing content as more helpful, that perceived helpfulness did not improve attitudes toward the influencer or boost engagement. Effects held for fictitious and real brands, known and unknown influencers, and across product categories.

**Why it matters:** It quantifies the asymmetry brands have long suspected — one influencer takedown outweighs an endorsement — while suggesting the trend is a poor business strategy for creators, who absorb the audience's bad mood without gaining credit.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brand teams should treat de-influencing as a monitorable reputational risk, not just a content genre: consider grievance channels for creators, contract language covering public negativity, and fast remediation (apology, replacement) — and test whether corrective responses actually recover attitudes, since this research did not measure recovery.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Viewers find "don't buy this" videos genuinely useful, yet the research suggests they don't reward the creator for it — the negative feeling the video creates spills onto the whole post, which may explain why honest-review accounts are harder to sustain than they look.

**Caveats:** Online panel and student samples; relatively small overall N (460 across three studies) with hypothetical exposure rather than real purchasing; the authors note findings may not transfer to influencers whose entire brand is built on "honest reviews"; brand recovery conditions untested.

### 15. Text-heavy social ads get remembered — just not the brand behind them

**Journal:** *Journal of Advertising Research* | **Published:** 2026-09-10
**Work ID:** W7212195252 | **DOI:** 10.1080/00218499.2026.2670019

**The study:** Two online experiments tested the visual salience of static in-feed social media ads, comparing image-based versus text-based creative paired with different types of visual brand elements. Image-based creative outperformed text-based on ad recall, and text-based creative actually reduced correct brand recall. There was also evidence that "counterprogramming" — pairing the creative type against the type of visual branding used — offsets some of text's disadvantages, particularly among a brand's non-buyers and for larger brands.

**Why it matters:** Text overlays are ubiquitous in paid social because they carry the message, but this suggests they can cost the one thing advertisers most need from a scroll-past impression: correct brand attribution.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Worth an A/B test on live in-feed inventory: run matched image-led versus text-led executions and measure branded recall, not just recall or engagement — and check whether the effect differs for your non-buyers, the audience this study found most sensitive.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It's a useful explanation for a common experience — remembering an ad vividly while having no idea who made it. The more words on the creative, the more likely that disconnect.

**Caveats:** Two online experiments with forced or simulated feed exposure rather than live platform behavior; static ads only; the abstract does not report sample sizes, countries, or brand categories; memory measures rather than sales.

### 16. Half of #womenshealth Instagram posts come from someone with something to sell

**Journal:** *Health Promotion International* | **Published:** 2026-09-01
**Work ID:** W7214105022 | **DOI:** 10.1093/heapro/daag141

**The study:** Researchers content-analyzed 472 Instagram posts using the hashtag #womenshealth. Individual influencers created 63% of posts (162 from alternative health practitioners, 105 from medical doctors); 48% concerned hormonal and reproductive health, including 58 on menopause or perimenopause. Explicit health claims appeared in 69% of posts, but only 7% referenced scientific evidence — while 51% of account holders had a clear financial interest and 39% of posts directly sold an intervention.

**Why it matters:** It puts hard numbers on how thoroughly commercial content has colonized a health category that millions of women use for information, and signals rising regulatory attention for brands selling supplements, devices and services through creators.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Any brand active in wellness, femtech or supplements should audit creator partners against these baselines — disclosure, evidence citation, claim language — because a 7% evidence-citation rate in a 69%-claims environment is the kind of statistic that precedes advertising-standards crackdowns.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A practical literacy point for anyone researching menopause, fertility or hormones online: roughly half the accounts in this hashtag had a financial stake, two in five posts were selling something, and only one in fourteen pointed to any scientific evidence.

**Caveats:** Single platform, single hashtag, cross-sectional snapshot of 472 posts; coding of "clear financial interest" and claim quality involves researcher judgment; no measurement of what audiences believed or did as a result.

### 17. Cute mascots should talk about warmth; cool ones should talk about performance

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Behaviour* | **Published:** 2026-08-26
**Work ID:** W7204255947 | **DOI:** 10.1002/cb.70235

**The study:** Four experiments across different product contexts tested how brand character design (cute versus cool) interacts with advertising appeals (warmth-focused versus competence-focused). Matched pairings — cute character with a warmth appeal, cool character with a competence appeal — produced higher customer engagement and more favorable brand attitudes than mismatched pairings. The mechanism was a subjective sense of "feeling right": matched combinations simply felt fitting to viewers.

**Why it matters:** Brands are pouring mascots and illustrated characters into social feeds; this suggests the character's visual style constrains which claims it can credibly deliver, and that a mismatch quietly costs engagement.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Before committing to a character system, test the character style against your core message: if your positioning rests on performance and reliability, a cute mascot may undercut it. Note this is lab evidence on engagement intent, so validate with live creative tests on platform.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It's a small window into why some ads just feel off — not because the claim is wrong, but because the messenger's look doesn't match what it's saying.

**Caveats:** Four lab/online experiments with measured attitudes and engagement intentions rather than observed platform behavior; abstract does not report sample sizes, country, or participant source; fictional or controlled stimuli rather than live campaigns.

### 18. When a clever visual metaphor confuses people, a line of explanation fixes it

**Journal:** *Journal of Advertising Research* | **Published:** 2026-08-28
**Work ID:** W7204599802 | **DOI:** 10.1080/00218499.2026.2693333

**The study:** Five studies tested visual congruency in metaphorical advertising. Congruent executions — matching product color to background color, product shape to the slogan, or product values to the surrounding scene — produced more favorable brand attitudes than incongruent ones, with processing fluency (how easily the ad is understood) mediating the effect across three separate manipulations. A final study showed that adding explanatory information restored fluency and improved brand attitudes when the visuals were incongruent.

**Why it matters:** Award-seeking metaphorical creative often trades comprehension for cleverness; this indicates the penalty is real but cheaply reversible with a clarifying line of copy.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you're running conceptual or metaphorical visuals, pre-test comprehension separately from liking — and consider whether a short explanatory headline recovers the brand-attitude loss, as it did here, rather than assuming the audience will do the decoding work.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Ads that feel effortless to understand tend to be liked more, regardless of the idea's sophistication — which is why the puzzling billboard you couldn't decode probably didn't win you over.

**Caveats:** Multi-study lab research with attitude measures rather than purchase behavior; abstract does not report sample sizes, participant sources, or country; stimuli are controlled ad executions, not live campaigns.

### 19. Naming your rival works in America — and backfires in Europe

**Journal:** *International Marketing Review* | **Published:** 2026-07-29
**Work ID:** W7171628015 | **DOI:** 10.1108/imr-12-2024-0524

**The study:** Two between-subjects experiments with general-population consumers (not students) in France, the UK and the USA tested direct comparative ads (naming a rival), indirect comparative ads (implying one) and non-comparative ads. Study 1 (n=758) varied ad format, exposure time (4 vs 20 seconds) and country; Study 2 (n=800, France and UK) pitted direct comparative against non-comparative ads. Indirect comparison generally beat direct comparison, with US consumers noticeably more receptive to comparative advertising than French or British consumers, and direct comparison scoring highest on perceived manipulative intent. Comparative ads also needed time to work — short exposures undercut them.

**Why it matters:** Comparative creative is one of the few ad tactics whose effectiveness appears to swing sharply by country and by how long people look at it, which matters for brands running one global campaign across markets and short-form feeds.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** A prompt to test creative by market rather than porting a US-style 'call out the competitor' spot into Europe, and to check whether comparative claims survive 4-6 second skippable formats at all — these were controlled experiments, so treat the pattern as a hypothesis for in-market testing.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Explains why American ads happily name rivals while European ones nod vaguely at 'the leading brand' — and why the blunter version tends to feel more manipulative to audiences.

**Caveats:** Experimental ad exposure rather than real purchases; three countries only; processing style was measured rather than manipulated; outcomes are ad responses and intentions, not sales.

### 20. When people fear sliding down the ladder, 'wealth' advertising stops working — 'craftsmanship' doesn't

**Journal:** *Journal of Current Issues & Research in Advertising* | **Published:** 2026-09-17
**Work ID:** W7213527998 | **DOI:** 10.1080/10641734.2026.2724856

**The study:** Across five studies, researchers examined how anticipating downward social mobility changes consumers' responses to ads. People expecting their socioeconomic status to fall de-emphasized the economic side of their identity and distanced themselves from products advertised via economic capital cues (assets, wealth, status), while their preference for products advertised via cultural capital cues (arts, craftsmanship, taste) held steady or rose — the increase concentrated among those who believe status is inherent and biologically rooted.

**Why it matters:** In an economy where many consumers feel they are losing ground, aspirational wealth messaging may actively repel the very segment it targets, while heritage and craft framings stay safe.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Worth auditing premium campaigns for whether the luxury cue is money-coded or craft-coded, and testing craft/heritage framings against status framings with audiences in economically precarious segments or markets.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A window into how downward mobility quietly rewrites taste: when money feels shaky, people reach for things that signal knowing rather than owning.

**Caveats:** Multi-study experimental work, but likely relies on scenario-based or panel samples and stated preferences rather than observed purchases; mobility expectations are often induced rather than lived; abstract does not report sample sizes or countries.

### 21. During Black Friday-style sales blitzes, ad overload turns eager shoppers hostile

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-08-13
**Work ID:** W7202369464 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105045

**The study:** A three-study mixed-methods project on Online Retail Sales Events examined how intrusive social media advertising during high-intensity promotional periods triggers consumer reactance. A qualitative study identified what makes sales-event ads feel intrusive; two experimental studies (each preceded by pilots) manipulated intrusiveness and, later, participation motivation. Emotional reactance depended on a three-way interaction between perceived intrusiveness, social media cognitive fatigue and how motivated the shopper was to participate — and 'low information' intrusive ads in particular cut both event-level and product-level purchase intentions.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the peak-season strategy of maximizing ad frequency can suppress purchase intent precisely when shoppers are most fatigued, and that thin, information-poor creative is the worst offender.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Consider capping frequency and raising information density during mega-sale windows, and test whether fatigued-but-motivated segments respond better to fewer, more substantive ads — the effects here are experimental, so validate with holdout groups in live campaigns.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Explains the specific irritation of scrolling through a fortnight of countdown ads: the more worn down you are, the more an empty 'don't miss out' post pushes you away from buying.

**Caveats:** Experimental manipulations with stated purchase intentions rather than observed sales; single event context; sample sizes, country and recruitment source not specified in the abstract.

### 22. Ads can make food taste better — but only if you can imagine the smell

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Behaviour* | **Published:** 2026-08-11
**Work ID:** W7202223869 | **DOI:** 10.1002/cb.70205

**The study:** Three experiments using different food products compared multisensory advertising messages (invoking several senses) against single-sense messages and measured perceived taste. Multisensory messages boosted taste perception both with and without actual consumption, but the advantage depended on mental imagery: it emerged when participants imagined the product's scent (Study 2) or its appearance (Study 3), and single-sense messages produced no such effect.

**Why it matters:** For e-commerce, delivery apps and any channel where sampling is impossible, the finding suggests copy that prompts active sensory imagination — not just more sensory adjectives — is what shifts taste expectations.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Test creative that explicitly invites imagination ('imagine the aroma as it hits the pan') against conventional descriptive food copy in online menus and product pages, and check whether the effect survives outside a lab setting.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A reminder that a lot of how food tastes is decided before the first bite — by the words and pictures that got you to order it.

**Caveats:** Lab-style experiments with perceived taste as the outcome, not repeat purchase or real ordering behavior; sample sizes, country and recruitment not reported in the abstract; short time horizon.

### 23. Tourism marketers are quietly uneasy about the deepfake destination ads they're being asked to make

**Journal:** *Journal Of Vacation Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-17
**Work ID:** W7213453650 | **DOI:** 10.1177/13567667261489700

**The study:** Phenomenological interviews with 23 tourism marketing practitioners in South Korea explored how they experience deepfake and hyperreal destination advertising. Participants described a tension between the technology's persuasive power and their own professional integrity, voicing concern about representational ambiguity, misleading travelers and eroding brand credibility — and rather than adopting or refusing the tools outright, they engaged in ongoing 'moral boundary work' about acceptable use.

**Why it matters:** Synthetic imagery is arriving in category marketing faster than disclosure norms, and the people making the ads are already improvising the ethical rules — a governance gap for brands that sell experiences customers can later compare to reality.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** An argument for writing internal standards on synthetic and AI-generated imagery now — what may be enhanced, what must be disclosed — before a credibility incident sets the policy for you.
- **For general readers and consumers:** When a destination looks impossibly good in an ad, it may not just be filtering: practitioners themselves worry travelers will arrive to something that doesn't match.

**Caveats:** Small qualitative sample (N=23), single country (South Korea) and single sector; practitioner perceptions, not consumer outcomes or measured deception effects; no causal claims possible.

### 24. Hiding the sponsorship backfires: platform data shows disclosed ads out-earn covert ones

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing Communications* | **Published:** 2026-09-19
**Work ID:** W7213758328 | **DOI:** 10.1080/13527266.2026.2733426

**The study:** Researchers analyzed influencer posts on Xiaohongshu, China's lifestyle-commerce platform, classifying content into four types — user-generated, advocacy, disclosed sponsored and non-disclosed sponsored — and comparing engagement (views, likes, shares, comments, bookmarks) across micro, macro and mega influencers. Disclosed sponsored content drew the strongest engagement on all five metrics within the macro-influencer benchmark, while non-disclosed sponsored content underperformed across the board. The disclosure advantage was largest for mega influencers, who were also punished most harshly when sponsorship was hidden; micro influencers escaped some of that penalty.

**Why it matters:** It reframes ad disclosure from a compliance cost to a possible engagement asset, and suggests the biggest creators have the most to lose from covert sponsorship.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands that quietly discourage prominent #ad labeling may be leaving engagement on the table — worth A/B testing disclosure prominence by creator tier rather than treating it purely as legal risk management.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Audiences appear to reward creators who admit they are being paid, and to disengage when a big-name influencer's pitch looks like an undisclosed ad — a rare case where honesty and reach point the same way.

**Caveats:** Observational platform data from a single Chinese platform (Xiaohongshu); associations, not causal effects — popular or high-quality campaigns may be more likely to be properly disclosed. Engagement metrics are not sales.

### 25. Consumers preferred AI-made ads to human-made ones — at least for practical products

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-17
**Work ID:** W7203605245 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jcm-07-2025-8108

**The study:** Two online experiments with 275 Prolific participants compared reactions to advertisements labeled as AI-generated versus human-generated. Contrary to the common assumption that AI content feels cold, participants rated AI-generated ads more favorably, and the effect ran through greater affective involvement — more emotional engagement with the AI ads. Study 2 found the advantage held for utilitarian products but disappeared for hedonic ones.

**Why it matters:** It challenges the widespread fear that disclosing AI authorship in creative work automatically triggers consumer backlash, while marking out where the risk is real.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Test whether labeling ad creative as AI-assisted actually hurts — the penalty may be category-specific, concentrated in pleasure-driven and expressive products rather than functional ones.
- **For general readers and consumers:** People may not be as good as they think at sensing the emotional shortfall in machine-made persuasion; labeling can shape reactions more than the content itself.

**Caveats:** Online panel (Prolific), modest total N across two studies, hypothetical ads and attitude measures rather than real purchases; source was manipulated by labeling, so effects may reflect novelty or expectation-setting that could fade as AI ads become routine.

### 26. Big influencers should endorse; small influencers should just sell

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Behaviour* | **Published:** 2026-08-16
**Work ID:** W7203570653 | **DOI:** 10.1002/cb.70204

**The study:** Three experiments with a combined N=632 tested how influencer popularity interacts with communication style — soft endorsement versus direct advertising — on consumers' sense of connection and their intention to spread word of mouth. Endorsement-style messaging generated stronger eWOM intentions when delivered by highly popular influencers, but direct advertising worked better for lower-popularity influencers. Communication style, not popularity alone, drove the outcome.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that the same creative brief should not be handed to mega and micro creators, and gives a simple rule for matching message format to influencer tier.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Segment influencer briefs by follower size: test subtle endorsement framing with big-reach creators and more explicit product pitches with niche ones, rather than standardizing tone across the roster.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Why a hard sell from a small creator can feel honest while the same pitch from a celebrity account feels grating — audiences apply different expectations depending on who is talking.

**Caveats:** Experimental studies measuring stated eWOM intention, not observed sharing or sales; sample composition and country not specified in the abstract; stimuli are likely hypothetical influencer posts.

### 27. When ads sneak past attention: personalization and message framing jointly decide what people remember

**Journal:** *Journal of Advertising Research* | **Published:** 2026-07-27
**Work ID:** W7171463384 | **DOI:** 10.1080/00218499.2026.2651998

**The study:** Across five experiments, researchers examined incidental ad exposure — ads glimpsed while doing something else — on personalized platforms. They found a crossover pattern: when consumers perceived the platform as highly personalized, near-term (proximal) goal framing improved brand memory via a sense of serendipity, while on platforms perceived as less personalized, long-term (distal) framing improved memory via greater cognitive effort. Consumers low in need for cognition were most sensitive to this context-message fit.

**Why it matters:** Brand recall from low-attention exposure may depend less on creative quality than on whether the message framing matches how personalized the surrounding platform feels.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Consider varying copy framing by placement environment — immediate, here-and-now framing on highly targeted feeds versus future-oriented framing on broader or less personalized inventory — and measure recall, not just clicks.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The ads you barely notice still lodge in memory, and the feeling that a platform "knows you" changes which ones stick.

**Caveats:** Five lab/online experiments with unreported sample details; perceived personalization was measured or manipulated in controlled settings rather than observed on live platforms; memory outcomes, not purchase behavior.

### 28. Guilt-based green ads only work when all three ingredients are present

**Journal:** *International Journal of Advertising* | **Published:** 2026-08-02
**Work ID:** W7172220713 | **DOI:** 10.1080/02650487.2026.2709196

**The study:** A 2x2x2 between-subjects experiment with 402 participants tested three message components in green advertising: framing the behavior as a moral transgression, assigning personal responsibility, and conveying that individual action is effective. Highlighting morality alone did not reliably produce guilt — the responsibility component had to be present too. Positive attitudes toward the ad required all three components together.

**Why it matters:** It gives sustainability marketers a concrete checklist and warns that half-built guilt appeals — moral shaming without responsibility or efficacy — may generate no useful response.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit green creative for all three elements; moralizing copy without a clear "this is on you" and "your action works" component may be a wasted spend and risks reactance.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Why so much eco-guilt advertising washes over people: without a believable sense that your own action matters, guilt does not convert into anything.

**Caveats:** Single experiment with hypothetical ad stimuli and attitudinal outcomes rather than actual sustainable purchases; sample details and country not reported in the abstract; roughly 50 participants per cell across eight conditions.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Five studies in this batch, taken together, dismantle the assumption that consumers simply hate machine-made advertising. Backlash to Coca-Cola's AI holiday remake was driven by moral inferences about labor and corporate values rather than visual flaws ("Coke's AI Christmas ad drew 7,800 comments," W7204147563); Australians over 65 objected to implausible depictions rather than to AI disclosure itself (W7214074450); Prolific participants actually rated ads labeled AI-generated more favorably for utilitarian products (W7203605245); virtual influencers helped sales pitches built on sight and sound but hurt ones built on touch and taste (W7213402036); and tourism marketers making hyperreal destination ads are improvising their own ethical rules in the absence of standards (W7213453650). The common thread: audiences are policing plausibility and intent, not authorship — and the people producing synthetic creative know the rules haven't been written.

**Why pitch this now:** AI disclosure labels are arriving through platform policy and regulation just as brands plan another round of AI-assisted holiday campaigns, and the industry's working assumption — that labeling is a liability and better rendering is the fix — looks empirically wrong in both directions.

**Angle:** A reported feature on what consumers actually punish in AI advertising, built around the gap between how brands think disclosure works and how audiences reason about it, aimed at marketing and creative leadership plus the general readers who spent last December arguing about a Coke ad.

**Potential headlines:**
- Consumers aren't rejecting AI ads — they're rejecting what the ads imply about the company
- The AI label that made people like the ad more
- Your holiday campaign will be judged on who you replaced, not how it looks

**Potential outlets:**
- **Marketing Brew** — has an appetite for "the received wisdom is backwards" findings framed for planners heading into holiday campaign cycles.
- **The Drum** — actively covers the creative industry's AI labor and ethics fight, which is the moral-inference core of the Coke finding.
- **Fast Company** — runs consumer-facing AI-and-culture reporting where the disclosure-versus-plausibility distinction would land with non-marketers.
- **MIT Sloan Management Review** — a natural home for the governance framing: writing internal synthetic-imagery standards before a credibility incident writes them for you.
