### 1. What 141,000 Consumer Complaints Reveal About Why Disputes Escalate in Poorer Neighborhoods

**Journal:** *Journal of Service Research* | **Published:** 2026-10-17
**Work ID:** W7204606942 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10946705261485818

**The study:** Researchers analyzed 141,251 complaint narratives filed with the U.S. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, coding each for seven kinds of "perceived control" language and linking them to whether the consumer escalated into a formal dispute. In economically disadvantaged areas, cues tied to cognitive control (understanding what is happening) and behavioral control (being able to act) were most strongly associated with escalation, while information-control cues — simple disclosure — were associated with *less* escalation.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that in financial services, disputes are driven less by the failure itself than by whether people can comprehend and act on the remedy, and that firms leaning on disclosure as their recovery tool may be addressing the wrong problem.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit your recovery flows for comprehension burden and action cost — how many steps, forms and reading-level hurdles separate a complaint from a resolution — and test whether simplifying them reduces escalation, rather than assuming more disclosure counts as support.
- **For general readers and consumers:** When a bank or lender messes up, the thing that most predicts whether a complaint turns into a fight is not the money at stake but whether the customer can figure out what happened and do something about it — which is exactly where low-income consumers are most often left stranded.

**Caveats:** Observational analysis of self-selected complaint narratives from a single U.S. regulator and sector (consumer finance); associations, not causal effects; area-level economic disadvantage is a proxy for individual circumstances.

### 2. When Rival Airlines Share the Same Big Investors, Service Gets Worse and Fares Go Up

**Journal:** *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* | **Published:** 2026-10-26
**Work ID:** W7223706258 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11747-026-01213-w

**The study:** Using a multi-market dataset of U.S. airlines spanning 1995 to 2025, researchers examined whether "common ownership" — rival carriers holding overlapping institutional investors — shows up in the marketing mix. Carriers with higher common ownership tended to deliver lower service quality and charge higher prices. The effect was weaker for firms with high customer-asset value and stronger among rivals that compete head-to-head across many markets.

**Why it matters:** It connects an abstract corporate-finance phenomenon to the things consumers actually feel — price and service — and gives regulators and investors a marketing-side indicator of softened competition.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If your category has concentrated institutional ownership, treat customer-asset value as a defense: the study suggests firms with strong customer equity resist the service-quality slide, which makes the case for protecting experience investment when competitive pressure eases.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Those index funds in your retirement account may own several of the airlines you choose between — and this research suggests that overlap tracks with pricier tickets and thinner service.

**Caveats:** Single industry (U.S. airlines) and single country; observational panel data, so causality is inferred rather than experimentally established; service quality measured through available industry metrics.

### 3. Fixing a Bad Meal? Compensate the Friend Too — but Only in the Right Dose

**Journal:** *Journal of Services Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-09
**Work ID:** W7221159140 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jsm-07-2025-0452

**The study:** Three experiments with loyalty program customers tested how people respond to service recovery when they were with a companion. Customers reacted more favorably to recovery when a companion was present than when dining or traveling alone, and when companion presence was held constant, including the companion in the compensation worked best for low-tier members when the payout was small and for high-tier members when it was large. Recovery satisfaction and "felt status" both explained the effect.

**Why it matters:** Most compensation policies treat the complaining customer as a solo actor; this suggests that who else is at the table changes the return on the same dollar of recovery.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Test tier-contingent recovery rules: small gestures extended to the whole party for ordinary members, larger exclusive-feeling gestures for top-tier guests — and track whether including companions changes repurchase and referral, not just satisfaction scores.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Complaining in front of a friend isn't just awkward — the research suggests you're likely to come away happier with the apology than if you'd been alone, especially if the business makes the gesture to both of you.

**Caveats:** Scenario-based experiments measuring responses to hypothetical encounters rather than observed behavior after real failures; loyalty program context; sample composition and sizes not reported in the abstract.

### 4. Asking Service Staff to Sell: Whether It Energizes or Burns Them Out Depends on the Climate You Set

**Journal:** *Journal of Service Research* | **Published:** 2026-10-13
**Work ID:** W7221728768 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10946705261488275

**The study:** A field study of frontline employees plus a complementary experiment examined how workers interpret the demand to both serve and sell. Employees in a "promotion" psychological climate appraised the dual role as a challenge, which raised work engagement and performance; those in a "prevention" climate appraised it as a hindrance, which drove burnout and lower performance. Finding meaning in serving customers amplified the upside and buffered the downside.

**Why it matters:** Companies increasingly bolt sales targets onto service roles; this suggests identical job designs produce opposite performance outcomes depending on how management frames risk, error and growth.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Before adding sales quotas to service teams, examine whether your climate signals opportunity or error-avoidance — and consider whether reinforcing the meaning of customer service is a cheaper performance lever than incentive redesign.
- **For general readers and consumers:** That awkward moment when a helpful service rep pivots into a pitch often reflects pressure from above; the same employee can sound enthusiastic or exhausted depending entirely on how their workplace frames the task.

**Caveats:** Field portion is correlational with self-reported climate and appraisals; the causal evidence comes from a complementary experiment; sample size, industry and country not specified in the abstract.

### 5. Devout Customers Reported More Willingness to Be Rude to Staff — Unless Karma Was on Their Minds

**Journal:** *Journal of Services Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-26
**Work ID:** W7223658951 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jsm-08-2025-0544

**The study:** Three studies (n=202 restaurant scenario, n=279 hotel scenario, n=297 Prolific experiment) tested how religious belief relates to customer incivility after a service failure. Stronger religious belief was associated with greater incivility intentions toward frontline employees, operating through lower situational forgiveness of the employee. That pathway was weaker among people with strong belief in karma, and weaker still when karma-style action–consequence thinking was made salient experimentally.

**Why it matters:** It identifies a customer-side predictor of frontline abuse that firms never measure, and shows that priming consequence-thinking — not moralizing about belief — can soften it.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Rather than profiling customers, test neutral, universal conduct statements and recovery designs that actively invite forgiveness of the individual employee, and pair them with real escalation routes and supervisor backup for staff absorbing the abuse.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A counterintuitive finding about who lashes out at waitstaff: self-reported piety didn't predict more forgiveness of the person serving you — but thinking about how your actions come back around did.

**Caveats:** Hypothetical scenarios and stated intentions rather than observed behavior; online panel (Prolific) in Study 3; the first two studies are cross-sectional associations; findings limited to the specific beliefs, contexts and intention measures tested.

### 6. Not all workplace stress is bad: the kind that makes staff steal — and the kind that stops them

**Journal:** *Psychology and Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-15
**Work ID:** W7221898122 | **DOI:** 10.1002/mar.70295

**The study:** Across three studies with 708 frontline service employees, researchers separated "challenge" stressors (demanding work that offers a route to achievement and growth) from "hindrance" stressors (red tape, blocked progress, barriers). Challenge stressors were associated with *less* unethical behavior — theft, service sabotage, cutting corners — while hindrance stressors were associated with more. The mechanism was self-efficacy: challenging demands raised employees' belief in their own competence, hindrances eroded it. Self-directed development activity moderated both effects; peer-led development only buffered challenge stressors.

**Why it matters:** Service firms lose real money to employee theft and deliberate service sabotage, and the standard response is to reduce pressure across the board. This evidence suggests the type of pressure matters more than the amount — and that removing bureaucratic friction may do more for ethics than lightening the workload.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit your frontline stressors by type, not volume: test whether removing hindrance stressors (approval bottlenecks, conflicting policies, unclear authority) reduces shrinkage and sabotage more than cutting targets does. Worth treating as a hypothesis to pilot in a few stores or call centers before rolling out, since the evidence here is employee self-report rather than observed theft data.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It reframes a familiar workplace experience: the exhausting-but-meaningful shift and the exhausting-but-pointless one are not the same kind of tired, and the second one is the one that corrodes people's sense of competence.

**Caveats:** Relies on employee-reported measures of unethical behavior rather than observed misconduct; study designs and countries not specified in the abstract; self-efficacy mechanism inferred from mediation rather than manipulated directly.

### 7. Customers find humanoid service robots creepy — except when the service is embarrassing

**Journal:** *The Journal of Marketing Theory and Practice* | **Published:** 2026-10-05
**Work ID:** W7220660112 | **DOI:** 10.1080/10696679.2026.2723262

**The study:** Two experiments compared customer reactions to humanoid robot versus human frontline employees. Robots were rated lower on warmth and higher on "creepiness," which reduced customer comfort, lowered service expectations and dampened intentions to use the service. The exception was service contexts involving embarrassment: there, the human employee's own creepiness rating rose with the context while the robot's did not, narrowing or reversing the robot's disadvantage.

**Why it matters:** It gives service operators a selection rule rather than a blanket verdict on robots: deploy them where human presence is itself the source of discomfort, not as a general substitute at the counter.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Map your service touchpoints by customer embarrassment before deciding where humanoid robots go — pharmacy pickups, intimate-product retail, screening and intake conversations are candidate tests, while warmth-dependent hospitality moments are not. Because these are controlled experiments rather than deployments, treat the embarrassment boundary condition as something to validate in a live pilot with actual usage and satisfaction data.
- **For general readers and consumers:** There is a reason some people prefer the self-checkout or the app for certain purchases: the discomfort of being seen by another human can outweigh the coldness of a machine.

**Caveats:** Two experimental studies only; design, sample size and recruitment source not specified in the abstract; outcomes are behavioral intentions and perceptions rather than observed service use; "humanoid robot" likely presented via scenario or stimulus rather than real interaction.

### 8. Gluten-free shoppers aren't asking for more choice — they want bread that tastes good and is safe

**Journal:** *British Food Journal* | **Published:** 2026-10-16
**Work ID:** W7222470326 | **DOI:** 10.1108/bfj-01-2026-0132

**The study:** A survey of 444 Portuguese consumers of gluten-free products tested which factors track with satisfaction, using correlation and multiple regression. Product quality was the strongest correlate of satisfaction, followed by service quality and then assortment variety. Safety (freedom from cross-contamination) and taste were the most valued product attributes, and respondents reported clear dissatisfaction with availability in specific categories: bread, pastry and ready-to-eat meals.

**Why it matters:** In a fast-growing specialty food segment, retailers and manufacturers often compete on range expansion; this suggests the satisfaction payoff sits more in quality assurance and in filling a few specific, still-poorly-served categories.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you sell into the gluten-free segment, test depth over breadth — reformulation and contamination-control credentials in bread, pastry and ready meals may move satisfaction more than adding SKUs. Note this is one national market and self-reported satisfaction, so treat the category gaps as a research prompt for your own assortment and sales data.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For anyone shopping gluten-free, the study puts numbers on a common complaint: the shelf keeps getting longer but the bread aisle and the ready-meal cabinet still disappoint.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey, so no causal claims; single country (Portugal); perceptual measures with acknowledged risk of construct overlap; stated satisfaction rather than observed purchasing.

### 9. How close the disaster feels decides whether tourists boycott a destination

**Journal:** *Journal Of Vacation Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-12
**Work ID:** W7221524554 | **DOI:** 10.1177/13567667261496543

**The study:** Two experiments grounded in construal level theory tested how the "scope" of a service failure crisis at a travel destination — whether it happened to the respondent personally, to someone in their community, or to a stranger elsewhere — affects their willingness to boycott that destination. Boycott intentions were strongest for personally experienced failures, weaker for failures within one's own social circle, and weakest for distant, external cases. The effect ran through a chain of perceived relative deprivation (a sense of being treated unfairly compared with others) and then eroded trust in the destination, and was amplified among people with high power distance beliefs.

**Why it matters:** It gives destination marketers and service recovery teams a triage logic: the same incident carries very different reputational cost depending on how socially close the audience feels to the victim, and the damage operates through felt unfairness rather than the failure itself.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Crisis response playbooks usually grade incidents by severity; this work suggests grading them by social proximity to the audience too, and testing recovery messages that attack the sense of being unfairly treated — not just the operational fault. Because these are scenario experiments, treat the hierarchy as a hypothesis to validate against real booking and cancellation data.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It helps explain why a travel horror story that happened to a friend changes your plans while an identical story in the news barely registers — and why the sting is less about the incident than about feeling you got a worse deal than other travelers.

**Caveats:** Two scenario-based experiments rather than observed travel behavior; stated boycott intentions; sample details and country context not reported in the abstract; cultural moderator (power distance beliefs) measured, not manipulated at the country level.

### 10. For small-business clients, getting the order right beats having the slickest logistics tech

**Journal:** *Asian Journal of Business Research* | **Published:** 2026-09-30
**Work ID:** W7215042510 | **DOI:** 10.14707/ajbr.260231

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 236 Thai small and medium-sized enterprises about their logistics service providers, testing how logistics service quality feeds customer satisfaction and repurchase intention using structural equation modeling. Logistics service quality influenced repurchase both directly and through satisfaction. The notable detail: core operational basics — order accuracy and the quality of contact with staff — mattered more to SME satisfaction than technological sophistication or the provider's corporate image.

**Why it matters:** Logistics and B2B service providers often market digital tracking dashboards and brand credentials to small-business buyers, while this evidence points to fulfillment accuracy and the human account contact as the real retention levers.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit what your SME clients actually rate you on: test whether reallocating spend from platform features and brand campaigns into order accuracy and named human contacts moves repurchase rates. Note this is a single-country cross-sectional survey, so treat it as a prompt for a controlled service-level test rather than proof.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A useful reminder of what small-business owners value in their suppliers: reliability and a person who picks up the phone, not a fancier app.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey, cannot establish causation; stated repurchase intention rather than observed renewals; single country (Thailand); self-reported data from one respondent per firm with common method bias addressed only via Harman's single-factor test.

### 11. Corner shops can win the speed game without any of the quick-commerce infrastructure

**Journal:** *International Journal of Retail & Distribution Management* | **Published:** 2026-10-01
**Work ID:** W7215042272 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ijrdm-02-2026-0127

**The study:** A survey of 340 consumers across 60 urban "nanostores" — tiny independent neighborhood shops — in Bandung, Indonesia tested what makes shoppers see these micro-retailers as competitive against quick-commerce platforms. Convenience, satisfaction with delivery, and basic digital service quality (chat ordering and similar lightweight tools) all strengthened perceived competitiveness, which in turn lifted purchase intention on both product and price grounds. Perceived competitiveness carried the effects of convenience and delivery satisfaction, but digital service quality worked directly on shoppers rather than through any sense of relative advantage.

**Why it matters:** It reframes quick commerce as a perception of immediacy that proximity and a WhatsApp order can deliver, rather than something only dark stores and fleet algorithms can buy — with implications for platforms competing in dense informal retail markets.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If perceived immediacy rather than logistics scale drives choice, platforms and FMCG brands in emerging markets should test partnering with, rather than displacing, neighborhood stores — and test how little digital tooling is needed to make a shop feel "instant." Survey-based, single-city evidence, so validate with actual order data before building strategy on it.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For shoppers, the fastest option is often the shop around the corner with a messaging app — and the feeling of speed, more than literal delivery minutes, is what drives where we buy.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey analyzed with PLS-SEM, cannot establish causation; stated purchase intention rather than observed purchases; single city in one developing economy; consumers sampled at stores, risking selection toward existing customers.

### 12. When your hashtag gets hijacked, the angriest posts aren't the ones that spread

**Journal:** *Journal of Advertising* | **Published:** 2026-09-02
**Work ID:** W7206212528 | **DOI:** 10.1080/00913367.2026.2711413

**The study:** Researchers analyzed 14,511 social media posts tied to two famously hijacked brand hashtag campaigns — McDonald's and Walkers Crisps — using LDA topic modeling and VADER sentiment analysis, then surveyed 418 social media users about what makes them pass such content along. They built a typology (REACTS) of the narratives consumers tell inside "bashtag" networks, and identified the enablers and inhibitors of negative word-of-mouth diffusion. Counter to the well-established negativity effect, the most negative messages did not spread further once a conversation was already negatively skewed.

**Why it matters:** It suggests a hijacked hashtag is not an automatic death spiral: within an already hostile thread, extra outrage loses its marginal shock value, which changes the calculus on when and how hard to intervene.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Social and brand teams can use this to question the reflex of treating every negative spike as equally contagious — the data hint that diffusion depends on the surrounding sentiment mix, not just message valence. Worth building monitoring that tracks the composition of a hashtag conversation, not only its volume, and testing proactive response playbooks before a campaign hashtag launches.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For anyone who has piled onto a brand hashtag: the research maps the recognizable genres of consumer complaint-posting, and finds that in a pile-on, the harshest takes often get lost in the noise rather than becoming the ones everyone shares.

**Caveats:** Two brands and two campaigns in a single market context; the survey component (N=418) relies on self-reported sharing intentions rather than observed sharing; sentiment tools like VADER approximate tone imperfectly, especially with sarcasm, which is the native language of bashtags.

### 13. What a million words of app-store griping reveals about fintech users' well-being

**Journal:** *Internet Research* | **Published:** 2026-10-16
**Work ID:** W7222527276 | **DOI:** 10.1108/intr-04-2026-0726

**The study:** Instead of surveying users, the researchers mined unsolicited Arabic and English reviews of digital wallet apps from the Google Play Store and Apple App Store in Saudi Arabia, using a pipeline of hierarchical clustering, topic modeling, sentiment analysis, machine classification with human validation, and Ridge/Lasso regression. Four experiential factors emerged: complaint handling effectiveness, perceived system reliability, information ambiguity, and user autonomy. Effective complaint handling, reliability, and autonomy were linked to higher user well-being; ambiguous information — unclear fees, statuses, error messages — was linked to lower well-being.

**Why it matters:** It reframes the fintech scorecard from "will they keep using it" to "does using it make people feel better or worse," and names unclear communication as an actively harmful design choice rather than a minor annoyance.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** App and service leaders can treat their own review corpus as a standing customer-experience instrument — the method shown here extracts measurable constructs from feedback they already own. The practical test: audit where your product leaves users uncertain about what just happened to their money, and whether complaint resolution is resourced as a well-being lever rather than a cost center.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The thing that most erodes trust in a payment app isn't a missing feature — it's not knowing whether your transfer went through, what a fee is for, or who to ask. Readers may recognize that the apps they tolerate and the apps they resent differ mainly in how clearly they explain themselves.

**Caveats:** Single country (Saudi Arabia) and a single app category; app-store reviewers are a self-selected, extreme-opinion sample; well-being is inferred from review text rather than measured directly; regression on observational data cannot establish causation.

### 14. Rude customers make staff less caring — and the boss's favorites take it hardest

**Journal:** *Journal of service management* | **Published:** 2026-10-05
**Work ID:** W7220440663 | **DOI:** 10.1108/josm-05-2025-0238

**The study:** Researchers ran a four-wave, time-lagged survey of 316 customer-facing service employees in the United States, recruited via Prolific, to trace how customer incivility affects "compassionate service behavior" — the small acts of care frontline staff extend to customers. Incivility eroded compassionate behavior indirectly, by raising employees' sense of workplace uncertainty. The twist: the damage was strongest among employees with high-quality relationships with their managers (high leader–member exchange), apparently because uncertainty is a bigger shock to people used to relational clarity and predictability.

**Why it matters:** Abusive customers don't just hurt morale, they degrade the very warmth that service brands sell — and the usual managerial remedy, strong manager–employee bonds, may not buffer it the way leadership playbooks assume.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Service leaders could test whether their customer-incivility response protocols actually reduce felt uncertainty — clear escalation rules, explicit backing for staff who disengage from abusive customers — rather than assuming a good manager relationship inoculates the team. This is survey evidence from one national sample, so treat the high-LMX finding as a hypothesis to probe in your own staff data, not a settled fact.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Snapping at a cashier or support agent doesn't just ruin one interaction: the evidence suggests it quietly drains the employee's capacity to be kind to the next several customers in line.

**Caveats:** Online panel (Prolific) self-report across four waves rather than observed service behavior; single country (US); correlational design despite time lags, so causality is inferred, not proven.

### 15. Speed is a luxury killer: what 1.2 million Google reviews say about AI in high-end service

**Journal:** *International Journal of Quality and Service Sciences* | **Published:** 2026-10-08
**Work ID:** W7220919414 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ijqss-04-2026-0118

**The study:** Using a corpus of 1.2 million Google Maps reviews across 10 luxury chains, researchers hand-coded 2,000 reviews and compared that human coding against an automated large language model (Gemini) extraction, looking specifically at how customers talk about time. They find recurring "temporal fractures" where AI-driven acceleration delivers efficiency but strips out relational time — the unhurried attention that justifies premium pricing — and sort brands into five positions on an efficiency-versus-experience frontier (Decelerator, Bifurcator, Autonomy Provider, Fragmentor, Automator). Notably, the automated LLM pass missed what the authors call "Kairos deficits," failing to distinguish deliberate slowness from service failure.

**Why it matters:** It puts customer language behind a suspicion many premium operators have: automation gains that look free on the P&L can be billed to trust, loyalty and price premium, where no one is measuring.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Before rolling out the next automation in a premium channel, consider separating journeys where customers want speed from those where waiting is the product — and note the methodological warning that an LLM reading your review pile may not flag the difference between intentional pacing and a broken experience.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If a high-end hotel, restaurant or store feels oddly brisk and transactional now, you're not imagining it — and reviewers are describing exactly that loss in their own words.

**Caveats:** Review text is self-selected and skews to extremes; only 2,000 of 1.2 million reviews were human-coded; the archetype framework is interpretive and derived from the data rather than independently tested; confined to luxury chains with Google Maps presence; heavy conceptual vocabulary built on a modest coded sample.

### 16. Foreign-branded universities: students say the country ties matter more than the price

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing for HIGHER EDUCATION* | **Published:** 2026-10-12
**Work ID:** W7221546035 | **DOI:** 10.1080/08841241.2026.2745125

**The study:** An online survey of 1,199 students enrolled at Canadian-, Chinese- and US-affiliated universities located in Bangladesh, Egypt and the United Arab Emirates asked what drove their choice of a "country-branded university." Institutional reputation, perceived service quality, country brand authenticity and financial factors each influenced at least 70% of respondents. In all three institutions, country brand authenticity outranked financial factors — nearly three-quarters of students cited genuine ties to the foreign country as a reason for enrolling.

**Why it matters:** For the fast-growing transnational education market, it suggests the borrowed national brand is a core product attribute, not decoration — and that students are watching whether the foreign connection is real.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If your brand licenses or borrows a country identity, the finding implies the authenticity of that link — faculty, curriculum, degree parity, actual institutional involvement — may be worth more in recruitment messaging than tuition discounting; a hypothesis worth testing in enrollment data.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Families weighing an overseas-branded campus at home should ask what the foreign affiliation concretely delivers, because students in this study were buying the authenticity of that tie as much as the education.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey of stated, retrospective reasons rather than observed choice behavior; three host countries and three affiliated institutions only; cannot establish causation; results are largely descriptive percentages.

### 17. Faith-aligned features won't save a glitchy app: evidence from Islamic mobile banking reviews

**Journal:** *Journal of Islamic marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-02
**Work ID:** W7216405737 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jima-10-2025-0713

**The study:** Researchers applied lexicon-based text mining to customer reviews of Islamic mobile banking apps in Indonesia and built panel data regression models linking service dimensions and expressed emotions to satisfaction. Ease of use, convenience, perceived usefulness and adherence to Islamic attributes all contributed to satisfaction, while negative emotions in reviews dragged it down. The practical upshot: complaints clustered around system updates, verification processes, core banking functionality and stability — basic reliability, not religious compliance.

**Why it matters:** Values-based positioning raises expectations of service parity rather than excusing shortfalls; customers appear to treat competent, reliable execution as part of the ethical promise.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For any brand built on a values proposition — faith-based, ethical, sustainable — this is a prompt to audit whether operational basics meet mainstream category standards, since the review data suggest a failed login can read to customers as a broken promise.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It's a reminder that what people actually write in app reviews is mostly about things working, even when they chose the app for its principles.

**Caveats:** Single country (Indonesia) and single category; app-review text is self-selected and skews toward extremes; lexicon-based sentiment coding is approximate; associational models, not causal.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**

- **Frontline staff remain the gating variable — now on the labor side, not just the technology side.** The previous run's "When the AI is wrong, frontline staff stop thinking like customers" (W7214671269) established that governance and discretion decide whether service tech pays off. Three new studies extend the same logic to job design: "Asking Service Staff to Sell: Whether It Energizes or Burns Them Out Depends on the Climate You Set" (W7221728768), "Not all workplace stress is bad" (W7221898122) and "Rude customers make staff less caring — and the boss's favorites take it hardest" (W7220440663) all find that identical demands produce opposite outcomes depending on how they are framed, bounded and backed up.
- **Large language models reading customer feedback miss the relational layer.** "Speed is a luxury killer" (W7220919414) reports that an automated LLM pass failed to distinguish deliberate slowness from service failure — the same shortfall documented last run in "ChatGPT and HR managers read the same guest complaints" (W7214601318), where the model was strong on operational pattern recognition and thin on interactional repair.
- **Review and complaint corpora are consolidating into the default CX measurement instrument.** "What a million words of app-store griping reveals about fintech users' well-being" (W7222527276), "Faith-aligned features won't save a glitchy app" (W7216405737), W7220919414 and the 141,000-complaint analysis (W7204606942) continue the method established last run by "There is no universal recipe for good service" (W7202253581) and the halal luxury hotel review study (W7203443165).
- **Luxury service is being hollowed out by its own growth and efficiency plays.** W7220919414 finds automation strips the unhurried "relational time" that justifies premium pricing, sitting directly alongside the previous digest's "reversed conspicuousness" finding in luxury brand extensions (W7214625896).
- **Humanoid interfaces still carry a penalty.** "Customers find humanoid service robots creepy" (W7220660112) echoes "The more human your AI looks, the harder customers fall when it fails" (W7204275888) and "When a service robot fumbles, the customers watching are the ones who walk away" (W7203866299).

**Advances**

- **Robot deployment gets a boundary condition.** Where the previous batch established the anthropomorphism penalty, W7220660112 identifies where it reverses: in embarrassing service contexts the human employee's own creepiness rating rises while the robot's does not — a selection rule rather than a blanket verdict.
- **Recovery triage extends from channel to social context and loyalty tier.** "Fixing a Bad Meal? Compensate the Friend Too" (W7221159140) adds companion presence and tier-contingent payout size to the triage logic previously built on failure severity and remedy type (W7201883691, W7203609438).
- **Crisis attribution moves from who spreads it to how close it feels.** "How close the disaster feels decides whether tourists boycott a destination" (W7221524554) adds social proximity and felt relative deprivation to the attribution work in "In an online firestorm, it's the obsessives who spread the damage" (W7213665568) and "When AI fails customers, apologize" (W7172201743). "When your hashtag gets hijacked, the angriest posts aren't the ones that spread" (W7206212528) pushes further, finding the negativity effect breaks down inside already-hostile threads.
- **Service exclusion acquires a quantitative anchor.** The previous run's qualitative thread — the self-checkout efficiency paradox (W7197051994) and disabled customers' dignity accounts (W7204466776) — is now matched by W7204606942, which links comprehension and action burden to dispute escalation in economically disadvantaged areas across 141,251 complaints.

**Counterpoints**

- **Transparency is being reframed rather than rejected.** The previous batch suggested less communication can be safer ("When a package is late, saying nothing may protect the retailer," W7172357977) and that more tracking detail yields little for hurried shoppers (W7212038717). The new evidence shifts the variable: W7222527276 names information ambiguity — unclear fees, statuses, error messages — as actively harmful to well-being, and W7204606942 finds comprehension and the ability to act, not disclosure volume, predict escalation. The question is becoming clarity, not quantity.
- **Values-based segments may need fewer bespoke attributes than last run implied.** The halal luxury hotel review study (W7203443165) surfaced service-quality themes distinctive to the faith-based setting; W7216405737 finds complaints about Islamic mobile banking cluster on logins, verification and stability rather than religious compliance, suggesting values positioning raises expectations of category-standard execution rather than creating a separate scorecard.

**New themes**

- **Market structure as a customer-experience variable.** "When Rival Airlines Share the Same Big Investors, Service Gets Worse and Fares Go Up" (W7223706258) brings common ownership — a corporate-finance phenomenon — into the marketing mix, with no precedent in the previous digest.
- **Customer-side predictors of frontline abuse.** "Devout Customers Reported More Willingness to Be Rude to Staff — Unless Karma Was on Their Minds" (W7223658951) opens a line the previous run only addressed from the employee side.
- **Informal and micro-retail competing with quick commerce.** "Corner shops can win the speed game without any of the quick-commerce infrastructure" (W7215042272) is a new emerging-market retail thread.
- **B2B and specialty-category basics.** "For small-business clients, getting the order right beats having the slickest logistics tech" (W7215042510) and "Gluten-free shoppers aren't asking for more choice" (W7222470326) both argue against feature and assortment expansion in favor of execution — new territory for this topic.
- **Borrowed national brands as a product attribute.** "Foreign-branded universities" (W7221546035) introduces country brand authenticity as a service-choice driver.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Four studies in this batch converge on the same unmeasured input to customer experience — the condition of the people delivering it. Customer incivility eroded staff capacity for compassionate service through felt uncertainty, and hit employees with the strongest manager relationships hardest ("Rude customers make staff less caring," W7220440663); bolting sales targets onto service roles energized or burned out identical workers depending on whether management framed risk as opportunity or error ("Asking Service Staff to Sell," W7221728768); the type of pressure, not the amount, predicted theft and service sabotage, with bureaucratic friction the corrosive kind ("Not all workplace stress is bad," W7221898122); and one of the clearest predictors of abuse directed at staff turned out to be a customer trait no firm tracks ("Devout Customers Reported More Willingness to Be Rude to Staff," W7223658951).

**Why pitch this now:** Retailers, banks and hospitality groups are simultaneously adding sales quotas and AI tools to shrinking frontline teams, reporting shrinkage as a board-level problem, and describing customer abuse as a retention crisis — and this evidence says those three stories are the same story.

**Angle:** A reported feature on the frontline as an uncosted line item in the experience economy: how abusive customers, badly framed dual roles and approval bottlenecks quietly produce the thin, transactional service brands then try to fix with technology. For CX, operations and HR leaders, with a general-interest version on what rudeness at the counter actually does to the next person in line.

**Potential headlines:**
- Abusive customers measurably reduce how kindly staff treat everyone after them
- Pointless paperwork, not hard targets, is what pushes frontline staff to cut corners
- Your shrinkage problem might be a job-design problem

**Potential outlets:**
- **Retail Dive** — covers frontline labor, shrinkage and store operations as linked problems, which is exactly the connection the stressor-type evidence makes.
- **MIT Sloan Management Review** — the climate-and-appraisal findings are a job-design argument for managers, with a clean testable prescription rather than a general plea for kindness.
- **Restaurant Business** — hospitality operators are the readers living the customer-abuse and tip-era civility problem, and the companion-compensation and incivility work both land in their rooms.
- **Vox** — strong on everyday social behavior explained through research, and the "who lashes out at waitstaff, and what actually curbs it" material is a natural consumer-facing piece.

## Story Ideas by Study

- **What 141,000 Consumer Complaints Reveal About Why Disputes Escalate in Poorer Neighborhoods (Work ID W7204606942):** Disputes track with whether consumers can understand and act on a remedy rather than the size of the loss, which makes disclosure-heavy recovery a misdiagnosis in financial services, for a personal-finance column or a financial-services trade feature.
- **When Rival Airlines Share the Same Big Investors, Service Gets Worse and Fares Go Up (Work ID W7223706258):** Thirty years of U.S. airline data linking overlapping institutional ownership to higher fares and thinner service, with customer-asset value as the apparent defense, for a business or antitrust-focused general-interest piece.
- **Fixing a Bad Meal? Compensate the Friend Too (Work ID W7221159140):** Loyalty tier plus who else is at the table changes the return on the same recovery dollar — small gestures to the whole party for ordinary members, larger exclusive ones for top tiers, for a hospitality trade explainer or a loyalty-marketing brief.
- **Asking Service Staff to Sell (Work ID W7221728768):** The same sell-and-serve mandate produces engagement or burnout depending on whether the workplace climate signals growth or error-avoidance, for a CMO- or COO-audience column on sales targets in service roles.
- **Devout Customers Reported More Willingness to Be Rude to Staff (Work ID W7223658951):** Self-reported piety tracked with less forgiveness of the individual employee, while karma-style consequence thinking softened it — a counterintuitive consumer-psychology explainer for a general-interest outlet.
- **Not all workplace stress is bad (Work ID W7221898122):** Challenge stressors were associated with less theft and sabotage while red tape was associated with more, which argues for clearing bottlenecks before cutting targets, for a retail operations or loss-prevention trade feature.
- **Customers find humanoid service robots creepy — except when the service is embarrassing (Work ID W7220660112):** A deployment rule instead of a verdict — put robots where human presence is itself the discomfort, such as pharmacy pickup or intake, for a retail-technology trade explainer.
- **Gluten-free shoppers aren't asking for more choice (Work ID W7222470326):** Quality and contamination safety outranked assortment breadth, with bread, pastry and ready meals named as the still-failing categories, for a grocery or food-industry trade brief.
- **How close the disaster feels decides whether tourists boycott a destination (Work ID W7221524554):** Crisis playbooks grade incidents by severity; this grades them by social proximity to the audience and routes the damage through felt unfairness, for a travel trade outlet or a crisis-communications column.
- **For small-business clients, getting the order right beats having the slickest logistics tech (Work ID W7215042510):** SME buyers rated order accuracy and a named human contact above tracking platforms and brand credentials, for a B2B logistics or supply-chain trade brief.
- **Corner shops can win the speed game without any of the quick-commerce infrastructure (Work ID W7215042272):** Perceived immediacy, deliverable with proximity and a chat app, rivals dark-store logistics in dense urban markets, for a retail trade feature on quick commerce in emerging markets.
- **When your hashtag gets hijacked, the angriest posts aren't the ones that spread (Work ID W7206212528):** Within an already hostile thread, extra outrage loses its marginal shock value — a challenge to treating every negative spike as equally contagious, for a PR or social-marketing trade outlet.
- **What a million words of app-store griping reveals about fintech users' well-being (Work ID W7222527276):** Unclear fees, statuses and error messages are an active harm, not a minor annoyance, and complaint handling reads as a well-being lever rather than a cost center, for a fintech trade outlet or a consumer-money column.
- **Rude customers make staff less caring — and the boss's favorites take it hardest (Work ID W7220440663):** The surprise is that strong manager relationships amplified rather than buffered the damage, which undercuts the standard leadership remedy, for a management or HR-focused column.
- **Speed is a luxury killer (Work ID W7220919414):** Reviewers describe premium brands trading relational time for efficiency, and the LLM reading the reviews couldn't tell intentional pacing from a broken experience, for a luxury business feature or a data-and-measurement piece for analytics-minded readers.
- **Foreign-branded universities: students say the country ties matter more than the price (Work ID W7221546035):** Country brand authenticity outranked financial factors across three host markets, making the realness of the affiliation a recruitment asset rather than decoration, for a higher-education trade outlet.
- **Faith-aligned features won't save a glitchy app (Work ID W7216405737):** For any values-based brand, a failed login reads as a broken promise — complaints clustered on stability and verification, not religious compliance, for a brand-strategy column or a fintech trade brief.
