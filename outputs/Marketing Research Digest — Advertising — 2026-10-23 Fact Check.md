### Study 3: People enjoy AI-made ads — until they are told an AI made them
**Work ID:** W7223070691 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline reverses the enjoyment finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "People enjoy AI-made ads — until they are told an AI made them"
- **Abstract says:** "Creative advertisements elicited joy, captured by facial expression analysis, regardless of whether they were created by humans or AI... These findings suggest that AI disclosure does not diminish initial enjoyment. Instead, it may act as a persuasion knowledge cue that activates cognitive regulation."
- **Problem:** The abstract explicitly says disclosure did NOT reduce enjoyment; it changed how viewers translated that enjoyment into an evaluation. The headline implies the enjoyment itself stopped.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to something like: 'People still enjoy AI-made ads once told an AI made them — they just stop letting that enjoyment count.'"

### Study 5: Telling people an ad was made by AI makes it seem less creative — unless you give the AI a job title
**Work ID:** W7222851502 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Untested ethical-vs-aesthetic claim** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "People don't object to AI ads on ethical grounds so much as aesthetic ones — the word "AI" primes us to see the work as less creative before we've even judged it on merit."
- **Abstract says:** "Across three studies, we show that AI disclosure reduces consumers’ attitudes toward the advertisement and purchase intentions through decreased perceived creativity."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports perceived creativity as the mediator; it does not test or compare ethical objections, so the digest's comparative claim about why people object is not supported.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rewrite as: 'The tested mechanism was perceived creativity — the AI label made the work seem less creative, which dragged down attitudes and intentions. The study did not test ethical objections.'"

**Issue B. Mechanism relabelled as 'credibility'** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "positioning AI as a team member rather than a replacement for human creativity may blunt the credibility hit"
- **Abstract says:** "the negative influence of AI disclosure (vs. human designer disclosure) is attenuated when AI is identified as an employee"
- **Problem:** The measured outcomes were ad attitude and purchase intention via perceived creativity; 'credibility' is not an outcome in the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say 'may blunt the hit to perceived creativity and ad attitude'."

### Study 8: A short sustainability class changed how students responded to green advertising
**Work ID:** W7220917637 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. 'Why it matters' overreaches on the PCE mechanism** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "It suggests green advertising only works on audiences already primed to believe individual action matters"
- **Abstract says:** "Greater reported exposure was associated with higher PCE and stronger self-reported responsible consumption intentions, with PCE mediating that association. Study 2 uses a 2 × 2 experiment... Students who received the training reported higher purchase intentions for a sustainability-framed advertisement than for a conventional advertisement, whereas this difference did not emerge among students who did not receive the training."
- **Problem:** PCE was measured as a mediator in a cross-sectional student survey, not tested as a moderator of ad response in the experiment; the digest treats it as the established precondition for green advertising to work, and 'only works' is stronger than the evidence.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe: 'Brief sustainability training made students more responsive to a green-framed ad; a separate survey links curricular exposure to beliefs that individual choices matter, though that link is correlational.'"

### Study 12: Body-positive fashion ads work best on people who already believe in body positivity — and can backfire with the body-confident
**Work ID:** W7222466110 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Backfire" overstates correlational result** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Body-positive fashion ads work best on people who already believe in body positivity — and can backfire with the body-confident"
- **Abstract says:** "Purchase intentions were enhanced among consumers with favorable body-positivity attitudes. Conversely, purchase intentions were lower among consumers who already possess a high body self-image."
- **Problem:** "Backfire" implies an ad-caused negative reaction; the abstract reports lower stated purchase intentions in a cross-sectional survey, i.e., an association, not a demonstrated adverse effect. (The abstract's practical implications do use "alienate," but that is an authors' inference.)
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "and land less well with the body-confident" or "are associated with lower purchase intentions among the body-confident.""

### Study 13: Consumers put AI-made ads on trial — and heavy AI users aren't the most forgiving
**Work ID:** W7221622840 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline misstates the AI-use finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Consumers put AI-made ads on trial — and heavy AI users aren't the most forgiving"
- **Abstract says:** "AI familiarity and perceived AI literacy shaped the interpretive resources participants used in making these judgements, but frequent AI use did not necessarily imply critical understanding."
- **Problem:** The abstract says frequent AI use did not necessarily bring critical understanding — a statement about depth of understanding, not about leniency or harshness. "Aren't the most forgiving" implies a comparison of favorability that the abstract does not report.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to something like "— and heavy AI users aren't necessarily more discerning.""

**Issue B. Hedge dropped** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "but frequent AI use did not translate into more critical or sophisticated understanding"
- **Abstract says:** "frequent AI use did not necessarily imply critical understanding"
- **Problem:** The abstract hedges with "not necessarily"; the digest states a flat null, which is firmer than the qualitative evidence supports.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add the hedge: "did not necessarily imply more critical understanding.""

### Study 14: Those stadium ad boards? Most viewers can't remember them — unless they love the home team
**Work ID:** W7223185198 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Unsupported recall rate implied in headline** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Those stadium ad boards? Most viewers can't remember them — unless they love the home team"
- **Abstract says:** "recall differed substantially across individual brands, and home-team fans showed higher recall for several prominent sponsors than non-home-team fans."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports relative differences in recall, not an absolute level; nothing supports "most viewers can't remember them."
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe around the comparison, e.g., "Recall of stadium ad boards depends on the brand — and on whether you support the home team.""

**Issue B. Brand familiarity presented as a finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "it depends on who the viewer supports and whether the brand is already familiar"
- **Abstract says:** "sponsor recall in realistic broadcast settings is likely shaped by the interplay of team identification, sponsor–team relevance, prior brand familiarity, and viewing context"
- **Problem:** Prior brand familiarity is offered by the authors as a likely explanation, not as a measured result; the digest presents it as established.
- **Suggested fix:** "Note that familiarity is an author-suggested explanation for the brand-level differences rather than a tested variable."

### Study 15: 180,000 hotel reviews show Americans punish weak sustainability claims hardest
**Work ID:** W7223212068 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Superlative beyond the comparison set** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "180,000 hotel reviews show Americans punish weak sustainability claims hardest"
- **Abstract says:** "American consumers prioritize individual expectations and express stronger criticism when sustainability efforts fall short"
- **Problem:** The abstract compares three countries and reports stronger criticism in text, not punishment or a global superlative; "punish" also implies behavioral consequences (bookings, ratings penalties) not established.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "US reviewers voice the sharpest criticism when green claims fall short — compared with South Korean and Indian reviewers.""

**Issue B. Downside-risk inference** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the downside risk of overclaiming on sustainability is concentrated in individualist markets like the US"
- **Abstract says:** "American consumers prioritize individual expectations and express stronger criticism when sustainability efforts fall short"
- **Problem:** Generalizing from US/Korea/India review text to "individualist markets" as a class, and to business risk, goes beyond the observational data.
- **Suggested fix:** "Limit to the three markets studied and frame as a difference in review tone rather than measured risk."

### Study 16: A telecom's 174,000 ad records reveal threshold effects: more frequency helps, but only in steps
**Work ID:** W7223172682 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Rounded R-squared without variance** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "XGBoost fit best (test R-squared 0.82)."
- **Abstract says:** "with a test set coefficient of determination of 0.8236 ± 0.2061"
- **Problem:** Rounding is fine, but the large ± 0.2061 spread is central to how much confidence the fit deserves; it appears only vaguely in the caveats as "high variance."
- **Suggested fix:** "Report as "test R-squared 0.82 (±0.21)" in the body text."

**Issue B. Causal framing in caveat** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "limiting how much is genuinely new causal insight"
- **Abstract says:** "Specification tests indicate that ACTE interpretability primarily stems from algebraic relationships of constitutive features, with limited independent contributions from non-constitutive features."
- **Problem:** The study is predictive/associational and never offers causal insight; calling the limitation one of "causal insight" implies the rest of the work is causal.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "limiting how much is genuinely new explanatory insight.""

### Study 17: A paid Facebook campaign reached 48,000 young Ghanaians — but WhatsApp forwards brought in the applicants

### Study 18: Knowing you're being tracked makes some people block ads — and others lean in
**Work ID:** W7222483887 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal headline for survey data** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Knowing you're being tracked makes some people block ads — and others lean in"
- **Abstract says:** "Employing an online survey (N = 327)... Conceptual persuasion knowledge was directly associated with competitive and cooperative evaluations, as well as sentry and seeker response strategies."
- **Problem:** "Makes" asserts causation from a cross-sectional survey of self-reported strategies; the abstract reports associations only. The caveats acknowledge this but the headline and "Why it matters" still use causal language.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "Awareness of tracking is linked to blocking for some people — and leaning in for others.""

**Issue B. Direct awareness-to-strategy path omitted** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Awareness of how tracking works predicted skepticism but not perceptions of fairness."
- **Abstract says:** "Conceptual persuasion knowledge was directly associated with competitive and cooperative evaluations, as well as sentry and seeker response strategies."
- **Problem:** The digest reports only the skepticism/fairness result and omits that tactic awareness was directly associated with both response strategies, which is relevant to the digest's own claim about education splitting audiences.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add a clause noting awareness was also directly associated with both seeker and sentry strategies."

### Study 21: Annoying AI food ads still made people hungry — survey of 783 Vietnamese social media users
**Work ID:** W7213274355 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal phrasing in headline and body** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Annoying AI food ads still made people hungry — survey of 783 Vietnamese social media users"
- **Abstract says:** "Survey data were collected from 783 Vietnamese social media users ... Informativeness, entertainment, irritation and credibility were positively associated with both inspired-by and inspired-to"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports associations from a cross-sectional PLS-SEM survey; "made people hungry" (and in the consumer angle, "still nudged them toward ordering") asserts a causal effect the design cannot support. The Caveats flag the correlational nature, but the headline and body text contradict that caveat.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to association language, e.g. "Ads rated as irritating were still linked to higher inspiration and self-reported ordering"."

**Issue B. Which skepticism path was moderated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Skepticism toward AI-generated content weakened the link between feeling inspired and actually ordering"
- **Abstract says:** "Skepticism toward AI-generated food advertising weakened the inspired-by–behavior link"
- **Problem:** The abstract specifies the moderation applied to the inspired-by–behavior link only, not inspiration generally; the digest generalizes across both inspiration states.
- **Suggested fix:** "Specify that skepticism weakened the 'inspired-by'-to-ordering link specifically, one of the two inspiration stages."

**Issue C. Moderator label** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "while sheer familiarity with AI content had no moderating effect"
- **Abstract says:** "whereas AI exposure showed no significant moderating effect"
- **Problem:** The abstract's moderator is "AI exposure", which the digest renames as "familiarity with AI content" — a close but not identical construct.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use the abstract's term, e.g. "prior AI exposure showed no moderating effect"."
