### Study 1: Store brands pay off less on their own shelf space than at the negotiating table
**Work ID:** W7203936299 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Relative size of indirect vs. direct gains** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Crucially, much of the profit came indirectly, via better terms extracted from national brands"
- **Abstract says:** "These gains arise both through improved direct profits from the private label, and indirectly through a better bargaining outcome on national-brand products."
- **Problem:** The abstract states that gains arise through both channels without ranking them; the digest (and the headline, "pay off less on their own shelf space than at the negotiating table") asserts a magnitude comparison the abstract does not support.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "profit came through two channels — direct private-label margin and better terms extracted from national brands" and soften the headline to "Store brands pay off on the shelf and at the negotiating table.""

**Issue B. "Drug patent" detail** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "a drug patent expiration that coincided with waves of private-label entry"
- **Abstract says:** "Exploiting a novel setting in which a patent expiration concided with private-label entries"
- **Problem:** The abstract does not identify the patent as a drug patent, nor describe "waves" of entry.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say "a patent expiration that coincided with private-label entry" or mark the drug detail as unverifiable from the abstract."

### Study 4: Hiring an ex-official to your board measurably improves government sales — except when procurement is truly competitive
**Work ID:** W7210266064 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Dollar-value claim** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "This is a rare dollar-value read on the revolving door"
- **Abstract says:** "the authors find that revolving door directors exert significantly positive effects on three dimensions of customer relationship performance: customer acquisition, customer retention, and cross-selling performance"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports effects on customer relationship performance dimensions, not a monetary valuation of revolving door directors; readers are told the study quantified dollars when nothing in the abstract does.
- **Suggested fix:** "Replace with "a rare quantitative read on the revolving door" or describe the outcomes as acquisition, retention and cross-selling performance."

**Issue B. Causal headline on observational panel data** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Hiring an ex-official to your board measurably improves government sales"
- **Abstract says:** "By analyzing multisource, secondary panel data of 1,731 publicly traded U.S. firms in the B2G market between 2005 and 2021, the authors find that revolving door directors exert significantly positive effects"
- **Problem:** The headline states a causal effect of a hiring decision from secondary panel data; the body and caveats correctly hedge to association, so the headline is out of step.
- **Suggested fix:** "Soften to "is linked to better government sales" to match the body text and caveats."

### Study 5: Return fraud isn't driven by lenient policies — it's driven by peer norms and money pressure
**Work ID:** W7212072927 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal framing of survey findings** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Return fraud isn't driven by lenient policies — it's driven by peer norms and money pressure"
- **Abstract says:** "Study 1 shows that social norms, financial pressure (pressure) and trait cynicism (rationalisation) have a significant impact on opportunistic return behaviour, while policy leniency (opportunity) and policy knowledge (capability) do not have a significant impact."
- **Problem:** "Driven by" implies causation from a cross-sectional survey; the caveats acknowledge this but the headline and "Why it matters" do not.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "is associated with" / "predicted by" in the headline and Why it matters."

**Issue B. "Perceived anonymity" added as a driver** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "generous policies aren't the trigger — social permission and perceived anonymity are"
- **Abstract says:** "situational factors like feeling of being monitored, digital reputation signals, type of product, return fee structure and anti-wardrobing rules are the key factors"
- **Problem:** The abstract names "feeling of being monitored" as a situational factor from the interviews; "perceived anonymity" is the digest's inversion and was not measured as a driver.
- **Suggested fix:** "Rewrite as "social permission and the absence of a sense of being monitored.""

### Study 9: AI-redesigned coffee packs won a shelf test — but only barely, and not for everyone

### Study 18: Eye-tracking shows busy product images make shoppers work harder — and feel less
**Work ID:** W7204047411 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Direction of perceptual fluency effect reversed** — Severity: Major

- **As written:** "Lower perceptual fluency was linked to reduced emotional arousal and attentional effort, an effect especially pronounced for functional products."
- **Abstract says:** "perceptual fluency shows a negative association with both emotional arousal and attentional effort, especially for functional products"
- **Problem:** A negative association means that lower perceptual fluency goes with HIGHER emotional arousal and attentional effort. The digest states the opposite, and this reversal contradicts the digest's own headline ("make shoppers work harder").
- **Suggested fix:** "Rewrite as "Perceptual fluency was negatively associated with emotional arousal and attentional effort — harder-to-process images went with more arousal and more effort, especially for functional products.""

**Issue B. "Why it matters" built on the reversed direction** — Severity: Major

- **As written:** "for utilitarian products, visual busyness appears to dampen both attention and emotional response"
- **Abstract says:** "perceptual fluency shows a negative association with both emotional arousal and attentional effort, especially for functional products"
- **Problem:** Because the underlying direction is reversed, the managerial takeaway is backwards: the abstract implies busier images draw more attentional effort and arousal via reduced fluency, not less.
- **Suggested fix:** "Recast the takeaway around what the abstract supports: complexity lowers processing fluency, which is associated with greater attentional effort and arousal, while element and colour complexity also impair conceptual fluency."

**Issue C. Consumer angle repeats the reversal** — Severity: Major

- **As written:** "when an image is hard to parse, browsers feel less and look less"
- **Abstract says:** "perceptual fluency shows a negative association with both emotional arousal and attentional effort"
- **Problem:** Hard-to-parse images correspond to low perceptual fluency, which the abstract associates with more arousal and more attentional effort, not less of either.
- **Suggested fix:** "Replace with a statement that hard-to-parse images make people work harder to process them, and drop the "feel less" claim."

**Issue D. Conceptual fluency result omitted** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "All three reduced perceptual fluency (how easily the image is processed), but only element and color complexity hurt conceptual fluency (how easily the meaning is grasped); layout complexity did not."
- **Abstract says:** "conceptual fluency positively influences attentional effort but does not significantly affect emotional arousal"
- **Problem:** The digest reports which dimensions affect conceptual fluency but drops what conceptual fluency then does, which is part of the dual-route story.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add that conceptual fluency was positively related to attentional effort but not to emotional arousal."

### Study 20: Two flavors of search ads on the same platform behave very differently when bad reviews pile up
**Work ID:** W7204166660 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Shopper-level mechanism inferred from ad performance data** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "shoppers appear to discount bad reviews when a listing carries a platform's authorized-brand credential, and to take them far more seriously on ordinary marketplace listings"
- **Abstract says:** "while SSA_Taobao's performance is sensitive to negative reviews (both intra- and inter-channel), SSA_Tmall remains ro-bust against such feedback"
- **Problem:** The study analyses seller-level sponsored-search sales performance; it does not measure how individual shoppers weigh reviews, so attributing the pattern to shopper discounting is an untested interpretation.
- **Suggested fix:** "Frame it as one possible explanation, e.g. "ad returns on Tmall storefronts held up as negative reviews accumulated, while marketplace-store ads did not — one possible reading is that the authorised-brand badge buffers doubt.""
