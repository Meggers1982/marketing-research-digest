### 1. AI-generated diversity backfires: synthetic models of color cost brands credibility

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-10-20
**Work ID:** W7223056505 | **DOI:** 10.1002/jcpy.70044

**The study:** Across five experiments, researchers tested how consumers react when ads disclose that the human models pictured were AI-generated. Disclosure lowered evaluations of the firm when the synthetic models were Black, Hispanic or Asian, but not when they were White — an asymmetry driven by doubts about whether the brand's inclusion efforts were authentic, which in turn fed suspicions of manipulative intent. The penalty disappeared when consumers were told the firm was owned by members of the depicted racial community.

**Why it matters:** Brands are adopting synthetic models as a cheap, scalable route to more diverse casting, and this evidence suggests that shortcut can read as hollow precisely where representation is supposed to count most.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you are piloting AI-generated talent, test disclosure effects by cast composition rather than in aggregate — and consider what verifiable signals of community ties (ownership, partnerships, creator credits) you can pair with synthetic imagery. These are controlled experiments, so treat the asymmetry as a risk to monitor in live campaigns, not a settled law.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For consumers, it is a window into how quickly inclusive imagery curdles into suspicion: representation that costs a brand nothing to produce may be read as representation the brand does not actually mean.

**Caveats:** Experimental studies, likely with online panels; evaluations and inferred intent rather than purchases; the ownership-signal remedy was tested in a controlled setting and racial categories and meanings are culturally specific.

### 2. Eye-tracking study: a crowded page pulls more eyeballs to ads — and makes each one less memorable

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing Research* | **Published:** 2026-10-15
**Work ID:** W7222550305 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00222437261498868

**The study:** Three eye-tracking experiments (N=99, N=250 and N=575) used more than a dozen purpose-built web pages to vary how many ads appeared and where. More clutter increased total attention devoted to the advertising space overall and shifted how viewers spread their gaze across ad positions, but a companion survey showed clutter reduced memory and recall for any individual ad.

**Why it matters:** It separates two things publishers and advertisers routinely conflate — whether an ad is looked at and whether it is remembered — with direct implications for how cluttered inventory should be priced.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Press publishers on position-level and clutter-level pricing: if adding units raises aggregate gaze but dilutes recall for your specific placement, viewability metrics may be flattering campaigns that are not registering. A natural test is matched buys on high- and low-clutter pages measured on brand recall, not just impressions.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It helps explain the everyday experience of browsing a busy site and remembering none of the ads you technically looked at — attention is not the same as memory.

**Caveats:** Lab-style eye-tracking on custom-built pages rather than live websites; recall measured via a complementary survey; short exposure and single-session design; generalization to mobile and social feeds untested.

### 3. People enjoy AI-made ads — until they are told an AI made them

**Journal:** *International Journal of Advertising* | **Published:** 2026-10-20
**Work ID:** W7223070691 | **DOI:** 10.1080/02650487.2026.2748259

**The study:** In a three-condition between-subjects experiment using facial expression analysis, participants watched creative ads labeled as having no disclosure, human–AI collaboration, or full AI creation. Creative ads produced measurable joy regardless of who or what made them, and greater joy predicted less counterarguing and more favorable ad attitudes — but only when AI involvement was not disclosed. With disclosure, viewers appeared to override their own positive emotional reaction when forming a judgment.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the problem with AI advertising is not the output quality but the label: disclosure functions as a persuasion-knowledge cue that triggers deliberate correction of a genuinely positive gut response.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If regulation or platform policy forces AI disclosure, the creative question becomes how to frame the label rather than whether the work lands emotionally — worth testing collaboration framings, placement and wording of disclosures against unlabeled controls.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A useful self-awareness check for viewers: the study implies people talk themselves out of liking something once they learn a machine helped make it, even though the enjoyment was real.

**Caveats:** Single experiment with one set of ad stimuli; facial-coding and attitude measures rather than purchase behavior; sample size and country not specified in the abstract; immediate post-exposure measurement only.

### 4. Knowing an ad is an ad does not stop you scrolling past it the same way

**Journal:** *International Journal of Advertising* | **Published:** 2026-10-15
**Work ID:** W7221986862 | **DOI:** 10.1080/02650487.2026.2739113

**The study:** Combining eye-tracking, scrolling data and engagement measures in a naturalistic social-media browsing task, researchers used latent class analysis to identify three profiles of users differing in persuasion knowledge and social-media use motivations. After correcting for classification error, the profiles showed no significant differences in visual attention to native ads, scrolling behavior or engagement — a null result that held in a robustness check on the fully exposed subsample.

**Why it matters:** It challenges the assumption behind much ad-literacy policy and self-regulation: in casual, low-involvement feed browsing, understanding persuasive intent does not visibly change what people look at or engage with.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Be skeptical of claims that savvier audiences automatically discount your sponsored content — in low-involvement browsing, literacy may not show up in behavior at all, which cuts both ways for brands relying on native formats and for those fearing backlash.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For readers, it is a reminder that spotting an ad and resisting it are different skills; in-feed attention seems to run largely on autopilot.

**Caveats:** A null finding, which can reflect limited power or the specific low-involvement browsing context; single study, single platform-style environment; profiles derived from self-reported knowledge and motivations; short session.

### 5. Telling people an ad was made by AI makes it seem less creative — unless you give the AI a job title

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-10-17
**Work ID:** W7222851502 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105175

**The study:** Across three experiments, researchers tested how consumers react when an advertisement is labeled as AI-generated versus human-designed. Disclosing AI use lowered attitudes toward the ad and purchase intentions, and the drop ran through a single mechanism: people judged the AI-made work as less creative. The penalty shrank depending on the product type, and notably when the AI was framed as an "employee" of the firm rather than an anonymous tool.

**Why it matters:** With disclosure rules for AI-generated content tightening in several markets, brands need to know what the label actually costs them and how the framing of that label changes the damage.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you must disclose AI involvement, test the wording: these experiments suggest that positioning AI as a team member rather than a replacement for human creativity may blunt the credibility hit. Worth running as a live A/B test on real campaigns before assuming the lab result scales.
- **For general readers and consumers:** People don't object to AI ads on ethical grounds so much as aesthetic ones — the word "AI" primes us to see the work as less creative before we've even judged it on merit.

**Caveats:** Online experiments with ad stimuli rather than observed purchases; stated attitudes and intentions; product categories and sample details limited in the abstract.

### 6. AI-made video ads work better when they sell with logic, not feelings — and human-like AI characters creep people out

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-10-13
**Work ID:** W7221595688 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105162

**The study:** Five between-subjects experiments with consumers recruited from an online panel crossed advertising appeal (rational vs. emotional), product type (high-tech vs. low-tech), and whether the AI-generated video featured no character, an animal-like character, or a human-like one. Rational appeals outperformed emotional appeals in AI-generated ads, especially for low-tech products. Perceived novelty lifted ad attitude while perceived uncanniness depressed it, and human-like AI characters amplified uncanniness specifically in emotional, low-tech executions.

**Why it matters:** As generative video tools move into mainstream ad production, this gives creative teams a first map of which AI-made executions land and which ones backfire.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands experimenting with generative video should consider steering AI-produced spots toward rational, informational appeals and away from photorealistic human characters in emotional storytelling — then validate with in-market creative testing, since these are panel responses to test stimuli.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The uncanny valley is now showing up in your social feed: synthetic humans selling emotional stories about everyday products are the configuration viewers find most unsettling.

**Caveats:** Online panel sample; forced exposure to test ads rather than natural viewing; stated attitudes, not behavior; one of the character effects did not translate into a significant difference in ad attitude.

### 7. "We're passing advertising revenue back to you" beats "special offer" as a discount explanation

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Behaviour* | **Published:** 2026-10-16
**Work ID:** W7222624740 | **DOI:** 10.1002/cb.70267

**The study:** Two experiments tested how consumers judge advertising-funded discounts — deals paid for by advertisers rather than the retailer — depending on how the discount is explained. A "fair-exchange" framing, presenting the discount as a structural return of ad revenue to the shopper, raised perceived legitimacy and, through it, purchase intention, compared with a conventional promotional framing. Suspicions of manipulative intent were more situational, surfacing mainly in utilitarian purchase contexts where they dragged purchase intention down.

**Why it matters:** Ad-subsidized pricing is spreading through retail media networks and ad-supported subscription tiers, and the research suggests the explanation attached to the price is itself a persuasion lever.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If your business model subsidizes prices with advertising dollars, test explicitly telling shoppers how the money flows rather than dressing it up as a generic promotion — but watch for backlash in practical, utilitarian categories where manipulation suspicions run higher.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Shoppers are increasingly asked to accept ads as the price of a discount; this research shows we judge not just whether the deal is good, but whether the underlying arrangement feels fair.

**Caveats:** Two scenario-based experiments with hypothetical purchases and stated intentions; sample size and country not specified in the abstract; no observed transaction data.

### 8. A short sustainability class changed how students responded to green advertising

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing Communications* | **Published:** 2026-10-08
**Work ID:** W7220917637 | **DOI:** 10.1080/13527266.2026.2743031

**The study:** Two studies examined whether sustainability coursework shapes later responses to green marketing. A survey of students at two U.S. public universities found that more reported sustainability curriculum exposure was associated with a stronger belief that one's own choices matter (perceived consumer effectiveness) and stronger responsible consumption intentions, with that belief mediating the link. A follow-up 2×2 experiment with students at four U.S. public universities found that those given brief sustainability training reported higher purchase intentions for a sustainability-framed ad than a conventional one, while untrained students showed no such difference.

**Why it matters:** It suggests green advertising only works on audiences already primed to believe individual action matters — meaning the effectiveness of sustainability claims depends on what happens long before the ad is seen.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands leaning on sustainability messaging should segment by consumer efficacy beliefs rather than demographics, and consider whether their communications need to build the belief that choices matter before making the green claim at all.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Whether a green claim moves you may have less to do with the brand and more to do with whether you've been taught your purchases make a difference.

**Caveats:** Student samples only; self-reported curricular exposure and stated purchase intentions rather than behavior; Study 1 is cross-sectional and cannot establish causation; U.S. public universities only; short time horizon for the training manipulation.

### 9. When creators talk about themselves, the product stops selling

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-10-19
**Work ID:** W7222936501 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105171

**The study:** Researchers combined observational data from a short video commerce platform with a randomized experiment to test "embedded content" — videos that mix product pitches with the creator's own personal, self-focused storytelling. A higher share of self-focused content was associated with lower product sales, and the experiment traced the result to two opposing paths: personal content lowers ad avoidance (viewers keep watching) but also lowers attention to the product itself, with the attention loss dominating. Heterogeneity analyses found the damage was softened when the commercial pitch was timed well, when the creator showed more positive facial expressions, and when the product was visually prominent on screen.

**Why it matters:** The dominant creator-commerce playbook — wrap the sell inside a personal story so people don't scroll past — may buy watch time at the cost of conversions.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands briefing creators should ask for product salience metrics, not just retention metrics, and test embed timing and on-screen product prominence as separate levers; the study suggests engagement and sales can move in opposite directions in this format.
- **For general readers and consumers:** That charming personal tangent in a shopping livestream isn't an accident — it's designed to stop you from scrolling. Knowing that may help you notice when you've watched five minutes and can't remember what was being sold.

**Caveats:** Single platform and likely single market (short video commerce); observational component cannot fully rule out which creators choose which content styles; experimental purchase intentions supplement, but don't replace, the sales data.

### 10. Saying "this ad was made by AI" cuts participation in cause campaigns

**Journal:** *Journal of Research in Interactive Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-22
**Work ID:** W7223243344 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jrim-01-2026-0059

**The study:** Across five experiments — including one with an interactive webpage and a real sharing task — researchers tested how disclosing an AI source affects participation in sustainability campaigns, using samples in China and the US. Campaigns attributed to AI drew lower participation than identical campaigns attributed to humans, and the mechanism was reduced felt obligation toward the sponsoring organization rather than lower perceived sincerity or authenticity. The penalty was larger for warmth-based appeals and for people in a normative mindset, and smaller for competence-based appeals and instrumental decision-makers.

**Why it matters:** As AI disclosure rules spread, brands running purpose and sustainability campaigns face a measurable participation cost — but the research points to framing choices that blunt it.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If disclosure is mandatory or planned, consider shifting cause messaging from warmth ("we care") toward competence ("this works") framing, and A/B test disclosure wording against participation rates rather than assuming it's cost-free.
- **For general readers and consumers:** People appear to feel less of a social debt to an organization when a machine did the asking — a hint that much of our response to charitable appeals is about reciprocating human effort.

**Caveats:** Experiments with recruited samples; most outcomes are intentions, though Study 1 used a consequential sharing task; two countries only; effects for AI disclosure may shift as AI-made ads become normalized.

### 11. Tell savers "how," tell spenders "why": matching money messages to mindsets

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Behaviour* | **Published:** 2026-10-18
**Work ID:** W7222865689 | **DOI:** 10.1002/cb.70260

**The study:** Three experiments, using both scenario-based manipulations and real financial advertisements, tested whether savings messages work better when their abstraction level matches a person's Personal Saving Orientation — a chronic disposition to build saving into daily life. People high in saving orientation responded more to concrete "how to save" appeals, while those low in saving orientation responded more to abstract "why save" appeals. The fit worked by raising perceived message relevance, then goal importance, then willingness to save.

**Why it matters:** Banks, fintechs and financial-literacy campaigns typically run one generic savings message; this suggests the same budget split across two framings targeted by mindset could outperform it.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Test a two-track creative strategy for savings products — tactical, step-by-step messaging for engaged savers and purpose-led messaging for the disengaged — and consider whether existing behavioral or account data can proxy for saving orientation in targeting.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If generic advice to "save more" never lands for you, the problem may be the framing: people who don't already think about saving respond to reasons, not instructions.

**Caveats:** Experimental studies measuring willingness to save rather than actual deposits; sample details and country not specified in the abstract; hypothetical scenarios in part of the design.

### 12. Body-positive fashion ads work best on people who already believe in body positivity — and can backfire with the body-confident

**Journal:** *Journal of Fashion Marketing and Management* | **Published:** 2026-10-16
**Work ID:** W7222466110 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jfmm-11-2025-0627

**The study:** A survey of 211 young women in Central and Eastern Europe measured responses to fashion advertisements featuring models of three different body sizes, analyzed with confirmatory factor analysis and mixed-effects regression. Purchase intentions rose among respondents holding favorable attitudes toward body positivity, but were lower among those with a high body self-image. Respondents' own body size, age, and education did not predict their responses.

**Why it matters:** It challenges the assumption that inclusive casting is universally safe, and suggests the relevant segmentation variable is values and self-image, not body type or demographics.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Treat body-positive creative as a values-targeted message rather than a default: test it against audiences segmented by attitudes toward inclusivity, and watch for indifference or resistance among highly body-confident consumers.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Who an ad's model looks like matters less than what you believe about bodies — people didn't respond to models who matched their own size, they responded to messages that matched their values.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional self-administered online survey (no causal claims), stated purchase intentions rather than purchases, young women in a single region, N=211 with social desirability risk acknowledged by the authors.

### 13. Consumers put AI-made ads on trial — and heavy AI users aren't the most forgiving

**Journal:** *Journal of Global Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-13
**Work ID:** W7221622840 | **DOI:** 10.1080/08911762.2026.2746555

**The study:** Researchers ran 36 stimulus-based interviews about Heinz's "A.I. Ketchup" campaign, analyzing how consumers judge AI-generated advertising. Responses amounted to a negotiation over legitimacy, with people weighing creative agency, procedural transparency, brand continuity and ethical responsibility together rather than separately. Notably, familiarity with AI shaped the vocabulary people used, but frequent AI use did not translate into more critical or sophisticated understanding.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the reputational question around generative AI in advertising is not "does it look good" but "was it legitimate" — and that brands will be judged on disclosure and authorship, not just output quality.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands planning AI-led campaigns should prepare an authorship-and-process narrative in advance; the research indicates consumers evaluate transparency and brand continuity alongside the creative itself.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Using AI tools every day doesn't make you better at spotting or judging AI-made content — a useful corrective to the idea that digital natives are automatically savvy.

**Caveats:** Qualitative interviews with 36 participants; a single brand campaign; findings are interpretive and not generalizable or quantified.

### 14. Those stadium ad boards? Most viewers can't remember them — unless they love the home team

**Journal:** *Sport Marketing Quarterly* | **Published:** 2026-10-22
**Work ID:** W7223185198 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10616934261499682

**The study:** Researchers ran an in-lab and out-of-lab experiment with 701 participants who watched controlled footage from a real association football match containing 16 sponsor brands on in-field perimeter boards, then tested aided and unaided recall. General football fandom intensity, whether the scene was a highlight or a routine passage of play, and screen size showed no consistent relationship with recall. What did matter: the specific brand (recall varied substantially across the 16 sponsors) and team identification — fans of the home team recalled several prominent sponsors better than non-home-team fans.

**Why it matters:** Perimeter advertising is one of the biggest line items in football sponsorship, and the study suggests its value cannot be inferred from exposure or audience size alone — it depends on who the viewer supports and whether the brand is already familiar.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Rights holders sell perimeter boards on impressions; this experiment suggests buyers should press for recall evidence segmented by team affiliation rather than raw eyeball counts. A reasonable test: compare recall lift among a sponsor's own club fanbase versus neutral viewers before renewing a league-wide deal.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If you watch a lot of football and feel like you never notice the ad boards, you're normal — unless the team on screen is yours, in which case your brain is quietly logging the sponsors.

**Caveats:** Exploratory design; controlled match footage rather than natural living-room viewing; recall measured immediately rather than over time; single sport and a fixed set of 16 brands, so brand-level differences may not generalize.

### 15. 180,000 hotel reviews show Americans punish weak sustainability claims hardest

**Journal:** *Journal of Global Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-22
**Work ID:** W7223212068 | **DOI:** 10.1080/08911762.2026.2747190

**The study:** Researchers text-mined and sentiment-analyzed 180,000 TripAdvisor reviews of green hotels written by travelers in the United States, South Korea and India, looking at how review length, rating and sentiment predicted perceived helpfulness and how the moral and cultural framing of green claims differed by market. Longer, higher-rated, more positive reviews were judged more helpful everywhere. But the content diverged: Indian and South Korean reviewers framed sustainability in communal and moral-responsibility terms, while American reviewers framed it around personal expectations and were markedly more critical when a hotel's green efforts fell short.

**Why it matters:** It suggests a single global green-marketing message is likely to misfire, and that the downside risk of overclaiming on sustainability is concentrated in individualist markets like the US.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For hospitality and travel brands, this points to localizing environmental claims — communal and ethical framing in India and South Korea, concrete delivery-against-promise framing in the US — and to treating American review platforms as an early-warning system for greenwashing backlash.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Traveler reviews are doing the work advertising used to: this is a look at how people actually judge whether a hotel's eco-credentials are real, and how that judgment differs depending on where they're from.

**Caveats:** Observational review data, not an experiment — associations only; TripAdvisor reviewers are not representative of all travelers; three countries only; cultural interpretations are inferred from text, not measured directly in respondents.

### 16. A telecom's 174,000 ad records reveal threshold effects: more frequency helps, but only in steps

**Journal:** *Journal of Forecasting* | **Published:** 2026-10-22
**Work ID:** W7223172682 | **DOI:** 10.1002/for.70231

**The study:** Using 174,584 real microlevel advertising records from a Chinese telecommunications company in a single month (July 2024), researchers compared six machine learning models against a historical-average baseline to predict advertising cost-conversion efficiency, then used SHAP to open the black box. XGBoost fit best (test R-squared 0.82). The interpretability analysis found conversion rate dominated; exposure frequency related to efficiency in a step-like rather than smooth way; production cost showed a reversal effect around the median — above a point, spending more on creative production stopped helping; and average display cost contributed almost nothing.

**Why it matters:** It gives empirical, nonlinear shape to two decisions media planners make constantly — how often to show an ad and how much to spend making it — in a dataset of real campaign records rather than simulated media mixes.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** The production-cost reversal is the testable claim: brands could run a controlled split of high- versus moderate-budget creative in the same placements to see whether their own data shows diminishing or negative returns past a threshold, instead of assuming more expensive assets perform better.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Little direct relevance for consumers beyond a glimpse of how precisely advertisers now measure what each impression costs and returns.

**Caveats:** One firm, one country, one industry and a single month; the authors themselves note that much of the model's explanatory power comes from algebraic relationships among the components of the efficiency metric, limiting how much is genuinely new causal insight; observational data, no experimental manipulation; high variance in the reported R-squared.

### 17. A paid Facebook campaign reached 48,000 young Ghanaians — but WhatsApp forwards brought in the applicants

**Journal:** *Health Promotion International* | **Published:** 2026-09-01
**Work ID:** W7223181558 | **DOI:** 10.1093/heapro/daag154

**The study:** This process evaluation tracked a two-month, multi-platform campaign recruiting Ghanaian adolescents and young adults to submit creative ideas for promoting HIV self-testing and PrEP. Paid Facebook advertising generated 55,437 impressions and an estimated reach of 48,596 users; LinkedIn added 650 impressions and 171 link clicks. Yet among the 103 eligible applicants, peer-to-peer WhatsApp sharing was the dominant self-reported route to awareness — a channel whose reach and conversion could not be measured at all.

**Why it matters:** It is a concrete case of the measurement gap between trackable paid media and untrackable dark-social sharing, where the channel that drives action is the one that produces no dashboard.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Campaign teams relying on platform analytics may be systematically crediting the wrong channel. Worth building a self-reported 'how did you hear about us' field into every conversion flow and designing assets explicitly for private forwarding, not just feed impressions.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Most of what spreads online now moves through private group chats rather than public feeds — which is why a message can feel ubiquitous among your friends and invisible to the companies measuring it.

**Caveats:** Descriptive process evaluation with no control group or causal design; the authors explicitly state the study cannot establish which platform worked best because exposures overlapped and channel metrics are not equivalent; single campaign in a single country; small conversion count (103 applicants); self-reported awareness source.

### 18. Knowing you're being tracked makes some people block ads — and others lean in

**Journal:** *Journal of Interactive Advertising* | **Published:** 2026-10-16
**Work ID:** W7222483887 | **DOI:** 10.1080/15252019.2026.2740038

**The study:** An online survey of 327 consumers tested how people cope with third-party cookie-driven behavioral advertising, modeling awareness of the tactic, skepticism, perceived fairness, and two response styles: 'sentries' who avoid and block, and 'seekers' who accept and use targeted ads. Awareness of how tracking works predicted skepticism but not perceptions of fairness. Notably, viewing targeted advertising as competitive — as something working against the consumer's interests — was linked to both avoidance and acceptance, suggesting the same adversarial read of the tactic can push people in opposite behavioral directions.

**Why it matters:** The common assumption that educating consumers about tracking produces uniform resistance looks too simple; the same knowledge appears to split audiences into blockers and pragmatic users.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Transparency disclosures may not reduce engagement across the board, but they probably do segment the audience. Brands could test whether explaining targeting logic shifts their own audience toward opt-in behavior or toward ad-blocking, rather than assuming one outcome.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Two people can understand cookie tracking equally well and react in completely opposite ways — one installs a blocker, the other decides relevant ads are a fair trade. Neither is being naive.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional online survey (N=327) measuring self-reported attitudes and strategies, not observed ad-blocking or click behavior; cannot establish causation; path-model findings only; single sample, no field validation.

### 19. In cities where white Americans are no longer the majority, they're more receptive to multicultural ads

**Journal:** *Journal of cultural marketing strategy.* | **Published:** 2026-10-01
**Work ID:** W7223013238 | **DOI:** 10.69554/yvyd2263

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 1,183 non-Hispanic white, African American and Hispanic respondents across nine US cities, comparing places where white residents are a numerical minority with places where they remain the majority. They measured two exposure variables (everyday interethnic contact and multicultural exposure during childhood) against four attitude outcomes, including receptivity to multicultural advertising. Non-Hispanic white respondents living in multicultural-majority cities reported more interethnic contact, more childhood exposure and stronger receptivity to multicultural advertising than their counterparts in white-majority cities — evidence of what the author calls "reverse acculturation," where influence flows in both directions.

**Why it matters:** It directly counters the long-standing worry that multicultural casting and culturally specific creative will alienate white audiences — at least in markets where sustained intergroup contact is already the norm. It also suggests geography, not just ethnicity, may be the more useful segmentation variable for multicultural campaigns.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands weighing how far to push multicultural creative could test it by market type rather than treating the country as one audience: the data suggest campaigns that read as risky in a white-majority DMA may play well — or better — in multicultural-majority metros. Worth validating with brand-lift tests in matched city pairs before generalizing from attitude scores to sales.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Where you grow up and who you live among appears to shape what feels normal in an ad. The study points to a quietly changing America in which cultural adaptation isn't a one-way street from minority to mainstream — the mainstream is adapting too.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey, so it cannot establish that living in a multicultural city causes greater receptivity (self-selection is plausible); self-reported attitudes rather than observed response to real ads; single country; nine cities only; published in a practitioner-oriented cultural marketing journal.

### 20. How smokers talk themselves into buying black-market tobacco — and what campaigns miss

**Journal:** *Tobacco Control* | **Published:** 2026-10-19
**Work ID:** W7222982807 | **DOI:** 10.1136/tc-2026-060469

**The study:** Researchers conducted in-depth interviews with 20 New Zealand adults who had bought illicit tobacco in the previous six months, recruited through social media, community advertising and snowball sampling, and analyzed the transcripts thematically. Buyers described an informal market running through friends-of-friends networks, framed cheap tobacco as a rational response to financial hardship, and cast their purchases as resistance to tax policies they considered unfair. A fourth theme was "convenient unknowing": people actively avoided information — about who profits, or what's in the product — that would complicate their moral self-image.

**Why it matters:** It shows that illegal purchasing is sustained not just by price gaps but by social permission and self-justifying stories, which means enforcement and tax policy alone are unlikely to shift demand.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For social marketers and public health communicators, the lever here isn't price information but legitimacy: campaigns that puncture the "it's not real crime" narrative and interrupt "convenient unknowing" may outperform standard cessation messaging. The same mechanics apply to any brand fighting gray-market or counterfeit versions of its product — the fight is over moral framing, not just value for money.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A small, vivid portrait of how ordinary people license themselves to break rules: draw a moral boundary around "real" crime, put your purchase on the acceptable side, and then carefully avoid learning anything that would move the line.

**Caveats:** Small qualitative sample (N=20) recruited partly via snowball sampling, so not representative; single country; self-reported accounts of illegal behavior; findings are interpretive themes, not measured effects.

### 21. Annoying AI food ads still made people hungry — survey of 783 Vietnamese social media users

**Journal:** *British Food Journal* | **Published:** 2026-09-15
**Work ID:** W7213274355 | **DOI:** 10.1108/bfj-06-2026-1044

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 783 Vietnamese social media users who had seen food video advertising and ordered food online, testing how four perceived features of AI-generated food ads related to two kinds of "inspiration" and then to self-reported ordering. Informativeness, entertainment and credibility all predicted inspiration, as expected — but so did irritation, positively rather than negatively. Skepticism toward AI-generated content weakened the link between feeling inspired and actually ordering, while sheer familiarity with AI content had no moderating effect.

**Why it matters:** If the irritation result holds up, it challenges a core assumption of digital ad design — that annoyance is purely a cost — at least in impulse-driven categories like food delivery, where arousal of any kind may convert.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Worth a controlled test rather than a strategy shift: run AI-generated food creative that varies on irritation (volume, repetition, hard-sell framing) against actual order data, and separately test whether clear AI disclosure reduces the skepticism that appears to break the inspiration-to-order chain. Note that the study measured perceptions, not randomized exposure.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A reminder of how little resistance a hungry scroller has: ads that viewers themselves rate as irritating still nudged them toward ordering, and the main thing that blunted the effect was suspicion that the glossy food wasn't real.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey analyzed with PLS-SEM — correlational paths, not causal evidence; self-reported ordering behavior rather than platform data; single country (Vietnam) and single category; perceptions of AI ads measured in the abstract rather than exposure to specific randomized creative.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**

- **Synthetic representation keeps backfiring in exactly the places it is meant to help.** The previous digest's "When 'diverse' virtual influencers backfire: disability avatars cut donation intent" (W7202162669) now has a close companion in "AI-generated diversity backfires: synthetic models of color cost brands credibility" (W7223056505). Both find that machine-made inclusivity reads as hollow, both locate the damage in a dissonance or authenticity inference, and both identify a fit-type remedy — endorser–cause fit in the earlier study, verified community ownership in the new one.
- **Proxy metrics keep decoupling from outcomes.** "Dating apps beat social media for reach — but social media delivered the completions" (W7214195424) is joined by four new cases: "Eye-tracking study: a crowded page pulls more eyeballs to ads — and makes each one less memorable" (W7222550305), "When creators talk about themselves, the product stops selling" (W7222936501), "Those stadium ad boards? Most viewers can't remember them" (W7223185198), and "A paid Facebook campaign reached 48,000 young Ghanaians — but WhatsApp forwards brought in the applicants" (W7223181558).
- **Green and purpose messaging works on the already-converted.** "Watching someone else go green feels good enough" (W7203716020) argued admiration substitutes for action; "A short sustainability class changed how students responded to green advertising" (W7220917637) shows the flip side — the ad only moves people who already believe their choices matter — and "180,000 hotel reviews show Americans punish weak sustainability claims hardest" (W7223212068) adds that the downside of overclaiming is culturally concentrated.
- **Disclosure wording, not disclosure itself, is the lever.** Last run's "Telling people an ad was made by AI made them trust it more, not less" (W7207577485) and "Telling people your Instagram model isn't real cuts both ways" (W7212138832) established framing as the variable. This run adds two concrete framings that measurably blunt the penalty: calling the AI an "employee" ("Telling people an ad was made by AI makes it seem less creative — unless you give the AI a job title," W7222851502) and switching from warmth to competence appeals ("Saying 'this ad was made by AI' cuts participation in cause campaigns," W7223243344).

**Advances**

- **Disclosure research moves from outcome to mechanism.** Where "Labeling an ad 'AI-generated' mostly produces a shrug" (W7211870608) and W7207577485 reported net effects, the new batch names the pathways: perceived creativity (W7222851502), felt obligation to the sponsor (W7223243344), authenticity doubt feeding manipulation inference (W7223056505), and deliberate correction of a genuinely positive emotional response ("People enjoy AI-made ads — until they are told an AI made them," W7223070691).
- **Eye-tracking shifts from stimulus aesthetics to media economics.** "Eye-tracking finds moviegoers read poster colors differently than the industry assumes" (W7213276828) tested creative; W7222550305 and "Knowing an ad is an ad does not stop you scrolling past it the same way" (W7221986862) test the environment — clutter, position and feed context — with direct implications for pricing inventory rather than designing art.
- **From "how are creatives reacting to AI" to "what does AI-made creative actually get wrong."** "Ad creatives are splitting into three camps on generative AI" (W7204571530) mapped practitioner strategy; "AI-made video ads work better when they sell with logic, not feelings" (W7221595688) gives the first execution-level map — rational over emotional appeals, and photorealistic human characters as the specific failure mode.

**Counterpoints**

- **The AI-disclosure trust bonus does not survive this batch.** W7207577485 found disclosure raised trust and W7211870608 found near-indifference. Four new studies find clear penalties — on attitudes and purchase intention (W7222851502), on judgment despite real enjoyment (W7223070691), on cause participation (W7223243344) and on firm evaluations when synthetic models are people of color (W7223056505). The honest read is that disclosure effects are highly sensitive to category, appeal type and cast, and the earlier "shrug" results may reflect low-stakes banner contexts.
- **AI familiarity is no longer a safe moderator.** W7211870608 reported that AI-experienced consumers judged disclosed AI copy more favorably. "Consumers put AI-made ads on trial — and heavy AI users aren't the most forgiving" (W7221622840) finds frequent AI use changes vocabulary but not critical sophistication, and "Annoying AI food ads still made people hungry" (W7213274355) finds familiarity with AI content had no moderating effect at all.
- **Persuasion knowledge may not show up in behavior.** Much of the disclosure literature above assumes recognition triggers correction. W7221986862 finds no behavioral difference across persuasion-knowledge profiles in naturalistic feed browsing — a null result that sits awkwardly against both the ad-literacy policy rationale and W7223070691's demonstrated correction effect, and suggests involvement level is the hinge.
- **AI authorship is not uniformly a liability for cause messaging.** "Let the AI do the guilt-tripping and keep the humans for the pep talk" (W7216305737) found disclosed AI authorship *helped* guilt appeals; W7223243344 finds AI attribution depresses participation most for warmth appeals. The two are reconcilable — machine authorship appears to strip social obligation, which helps when the message is a reproach and hurts when it is a plea — but the field needs to say so explicitly.

**New themes**

- **The economics of ad-subsidized pricing** — "'We're passing advertising revenue back to you' beats 'special offer'" (W7222624740) is the first study across these two runs to treat the advertising business model itself as the persuasive object.
- **Advertising as personal-finance infrastructure** — "Tell savers 'how,' tell spenders 'why'" (W7222865689) has no prior-run analogue.
- **Geography over demography in multicultural targeting** — "In cities where white Americans are no longer the majority, they're more receptive to multicultural ads" (W7223013238) and "Body-positive fashion ads work best on people who already believe in body positivity" (W7222466110) both argue segmentation should run on values and place, not identity categories.
- **Privacy knowledge splitting audiences rather than uniting them** — "Knowing you're being tracked makes some people block ads — and others lean in" (W7222483887).
- The previous run's strongest new theme — **direct measurement of children's and youth ad exposure** (W7214801403, W7203907887, W7214311385, W7207692856, W7116214838) — has no successor in this batch. Worth watching whether it resumes or was a one-run cluster.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Five studies in this batch independently show the advertising industry's core measures running in the opposite direction from the outcomes they are sold as proxies for. Clutter raises total gaze while destroying recall for individual units (W7222550305); creator videos that mix in personal storytelling cut ad avoidance but cut sales harder, because product attention collapses (W7222936501); stadium perimeter boards are priced on impressions but recall is driven by team allegiance and prior brand familiarity, not exposure (W7223185198); a paid Facebook campaign with 48,596 reach produced applicants mostly through unmeasurable WhatsApp forwards (W7223181558); and 174,584 real ad records show frequency pays in steps and creative production spend reverses above the median (W7223172682). W7221986862 adds the uncomfortable coda that in-feed attention looks largely automatic regardless of how well viewers understand what they are seeing.

**Why pitch this now:** Attention metrics have been sold across the industry as the post-cookie successor to viewability, retail media networks and creator commerce are absorbing budget on engagement-based reporting, and this evidence lands as CFOs are pressing marketing teams to justify incrementality rather than dashboard numbers.

**Angle:** A reported piece on the measurement layer the ad industry built on the assumption that attention, watch time and reach stand in for memory and sales — and the growing body of controlled evidence that, in the specific formats now absorbing the most money, they move in opposite directions. Aimed at media buyers, measurement vendors and effectiveness-minded CMOs, with a consumer-facing thread for readers who scroll past hundreds of ads a day and remember none of them.

**Potential headlines:**
- More people watched. Fewer people bought.
- The ad industry finally learned to measure attention. Attention turned out not to be the point.
- What your media dashboard can't see is the part that works

**Potential outlets:**
- **Marketing Week** — runs sustained, evidence-led argument about effectiveness measurement and would take the clutter-pricing and perimeter-board findings as a direct challenge to how inventory is sold.
- **Digiday** — the attention-metrics-versus-recall split and the unmeasurable dark-social channel are squarely a buy-side accountability story their readers can act on.
- **Retail Dive** — retail media and creator commerce are priced on engagement; the finding that watch time and conversion move in opposite directions in short-video commerce is a live commercial problem for that sector.
- **MIT Sloan Management Review** — well suited to the longer analytical framing of proxy metrics becoming organizational targets, including the nonlinear frequency and production-cost reversal evidence.

## Story Ideas by Study

- **AI-generated diversity backfires: synthetic models of color cost brands credibility (Work ID W7223056505):** The penalty appears only when synthetic models are Black, Hispanic or Asian — and disappears with verifiable community ownership, making it a brief-level casting question, for a brand-strategy trade feature with a careful consumer-psychology treatment in a general-interest outlet.
- **Eye-tracking study: a crowded page pulls more eyeballs to ads — and makes each one less memorable (Work ID W7222550305):** Clutter is sold as inventory and bought as attention, but it dilutes recall for the specific unit you paid for — a pricing argument for media buyers, for an adtech or media-planning trade explainer.
- **People enjoy AI-made ads — until they are told an AI made them (Work ID W7223070691):** Facial coding shows viewers genuinely enjoy the work and then talk themselves out of it once labeled — reframing mandated disclosure as a creative-writing problem, for a creative-effectiveness column or a marketing-trade news brief.
- **Knowing an ad is an ad does not stop you scrolling past it the same way (Work ID W7221986862):** A null result that undercuts the ad-literacy rationale behind much self-regulation — in casual browsing, recognizing an ad changes nothing you can observe, for a policy-facing trade piece or a media-regulation explainer.
- **Telling people an ad was made by AI makes it seem less creative — unless you give the AI a job title (Work ID W7222851502):** The objection is aesthetic, not ethical, and framing the AI as an "employee" shrinks the hit — a testable disclosure-wording prescription, for a marketing-trade news brief or a CMO-audience column.
- **AI-made video ads work better when they sell with logic, not feelings (Work ID W7221595688):** The first practical map of which generative-video executions land — rational appeals win, photorealistic human characters in emotional spots are the uncanny failure mode, for a creative-production or agency-trade feature.
- **"We're passing advertising revenue back to you" beats "special offer" (Work ID W7222624740):** As retail media and ad-supported tiers spread, explaining the money flow beats dressing it as a promotion — except in utilitarian categories, for a retail-trade explainer or a personal-finance column on what ad-funded discounts really cost.
- **A short sustainability class changed how students responded to green advertising (Work ID W7220917637):** Green claims only move people who already believe their choices matter, which makes efficacy belief the segmentation variable, for a sustainability-marketing column (flag the student samples).
- **When creators talk about themselves, the product stops selling (Work ID W7222936501):** The entire creator-commerce playbook buys watch time by sacrificing product attention — and the sales data shows which wins, for a creator-economy or retail-media trade feature.
- **Saying "this ad was made by AI" cuts participation in cause campaigns (Work ID W7223243344):** The mechanism is reduced felt obligation, not lost sincerity — so nonprofits facing disclosure rules should move from "we care" to "this works," for a nonprofit/fundraising trade piece or a purpose-marketing column.
- **Tell savers "how," tell spenders "why" (Work ID W7222865689):** Generic "save more" messaging fails the people who most need it; disengaged savers respond to reasons, engaged ones to instructions, for a personal-finance column or a fintech-marketing trade piece.
- **Body-positive fashion ads work best on people who already believe in body positivity (Work ID W7222466110):** Respondents did not respond to models matching their own size — they responded to models matching their values, and the body-confident were less moved, for a fashion-retail trade explainer (flag N=211, single region).
- **Consumers put AI-made ads on trial — and heavy AI users aren't the most forgiving (Work ID W7221622840):** Daily AI use does not make people better judges of AI-made work, which punctures the "digital natives are savvy" assumption, for a consumer-psychology explainer in a general-interest outlet.
- **Those stadium ad boards? Most viewers can't remember them (Work ID W7223185198):** Perimeter inventory is sold on impressions but recall depends on who the viewer supports — an argument for affiliation-segmented recall evidence before renewal, for a sports-business or sponsorship trade feature.
- **180,000 hotel reviews show Americans punish weak sustainability claims hardest (Work ID W7223212068):** Greenwashing risk is concentrated in individualist markets, and US review platforms function as an early-warning system, for a travel/hospitality trade piece or a global brand-strategy column.
- **A telecom's 174,000 ad records reveal threshold effects (Work ID W7223172682):** The production-cost reversal — past a point, more expensive creative stops helping — is the one genuinely testable claim here, for a media-planning or marketing-analytics trade explainer (flag the authors' own caveat about algebraic relationships inflating the model).
- **A paid Facebook campaign reached 48,000 young Ghanaians — but WhatsApp forwards brought in the applicants (Work ID W7223181558):** A concrete case of the dark-social measurement gap, with a cheap fix — a "how did you hear about us" field in every conversion flow, for a media-measurement trade explainer or a global-health communications piece.
- **Knowing you're being tracked makes some people block ads — and others lean in (Work ID W7222483887):** Transparency doesn't produce uniform resistance; it sorts audiences into blockers and pragmatists, for a privacy-and-adtech trade column or a consumer-technology explainer.
- **In cities where white Americans are no longer the majority, they're more receptive to multicultural ads (Work ID W7223013238):** Direct evidence against the alienation fear that constrains multicultural creative — and an argument for market-type rather than national targeting, for a brand-strategy trade feature or a general-interest piece on a changing American mainstream (flag the cross-sectional design).
- **How smokers talk themselves into buying black-market tobacco (Work ID W7222982807):** "Convenient unknowing" is a transferable concept for any brand fighting counterfeits — the fight is over moral framing, not price, for a public-health communications piece or a brand-protection trade explainer.
- **Annoying AI food ads still made people hungry (Work ID W7213274355):** Irritation predicted inspiration rather than suppressing it, challenging a basic assumption of digital creative design in impulse categories, for a food-and-beverage marketing trade brief (flag the correlational PLS-SEM design).
