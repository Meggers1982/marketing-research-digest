# Fact-Check Report: Marketing Research Digest — Food, Health & Wellbeing — 2026-10-31

**Primary audience:** marketers and brand leaders | **Secondary audience:** general readers and consumers

### Study 1: A popular "brain training" fix for junk food cravings just failed its biggest online test
**Work ID:** W7223232846 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Participant nationality not stated in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "512 UK adults (47.6% female, mean age 41.7)"
- **Abstract says:** "we administered ICT online (vs a control group) to 512 individuals (47.6% female, mean age = 41.68)"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports 512 individuals and a mock site "based on a popular UK chain" but never states the sample was UK-based. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say "512 adults" and note the mock site was modelled on a UK chain, or confirm sample nationality from the full text."

**Issue B. Generalization beyond the single trial** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "treat this as a warning that lab-validated nudges often evaporate once delivered online at scale"
- **Abstract says:** "Whilst single-session laboratory evidence suggests ICT may be a promising intervention, online studies show equivocal results... findings of this study suggest ICT delivered online is unlikely to be an effective stand-alone intervention"
- **Problem:** The abstract supports a conclusion about inhibitory control training specifically (and calls prior online results "equivocal"), not a general rule that lab-validated nudges "often evaporate" online.
- **Suggested fix:** "Limit the claim to inhibitory control training, e.g. "a reminder that this particular lab-validated task did not transfer to an online ordering context.""

### Study 2: Consumers rate the green policies that work least as the ones most likely to work
**Work ID:** W7223275804 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Strength of the "what actually works" claim** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "we overrate being nudged and underrate being constrained, even though constraint tends to deliver more change"
- **Abstract says:** "Findings highlight a fundamental misalignment between consumer-perceived and empirically assessed policy effectiveness."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports a misalignment with empirical effectiveness but does not itself quantify or assert that constraining policies deliver more behaviour change; the digest states it as established fact.
- **Suggested fix:** "Attribute the comparison to the external evidence base the authors cite, e.g. "which the authors note runs counter to empirical assessments of policy effectiveness.""

### Study 4: Fussy eating starts earlier than you think: babies' feeding patterns in month one predict pickiness at a year
**Work ID:** W7224627412 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Headline misstates the predictor** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "babies' feeding patterns in month one predict pickiness at a year"
- **Abstract says:** "Australian parents of infants aged ≤4 months were recruited... Food avoidance was measured via parent report of satiety responsiveness and slowness in eating using the Baby Eating Behaviour Questionnaire at waves 1-4... Group-based trajectory analysis identified food avoidance groups from waves 1-4."
- **Problem:** The predictor is trajectory-group membership estimated across four waves of infants recruited at up to four months old — not feeding patterns measured in the first month of life. Readers could take away that a one-month snapshot forecasts fussiness.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to something like "infants' food-avoidance trajectory over the first year predicts pickiness at 12 months.""

### Study 5: Before the first puff: what makes teenagers open to vaping, across 63 studies
**Work ID:** W7224916875 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Study-design characterization in caveats** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "included studies are largely cross-sectional and measure susceptibility, intention or willingness rather than actual initiation; pooled across varied countries and measurement approaches"
- **Abstract says:** "Across these studies, we examined determinants and consumer decision constructs, including how susceptibility, intention, and willingness were operationalized."
- **Problem:** The susceptibility/intention/willingness point is supported, but "largely cross-sectional" and "varied countries" are not stated in the abstract. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Keep the susceptibility/intention point and either source the cross-sectional and country-mix claims to the full text or drop them."

### Study 6: Meditation seems to change what people buy — but mainly for beginners
**Work ID:** W7220360145 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Dropped nuance on the moderator** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the route from mindfulness to life satisfaction ran through mindful consumption among new meditators, but not among regular meditators, for whom mindfulness fed life satisfaction directly"
- **Abstract says:** "meditation regularity, which strengthens both the mindfulness to mindful consumption association and the direct mindfulness to life satisfaction association, whereas the indirect effect through mindful consumption is significant among new meditators and non-significant among regular meditators"
- **Problem:** The digest implies regular meditators show no mindfulness–mindful consumption link, whereas the abstract says regularity strengthens that association; it is the indirect effect on life satisfaction that is non-significant for them.
- **Suggested fix:** "Note that regularity strengthened both associations, but the indirect route to life satisfaction via mindful consumption was significant only for new meditators."

### Study 7: "Natural" beats "tastes like chicken": what really sells meat alternatives
**Work ID:** W7223189021 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "By far" overstates effect comparison** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "perceived naturalness had by far the strongest effect"
- **Abstract says:** "with perceived naturalness of the product being responsible for the strongest effect"
- **Problem:** The abstract says naturalness had the strongest effect but gives no magnitude comparison supporting "by far."
- **Suggested fix:** "Drop "by far" and say naturalness had the strongest effect of the three."

**Issue B. Inference about nutritional ability** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "and that consumers are largely unable to judge nutrition for themselves"
- **Abstract says:** "results regarding the perception of nutritional properties of meat alternatives revealed that the participants did not perceive differences in nutritional profiles between the stimuli used in the experiment. This highlights the necessity of further research on consumer nutritional assessment of food"
- **Problem:** Not perceiving differences between three specific stimuli is evidence that prompts further research, not a demonstration that consumers are generally unable to assess nutrition.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as "participants in this study did not distinguish the nutritional profiles of the three products.""

### Study 8: People who think they're green shoppers tune out green products
**Work ID:** W7223183113 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Attention dismissed as uninformative** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Eye-tracking data showing shoppers looked at your eco-label may be measuring nothing useful"
- **Abstract says:** "initial attention to sustainability cues is necessary but insufficient to elicit positive emotional responses or support sustainable food purchase decisions"
- **Problem:** The abstract treats attention as necessary, just not sufficient; saying it measures "nothing useful" overshoots that and could push readers to abandon a measure the study still treats as a required first stage.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say attention is a necessary but insufficient signal — pair eye-tracking with emotional engagement measures rather than replacing it."

**Issue B. Hedged mechanism stated as fact** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Correcting that illusion produced contempt in response to the feedback, which then increased engagement as a form of self-affirmation."
- **Abstract says:** "when this illusion is corrected, consumers may experience contempt in response to the corrective feedback and in turn, increase emotional engagement with sustainable products as a form of self-affirmation"
- **Problem:** The abstract uses "may"; the digest's body text reports the contempt pathway as an established result (the caveat does flag replication need).
- **Suggested fix:** "Mirror the hedge: "correcting the illusion may produce contempt, which the authors argue then increases engagement.""

### Study 9: Knowing where food comes from doesn't make people buy local — effort does
**Work ID:** W7223125428 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal headline for survey data** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Knowing where food comes from doesn't make people buy local — effort does"
- **Abstract says:** "The comparative analysis showed that self-reported effort was consistently associated with regular consumption in both countries... Knowledge of provenance or seasonality failed to predict behaviour"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports associations from structured surveys with self-reported consumption; "effort does" asserts causation the design cannot support.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "effort is the factor that tracks it" or similar associational phrasing."

### Study 10: A premium price tag won't make your organic claim more believable
**Work ID:** W7224137779 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Randomization detail reversed** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Note that premium presentation wasn't fully randomized against framing, so the interaction null is weaker than it looks."
- **Abstract says:** "Within each message-specific sample, premium presentation was randomised, yielding four analytical cells rather than a fully randomised 2 × 2 factorial experiment."
- **Problem:** Premium presentation was randomized; what was not randomized was message framing, which was assigned via two separate samples. As written it misdescribes which factor lacked randomization.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say premium presentation was randomized within each message sample but framing was not randomized across samples, so the design is not a full 2×2."

**Issue B. Causal verb for a non-randomized factor** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Message framing drove the clearest differences in what motives consumers attributed to producers"
- **Abstract says:** "Message framing was associated with the clearest differences in egoistic, values-driven and strategic-driven motive attributions and in scepticism"
- **Problem:** Framing was manipulated between two separately recruited samples, not randomized, and the abstract uses "associated with"; "drove" implies a causal test.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "was associated with the clearest differences.""

**Issue C. Headline says price, study manipulated presentation** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "A premium price tag won't make your organic claim more believable"
- **Abstract says:** "whether such inferences vary with message framing and premium presentation... premium presentation showed more selective differences"
- **Problem:** The manipulation was premium presentation, not price; and the result was "more selective differences," not a flat null on believability.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "premium presentation does little to shift scepticism about an organic claim.""

### Study 11: Shoppers don't read every label — a third use them all, and 42% mostly chase price
**Work ID:** W7224129116 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Profiling contrast with demographics not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the price-conscious group stood apart on environmental consciousness and sustainability knowledge rather than demographics"
- **Abstract says:** "A further profiling analysis clarifies that the price‐conscious segment is distinct in terms of environmental consciousness, sustainability knowledge and other psychographics."
- **Problem:** The abstract mentions distinctiveness on psychographics but never contrasts this with demographics or says demographics did not differentiate the segment.
- **Suggested fix:** "Drop "rather than demographics" or replace with "and other psychographics"."

**Issue B. "Effectively ignores" labels overstates the segment finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the largest segment effectively ignores them and uses price as a shortcut — meaning label stacking may raise clutter without changing most choices"
- **Abstract says:** "consumers appear to vary in whether they use labels as focal cues for simplified decision‐making (e.g., lexicographic heuristics) or as input in an information gathering process (compensatory decision‐making)"
- **Problem:** The abstract describes heterogeneous label use and a price-conscious segment, but does not report that this segment ignores labels altogether or that added labels produce clutter without changing choices.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say the largest segment weighted price most heavily and placed less weight on the labels, rather than that it "effectively ignores" them."

**Issue C. Tailored digital labels framed as a replacement** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "tailored digital or app-based label delivery may beat a universal on-pack solution"
- **Abstract says:** "Such heterogeneity in the uses of eco‐labels could be addressed by supplementing product labels with virtual, individually tailored label solutions."
- **Problem:** The authors suggest supplementing on-pack labels, not that digital solutions outperform or replace them.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "supplementing on-pack labels with tailored digital solutions"."

### Study 12: Holding something heavy made ice cream taste crispier
**Work ID:** W7224004846 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Preference ranking overstated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Overall preference was highest with felt and lowest with double-sided tape and the antislip sheet"
- **Abstract says:** "Preference ratings were higher in the Felt condition than in the Double-sided tape and Antislip sheet conditions, without accompanying differences in specific oral texture ratings."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports only that felt beat two specific materials, not that felt ranked highest overall or that those two ranked lowest across all eight materials.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say preference was higher with felt than with double-sided tape and the antislip sheet."

**Issue B. Causal headline for an exploratory associational finding** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Holding something heavy made ice cream taste crispier"
- **Abstract says:** "These differences suggest that extrinsic hand–feel cues are associated with static oral texture ratings of a composite food."
- **Problem:** The authors frame the results as associations from an exploratory study and refer to texture ratings rather than taste; the headline asserts causation and uses "taste".
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as "Holding something heavy went with higher crispness ratings for ice cream"."

### Study 14: Small talk still restores hybrid workers — unless it's over chat
**Work ID:** W7224750355 | **Verdict:** ❌ Significant issues

**Issue A. Dropped finding that text had a better relatedness-to-energy ratio** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "Text-based exchanges cost less energy but also delivered significantly less relatedness than audio-visual ones."
- **Abstract says:** "text-based (vs audio-visual) exchanges were significantly associated with lower relatedness and energy expenditure and a higher relatedness-to-energy ratio"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports that text was associated with a *higher* (more favorable) relatedness-to-energy ratio, which the digest omits; the headline ("unless it's over chat") and the "typing is cheap and low-connection" framing imply text fares worse overall than the data show.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add that text exchanges, while lower on relatedness, showed a more favorable relatedness-to-energy ratio, and soften the headline accordingly."

**Issue B. Omitted non-moderation by modality** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "It suggests informal chatter is a genuine well-being resource at work, but the channel matters"
- **Abstract says:** "Communication modality did not moderate these associations."
- **Problem:** The abstract explicitly reports that modality did not moderate the informality–well-being chain; the digest presents channel as conditioning the effect.
- **Suggested fix:** "Note that modality did not moderate the main associations and only showed direct links with relatedness and energy."

**Issue C. Causal verb for within-person associations** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "which in turn predicted greater satisfaction of relatedness needs and a better relatedness-to-energy ratio"
- **Abstract says:** "higher striving potential was associated with more relatedness need satisfaction and a more favorable relatedness-to-energy ratio, though not with energy expenditure"
- **Problem:** "Predicted" implies directionality in a correlational experience-sampling design, and the null link with energy expenditure is dropped.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "was associated with" and mention the null association with energy expenditure."

### Study 15: At stadium concerts, fans buy the T-shirt before the beer
**Work ID:** W7221437963 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. "Overpriced" substituted for "price sensitive"** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Food and drink, by contrast, were seen as fleeting, overpriced, and awkward to consume"
- **Abstract says:** "food and beverages are perceived as short-lived, more price sensitive and less compatible with immersive, high-density event conditions"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports greater price sensitivity in the category, not that attendees judged food and drink to be overpriced.
- **Suggested fix:** "Use "more price sensitive" or "judged more on price"."

**Issue B. Spending concentration not measured** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "this suggests the merchandise line is where discretionary spending and emotional value concentrate during high-density, time-bound events"
- **Abstract says:** "Findings reveal a strong pattern in which merchandise is prioritised relative to food and beverages."
- **Problem:** The study observed and discussed prioritization in a qualitative design; no spending data were collected, so claims about where discretionary spend concentrates go beyond the evidence (the Caveats note this, but the body states it as a finding).
- **Suggested fix:** "Say attendees prioritized merchandise in how they talked about and approached on-site retail, without implying measured spend."

**Issue C. Unsupported claim about venue operations** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Stadiums and promoters typically build operations around food and beverage capacity"
- **Abstract says:** "merchandise, food and beverage sales represent key revenue streams"
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only; the abstract treats both as key revenue streams and does not characterize current operational priorities.
- **Suggested fix:** "Attribute this industry characterization or remove it."

### Study 17: Call it "veggie" and the health-conscious will buy your indulgent snack — guilt, not taste, does the selling
**Work ID:** W7221783757 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Half of the additional-claims finding dropped** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "extra claims such as "reduced sodium" or "natural and additive-free" could actually backfire with them over fears the product would taste flat"
- **Abstract says:** "when additional health‐related claims such as "reduced sodium" or "natural and additive‐free" are appended to the same product label, they also enhance purchase intentions among highly health‐conscious consumers by lowering perceived guilt. However, for low health‐conscious consumers, such claims may backfire"
- **Problem:** Only the backfire half is reported; the positive effect of extra claims among highly health-conscious consumers is omitted, which understates the segmentation result.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add that the same extra claims lifted intentions among highly health-conscious consumers by lowering guilt."

### Study 18: Students said they knew better — then left 51 grams of food on the plate anyway
**Work ID:** W7220842762 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Awareness campaigns not tested** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "It is direct evidence that awareness campaigns can move perceptions without moving the bin"
- **Abstract says:** "consumer knowledge, portion size perception, and available eating time influenced only perceived waste behavior, revealing a disconnect between awareness and objectively measured PW"
- **Problem:** The study measured self-reported knowledge in a cross-sectional survey; it did not evaluate any awareness campaign or intervention, so "direct evidence" about campaigns overstates the design.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reframe as evidence that self-reported knowledge tracked perceived but not measured waste, implying campaigns built on knowledge alone may not be enough."

### Study 20: Shoppers judge near-expiry canned goods more harshly than near-expiry fresh food
**Work ID:** W7220982864 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Moderation path slightly simplified** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the health-risk penalty was stronger for *non-perishable* foods than for perishable ones"
- **Abstract says:** "perishability moderates the relationship between perceived nearness to the expiry date and perceived health risk... the positive effect of perceived nearness to the expiry date on perceived health risk is stronger for non-perishable than for perishable food products"
- **Problem:** The moderation was tested on the nearness-to-health-risk path, not on purchase intentions directly; readers may infer a stronger purchase-intention penalty was demonstrated for non-perishables.
- **Suggested fix:** "Specify that perishability moderated the effect on perceived health risk, which in turn mediated purchase intentions."

**Issue B. Country claim not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "single-country context"
- **Abstract says:** "Two experimental studies were conducted to test the proposed effects. The first study (N = 111)... The second study (N = 210)..."
- **Problem:** Unverifiable from abstract only; the abstract does not state where participants were recruited.
- **Suggested fix:** "Note that sample composition and country are not reported in the abstract."
