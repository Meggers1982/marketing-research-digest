### 1. The promotion that offers no discount — and still moves product

**Journal:** *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* | **Published:** 2026-10-02
**Work ID:** W7215933723 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11747-026-01202-z

**The study:** Researchers define "symbolic promotions" — campaigns that reward shared group identity rather than offering money off — and test them two ways. A nine-year longitudinal analysis of daily sales for 22,084 books tracked the Quebec campaign "On August 12, I buy a Quebec book!" and found a substantial sales lift, with bigger gains for lesser-known authors and publishers than for established names. A follow-up experiment showed the mechanism is curiosity plus "community rootedness," which raised participation, social sharing and future brand consideration.

**Why it matters:** It shows a promotional lever that builds sales without eroding price integrity or training shoppers to wait for discounts — and one that disproportionately helps smaller brands get discovered.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands exhausted by discount dependency have a testable alternative: tie a purchase occasion to a group identity customers already hold (local, regional, professional, fandom) rather than to a price cut. The field evidence here comes from one category in one market, so the question for marketers is which identities are strong enough in their own category to carry a buying occasion, and whether the lift survives repeated annual use.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It's a window into why you might buy a book on a particular day for no economic reason at all: a well-framed appeal to "people like us" can be as motivating as 20% off, and it tends to steer attention toward unfamiliar names rather than the usual bestsellers.

**Caveats:** The field data covers a single category (books) in a single market (Quebec) with a strong existing cultural-identity movement; the mechanism evidence comes from an experiment measuring stated intentions rather than purchases.

### 2. A profession that is majority women — and still hands authority to men's norms

**Journal:** *Public Relations Review* | **Published:** 2026-09-21
**Work ID:** W7213930759 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.pubrev.2026.102777

**The study:** Using a Foucauldian discursive framework, the researcher interviewed senior women leaders in India's public relations and corporate communication sector to examine how gendered power works inside a field where women are the numerical majority. Findings describe a "symbolic burden": women perform what the author calls gendered recalibration, balancing leadership demands against culturally coded expectations of respectable femininity in order to be read as legitimate. Their agency is ambivalent — strategies that win authority in the moment can also reproduce the hierarchy that made them necessary.

**Why it matters:** Headcount parity is often treated as proof that a communications function has solved its gender problem; this study argues that discursive norms about what leadership looks like can keep authority out of women's hands even in women-majority organizations.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Communications and marketing leaders benchmarking diversity on representation alone should look instead at who gets to speak for the organization, chair the crisis room and own client relationships. The study's prescription is organization-level: interrogate the norms by which leadership is recognized, rather than adding another women's development program.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For anyone working in a female-dominated profession, the finding names a familiar dynamic — being numerically dominant and still having to manage how you come across in order to be taken seriously.

**Caveats:** Qualitative interview study in a single country (India) and a single sector; no sample size reported in the abstract; interpretive rather than causal, and findings describe lived experience rather than measured outcomes such as promotion rates.

### 3. Residents are branding your city whether you invite them to or not

**Journal:** *European Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-11
**Work ID:** W7202171291 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ejm-07-2025-0540

**The study:** Two qualitative studies across five European cities examined how residents shape place brand meaning. Study 1 combined interviews (n=28), netnography and participant observation in Luton; Study 2 used photo-elicited interviews (n=20) across London, Leicester, Oxford and Budapest. The authors map a "place brand co-creation spectrum" along structure, intentionality and temporality, identifying four co-creation archetypes and seven resident roles — including entirely unplanned and passive contributions that still alter what a place stands for.

**Why it matters:** Place marketing budgets typically fund structured engagement — workshops, ambassador schemes, hashtag campaigns — while the study argues that most brand meaning is generated by residents going about ordinary life.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For destination and city marketers, the usable idea is a diagnostic: audit which resident roles your place actually has and where unplanned meaning-making is running counter to the official brand, instead of assuming engagement equals participation in your program. This is an interpretive model from five cities, so treat it as a framework to test locally rather than a validated measurement tool.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Your everyday complaints, photos and local pride are the raw material of your town's reputation — far more so than the logo the council paid for.

**Caveats:** Qualitative and interpretive; small combined sample (48 interviewees); five cities, four of them in the UK; examines only resident–municipality dynamics, excluding visitors and businesses; no outcome measures.

### 4. AI training didn't move the needle — but boss-level backing did

**Journal:** *Journal of Communication Management* | **Published:** 2026-09-02
**Work ID:** W7205004055 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jcom-03-2026-0141

**The study:** A cross-sectional survey of 234 public relations and communication professionals in the United Arab Emirates tested which factors predicted self-reported communication effectiveness when AI is in the mix: individual AI competency, organizational AI support, perceived value of AI, and perceived AI risk. In hierarchical regression, only organizational AI support — governance frameworks, leadership commitment, institutional readiness — significantly predicted effectiveness. Personal skill, enthusiasm about AI and worry about AI all washed out, and there were no significant differences between those who had and hadn't received AI training.

**Why it matters:** Communication teams are pouring budget into AI tools and individual upskilling, but this evidence points to the organization's governance and support structures, not personal proficiency, as the thing that correlates with getting results out of AI.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If AI pilots in your comms function are underdelivering, the constraint may be institutional rather than technical. Worth testing internally: compare teams with clear AI policies, leadership sponsorship and ethical oversight against teams that merely have licenses and training, and see whether output quality actually diverges — this is correlational evidence from one market, not proof that governance causes performance.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The familiar story that workers just need to 'learn AI' to stay relevant looks shakier here: in this sample, whether an employee had been trained on AI made no detectable difference to how effective they said their work was. What mattered was whether their employer had built a system around it.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey, so causation cannot be established; self-reported perceptions of effectiveness rather than objective performance measures; single country (UAE) and single profession; the authors themselves flag response bias and limited generalizability.

### 5. Compliance programs barely work on their own — leaders have to be seen living them

**Journal:** *International Journal of Bank Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-16
**Work ID:** W7213244866 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ijbm-11-2025-0928

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 752 employees across four countries and five industries, including financial services, measuring three dimensions of compliance culture — formal organizational governance, individual internalization of compliance values, and visible leadership commitment — against employees' stated willingness to blow the whistle on wrongdoing. All three predicted whistleblowing intentions, explaining about 36% of the variance. The headline result: leadership compliance mediated 71% of the effect of formal organizational compliance, meaning rulebooks and reporting infrastructure mostly influence reporting behavior indirectly, through whether leaders visibly embody them. Clan and adhocracy cultures amplified the relationships; hierarchical cultures showed only contingent effects.

**Why it matters:** Reputational blowups usually start as internal problems nobody reported, and this suggests that investment in compliance architecture translates into actual reporting mainly when leaders are seen to model it.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** A prompt to audit the gap between policy and visible leader behavior: does your organization measure whether employees can name a time a senior leader acted on compliance values, not just whether a hotline exists? The study also suggests one-size-fits-all compliance rollouts may misfire, since effects varied by industry, country and culture type.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It explains something most employees intuitively know — people decide whether to report misconduct based on what they've watched their bosses do, not on what the staff handbook says.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey, so no causal claim; the outcome is whistleblowing intention, not observed reporting behavior; individual-level OLS used to analyze what are partly organizational phenomena; the four countries and five industries are not specified in enough detail to judge representativeness.

### 6. The PR manager's job is quietly becoming 'ethical advisor to the machine'

**Journal:** *Journal of Public Relations Research* | **Published:** 2026-09-15
**Work ID:** W7213310447 | **DOI:** 10.1080/1062726x.2026.2733651

**The study:** Through in-depth interviews with U.S. public relations practitioners, this study examined how generative AI is reshaping professional roles and identity. Practitioners described a shift away from the long-standing manager-versus-technician divide toward a human-machine hybrid role defined by AI fluency, ethical stewardship and the strategic interpretation of AI-generated output. The authors argue the managerial role is expanding to include an explicit ethical advisor function, and propose a framework for PR roles in the AI era.

**Why it matters:** If AI absorbs the technician work — drafting, monitoring, first-pass content — the defensible value of a communications professional shifts to judgment, interpretation and accountability, which has direct implications for how teams are staffed, titled and evaluated.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Consider whether your job descriptions and performance reviews still reward output volume rather than the judgment layer: who signs off on AI-generated claims, who catches the reputational landmine, who advises the C-suite on whether an AI use is defensible. These are practitioner accounts rather than measured outcomes, so treat the framework as a hypothesis to test against your own team's workload.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A concrete look at what 'AI changes jobs' actually means inside one white-collar profession: not mass elimination so far, but a reshuffling toward the work of checking, interpreting and taking responsibility for what the machine produced.

**Caveats:** Qualitative interview study in a single country (U.S.); sample size not reported in the abstract; captures practitioners' self-perceptions of their roles rather than observed changes in work or outcomes; no measurement of effectiveness or client impact.

### 7. A brand's age may be a reputational airbag when scandal hits

**Journal:** *International Journal of Strategic Communication* | **Published:** 2026-09-24
**Work ID:** W7214178032 | **DOI:** 10.1080/1553118x.2026.2733124

**The study:** In a preregistered experiment with a representative Dutch sample (N=600), researchers presented a fictional brand with varying founding dates, measured participants' perceptions of the brand's heritage (longevity, continuity, tradition, adaptability), and then exposed half of them to a news article accusing the brand of unethical behavior. Stronger heritage perceptions were linked to weaker negative emotions and negative thoughts about the brand after the accusation, with perceived authenticity appearing to carry much of the effect. The authors call this shortcut the "heritage heuristic": people seem to judge a long-established organization more charitably even when its history has nothing to do with the alleged wrongdoing.

**Why it matters:** It suggests that heritage cues built into everyday branding — founding year, continuity of values, long-running traditions — may function as pre-paid crisis insurance rather than mere nostalgia decoration.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If heritage buffers negative publicity, the investment has to be made long before the crisis. Brands with real longevity could test whether making founding dates, continuity and enduring values more prominent in routine communications changes how audiences absorb bad news; the caution is that this was a fictional brand in a controlled setting, so the effect's size against a real, well-covered scandal is unknown — and young brands have no obvious substitute.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It's a useful bit of self-knowledge for consumers: we appear to forgive old companies more readily than new ones, even when age has no bearing on whether the accusation is true. "Established 1887" is a persuasion cue, not evidence of good conduct.

**Caveats:** Online experiment with a fictional brand and hypothetical news article rather than a real scandal; single country (Netherlands); measures emotions and cognitions, not actual purchasing; heritage perceptions were measured rather than fully manipulated.

### 8. Half-leaving a sanctioned market may be worse for your reputation than staying put is good for it

**Journal:** *International Marketing Review* | **Published:** 2026-08-11
**Work ID:** W4414312759 | **DOI:** 10.1108/imr-10-2025-0502

**The study:** Across four studies combining longitudinal secondary data with experiments, researchers tested how consumers in a firm's home country judge companies that respond to economic sanctions by exiting a sanctioned market fully, exiting partially, or staying. Full exit drew the most positive legitimacy judgments and support; partial exit and no exit fared worse. Individual differences — susceptibility to normative influence, cognitive empathy, individualism, and ethical views about sanctions themselves — shaped how harshly consumers judged.

**Why it matters:** Companies facing geopolitical pressure often choose a middle path — scaling back, pausing investment, keeping "essential" operations — and this evidence suggests that hedge buys little moral credit with the home audience.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For multinationals, the finding reframes exit decisions as reputation decisions with a nonlinear payoff: half-measures may deliver the operational cost of leaving without the legitimacy benefit. Worth testing with your own customer base, since the research also shows judgments vary sharply by consumer values rather than applying uniformly.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Consumers have become de facto enforcers of foreign policy, and the research shows they read corporate ambiguity as evasion. It also shows that whether you punish a company depends heavily on whether you believe sanctions are ethical in the first place.

**Caveats:** Experimental components likely rely on scenarios and stated support rather than observed purchases; home-country consumer framing may not generalize across markets; details of samples and sample sizes are limited in the abstract.

### 9. Why crisis playbooks break down in practice: lawyers, nervous clients and the clock

**Journal:** *Journal of Communication Management* | **Published:** 2026-08-19
**Work ID:** W7203759226 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jcom-12-2025-0336

**The study:** Through semi-structured interviews with senior public relations consultants in Johannesburg, South Africa, researchers examined how practitioners actually use the "base responses" prescribed by Situational Crisis Communication Theory — instructing information (what to do to stay safe) and adjusting information (expressions of concern and corrective action). Consultants endorsed these responses as ethically essential but calibrated tone by crisis type: guidance and reassurance for victim crises, regret for accidents, explicit apology and correction for preventable ones. Crucially, deviations from textbook sequencing were usually driven by legal counsel, client risk aversion and media pressure rather than practitioner ignorance.

**Why it matters:** It locates the real bottleneck in crisis response not in knowing the right message but in the organizational politics — especially legal review — that delay or dilute it.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** The actionable question for communications leaders is governance, not theory: who has sign-off authority in the first hour, and how is the legal-versus-communications tradeoff resolved in advance? This also offers a rare practitioner view from the Global South, where many crisis frameworks developed in the US and Europe are applied untested.
- **For general readers and consumers:** When a company's first statement after a disaster sounds stilted and evasive, it is often because lawyers edited it — not because nobody thought to say sorry.

**Caveats:** Small qualitative interview study in a single city and country; captures practitioner beliefs and self-reports rather than measured crisis outcomes; no sample size given in the abstract.

### 10. India's bidi industry borrowed Big Tobacco's playbook — including the charity photo-ops

**Journal:** *Tobacco Control* | **Published:** 2026-10-05
**Work ID:** W7220403023 | **DOI:** 10.1136/tc-2025-059812

**The study:** Researchers interviewed 200 bidi rollers (workers who hand-roll India's dominant smoked tobacco product) plus 17 key stakeholders — middlemen known as munshis, community and youth club representatives, and labor union leaders — across Murshidabad in West Bengal and Jabalpur/Narsinghpur in Madhya Pradesh, using trusted community insiders to gain access. Thematic analysis found that bidi companies run image-building corporate social responsibility programs that mirror multinational cigarette company tactics, while median wages ran at just 38–53% of government-mandated rates. The study also documented provident fund fraud, false worker registration, unjustified product rejections that feed untaxed illicit trade, and entrenched child labor in West Bengal.

**Why it matters:** It is a documented case of CSR functioning as reputational cover rather than social investment, in an industry that accounts for 85% of smoked tobacco in India — a reminder that good-works communications are increasingly being audited against supply-chain reality.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** A cautionary case on the reputational half-life of CSR: when community-giving programs sit atop verifiable wage and labor violations, the giving itself becomes the evidence used against the company. Brands with exposure to informal, contractor-mediated labor should ask whether their social-impact storytelling could survive a field study of the people at the bottom of the chain.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The charitable activity a company advertises and the conditions of the people who make its product can be two completely separate operations — here, workers earning roughly half the legal minimum wage were employed by firms publicizing community good deeds.

**Caveats:** Qualitative interview study in two districts of a single country; findings reflect worker and stakeholder accounts rather than audited company records; no comparison group of non-bidi employers.

### 11. Not all opposition is persuadable: eight 'ethical personas' behind community resistance to offshore wind

**Journal:** *Journal of Social Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-29
**Work ID:** W7214777517 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jsocm-06-2026-0237

**The study:** A three-stage study analyzed 3,215 public submissions and 4,134 Facebook and Reddit comments about offshore wind projects, coding attitudes and cognitive biases, then built and validated eight community personas through two participatory roundtables. The researchers found that community responses are organized by moral orientation — utilitarian cost-benefit reasoning among supporters, biocultural and justice-based ethics among the persuadable, and populist institutional distrust among the resistant. Critically, persuadable opponents showed 'calibrated negativity' with implicit paths to resolution, while resistant opponents showed 'totalizing negativity' in which counter-evidence is reinterpreted as further proof of deception. The authors also identify 'proxy framing' — people deploying whatever arguments are available as stand-ins for identity-based resistance.

**Why it matters:** It gives communicators a diagnostic for distinguishing audiences whose objections can be answered from audiences for whom more facts actively deepen distrust — and argues resources aimed at the latter are better spent upstream in the media ecosystem than in direct engagement.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** The 'information deficit' assumption is the default in corporate and public-affairs communication, and this work suggests it misallocates budget. Test segmenting stakeholder audiences by moral orientation rather than demographics or stated attitude, and build a triage rule for when more evidence helps versus backfires — treating the reachability spectrum as a hypothesis to validate in your own category, not a settled model.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The argument someone makes against a project may not be the real reason they oppose it — 'proxy framing' means stated objections about birds, views or property values can be stand-ins for a deeper identity-level resistance, which explains why debunking rarely changes minds.

**Caveats:** Based on public submissions and social media comments, which skew toward the vocal and engaged rather than the general population; persona framework is interpretive coding validated by expert roundtables rather than tested against behavioral outcomes; single infrastructure category and jurisdiction.

### 12. Wine and weed: German drinkers who use cannabis often use both at once — and motive predicts which

**Journal:** *British Food Journal* | **Published:** 2026-09-29
**Work ID:** W7214712049 | **DOI:** 10.1108/bfj-10-2025-1455

**The study:** An online survey of 523 German wine drinkers under 60, including 215 cannabis consumers, used logistic regression to model whether people consume wine and cannabis together or keep them separate. Frequent cannabis users were more likely to combine the two, suggesting complementarity rather than substitution; wine consumption frequency on its own predicted nothing. People who drank wine for socializing and enhancement but used cannabis for socializing and coping were more likely to keep the two occasions apart.

**Why it matters:** As cannabis legalizes across Europe and youth alcohol consumption declines, the wine industry has treated cannabis mainly as a competitive threat; this suggests the relationship is more complementary than substitutive, and that occasion motive — not category — is the useful segmentation variable.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If motive rather than product drives occasion, the alcohol industry's segmentation may be built on the wrong axis. The authors suggest THC-infused products for social and enhancement occasions and CBD products for solitary coping occasions — a hypothesis worth testing in markets where such products are already legal, before assuming cannabis is purely cannibalistic.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A useful lens on your own habits: the reason you reach for a drink — to be social, to feel good, to cope — predicts more about your consumption patterns than how much you drink.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional online survey in a single country (Germany), so no causal inference; self-reported consumption frequency and motives rather than observed behavior; the product recommendations (THC and CBD wines) are the authors' extrapolation, not something tested; subsample of cannabis users is modest (N=215).

### 13. Why free pads don't get used: stigma, not access, is regulating the menstrual products market

**Journal:** *Social Marketing Quarterly* | **Published:** 2026-09-26
**Work ID:** W7214428578 | **DOI:** 10.1177/15245004261489970

**The study:** Twenty-five in-depth interviews with Indian women aged 18–45 — students, working professionals and homemakers — plus a validation focus group with gynecologists, an NGO representative, a menstrual product specialist and policymakers, examined how cultural taboos shape product choice, willingness to try alternatives, and disposal. Five themes emerged: silence as negative socialization, affordability as a moral economy, stigma as informal market regulation, experimentation as identity risk, and disposal as 'the afterlife of taboo.' Together they describe a cycle in which stigma narrows choice, deters trial of alternatives such as cups and reusables, and undermines sustainable disposal.

**Why it matters:** It explains a persistent failure pattern in both social marketing and commercial category entry: distribution and price interventions underperform when the social cost of being seen using a product is the real barrier.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** A reframing of trial barriers that generalizes beyond this category: when switching carries social or identity risk, awareness campaigns and price promotions won't move adoption. Consider whether your category's low trial rate is an information problem or a visibility problem, and whether normalizing public conversation is the actual job of the campaign.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The finding that product disposal is shaped by taboo as much as purchase is a reminder of how much of everyday consumption is governed by what we think others will see — and why sustainable alternatives struggle in categories people prefer not to discuss.

**Caveats:** Small interpretive qualitative study (25 interviews) in a single country with strong privacy norms; findings are thematic interpretation, not measured effects on behavior; no quantification of how widely the patterns hold.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Five studies in this batch, from very different corners of the field, converge on the same uncomfortable finding: the formal apparatus an organization builds to prove it behaves well is a poor predictor of whether it actually does. Compliance culture only reaches employees through visible leader behavior — leadership compliance mediated 71% of the effect of formal governance on whistleblowing intentions ("Compliance programs barely work on their own," W7213244866); AI training made no detectable difference to communication effectiveness while organizational AI support did ("AI training didn't move the needle," W7205004055); crisis playbooks are diluted in practice by legal counsel and client nerves rather than by ignorance ("Why crisis playbooks break down in practice," W7203759226); headcount parity in a women-majority profession coexists with norms that still withhold authority ("A profession that is majority women," W7213930759); and CSR programs sit directly on top of wages at 38–53% of the legal minimum ("India's bidi industry borrowed Big Tobacco's playbook," W7220403023).

**Why pitch this now:** Corporate affairs budgets are being poured simultaneously into AI governance, supply-chain due-diligence reporting, and refreshed codes of conduct — at exactly the moment when external auditing of those claims, by regulators, researchers, and journalists, is getting cheaper and more granular than the claims themselves.

**Angle:** A feature on the widening gap between the documented organization and the observed one — written for communications, compliance, and corporate affairs leaders who are being asked to show evidence of culture rather than evidence of paperwork, with a secondary read for anyone who has sat through a training module that changed nothing.

**Potential headlines:**
- The Paperwork of Good Conduct Rarely Changes Behavior
- Your AI Training Made No Difference. Your AI Policy Did.
- When the Scandal Breaks, No One Will Care That You Had a Program

**Potential outlets:**
- **PRWeek** — the crisis-governance finding (who holds first-hour sign-off, and how legal review dilutes the response) is a live operational argument for its in-house comms readership.
- **MIT Sloan Management Review** — built for exactly this kind of multi-study management claim about mediation, culture types, and why institutional support beats individual upskilling.
- **Raconteur** — its governance, risk, and responsible-business reports pair naturally with the compliance-culture and supply-chain CSR evidence.
- **Fortune** — covers corporate accountability and ESG credibility for a senior business audience that will recognize the "program exists, behavior didn't change" problem.
