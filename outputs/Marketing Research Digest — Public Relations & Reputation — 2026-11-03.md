### 1. When populism rises, green packaging quietly disappears

**Journal:** *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* | **Published:** 2026-10-08
**Work ID:** W7214508893 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11747-026-01211-y

**The study:** Researchers treated the 2017 US announcement that it would exit the Paris Agreement as a quasi-natural experiment and ran a difference-in-differences analysis on a global sample of new product launches by consumer-packaged goods manufacturers. Firms more exposed to anti-climate-action populism measurably cut back on environmentally friendly packaging in subsequent launches. The pullback was weaker where subnational institutions pushed the other way — greater economic freedom and tighter local cultures blunted the retreat.

**Why it matters:** It shows sustainability commitments in packaging are far more politically contingent than corporate messaging implies, flexing with the perceived direction of government signals rather than with consumer demand alone.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If your sustainable packaging roadmap is justified internally by regulatory expectation rather than consumer or retailer pull, it is vulnerable the moment the political wind shifts — a good prompt to audit which green commitments would survive a hostile policy environment, and how a visible retreat would read to stakeholders who were told the commitment was values-driven.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The eco-friendly packaging on a shelf is partly a political artifact: the same brand may make greener choices in one market or one election cycle than another, which is worth remembering when a company presents its packaging as a matter of principle.

**Caveats:** Observational quasi-experiment rather than a randomized design; centers on one policy shock in one country and on packaging choices in new launches, which is a narrow slice of sustainability behavior; no consumer response measured.

### 2. Big Pharma's pandemic tweets: dry facts beat upbeat framing

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-27
**Work ID:** W7223996694 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jcm-03-2026-9074

**The study:** Researchers analyzed 6,466 tweets posted by six major pharmaceutical companies during 2020–2021, combining BERT-based topic modeling, lexicon-based detection of misinformation-countering strategies, and emotion analysis, then linked content features to likes and retweets. Science-based content drew significantly more engagement than other content types; "boosting" accurate information was far more common than directly refuting falsehoods and was also more widely amplified; and neutral, factual framing outperformed positively framed messaging.

**Why it matters:** In a contested information environment, perceived epistemic credibility — not emotional warmth or optimism — appears to drive audience amplification of corporate messages.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Communications teams facing a misinformation flashpoint can test the counterintuitive option: lead with plain, neutral evidence and amplify what is true rather than publicly rebutting what is false. This is correlational engagement data from one industry and one platform, so it is a hypothesis to test in your own channels, not a proven rulebook.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It suggests audiences in a crisis reward sober information over cheerleading — and that the loudest corrective replies are not necessarily the ones that travel.

**Caveats:** Single platform (Twitter/X), single industry, six firms, and one extraordinary crisis period; engagement metrics are a weak proxy for belief change or behavior; associations are correlational and content categories rest on lexicon-based coding.

### 3. Why companies cannot count the emissions hiding in their supply base

**Journal:** *Journal of Business Research* | **Published:** 2026-10-29
**Work ID:** W7224156801 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jbusres.2026.116515

**The study:** Using data on 1,142 firms, researchers built a new empirical method for estimating emissions embedded in a company's supply base — the Scope 3 territory beyond its own operations and purchased energy. Greater geographical and industrial diversity in the supplier network significantly increased supply base emissions, with buyer network influence and supplier interconnectedness moderating those effects.

**Why it matters:** Most of a consumer brand's carbon footprint sits with its suppliers, and this quantifies a structural driver — supply chain sprawl — that firms rarely acknowledge when making public climate claims.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Before signing off on another net-zero communication, check whether your sourcing strategy is working against your climate targets: diversifying suppliers across more countries and industries, usually framed as resilience, may be inflating the emissions you will eventually have to report and defend.
- **For general readers and consumers:** When a brand advertises its carbon numbers, the figures typically cover its own factories and electricity — the far bigger share buried in its suppliers is often not measured at all.

**Caveats:** Observational cross-sectional firm data, so causal claims are limited; relies on voluntary and incomplete emissions disclosure, which the authors themselves flag as a data constraint; no consumer or reputational outcomes tested.

### 4. Consumers say they'd rather be regulated than lectured on plastic

**Journal:** *Journal of Macromarketing* | **Published:** 2026-11-01
**Work ID:** W7225111854 | **DOI:** 10.1177/02761467261503039

**The study:** Researchers studying single-use plastic milk packaging in New Zealand combined 20 consumer interviews with a best-worst scaling survey of 400 consumers to map what actually stops people from reducing plastic. Most of the barriers people named were "opportunity" constraints built into the retail and packaging system — no refill option, no return infrastructure — rather than gaps in knowledge or motivation. When ranking policy options, respondents preferred incentives and regulation over information-only campaigns. The authors package this into a framework (TISI) that separates consumers' market role (purchase, use, return, refill) from their citizen role (acceptance, perceived fairness, willingness to comply).

**Why it matters:** It suggests the standard sustainability playbook — awareness campaigns and on-pack nudges — is aimed at the wrong constraint, and that consumers may be more willing to accept rules and incentives than brands assume.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands running recycling or refill campaigns could test whether the bottleneck is really consumer awareness or missing infrastructure before spending on another education push; the data here also hints that publicly backing regulation may be less reputationally risky with consumers than companies fear, though that was measured in one category in one country.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For shoppers, this is validation of a common frustration: people often want to cut plastic but have no practical way to return or refill, and the research suggests the burden has been misplaced on individual virtue.

**Caveats:** Single country (New Zealand) and a single category (milk packaging); stated preferences and rankings rather than observed purchase or return behavior; modest survey sample (N=400) alongside 20 interviews.

### 5. Green business models scale just as well as the ordinary kind — but only via the same boring levers

**Journal:** *Journal of Business Research* | **Published:** 2026-10-30
**Work ID:** W7224854055 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jbusres.2026.116627

**The study:** Researchers applied fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis to 21 sharing-mobility providers, modeling market scaling as a function of five design choices: environmental ambitions, alignment with public stakeholders, breadth of value propositions, digital intensity, and revenue-stream diversification. Six configurations emerged, four tied to successful scaling and two to failure. Firms with strong environmental ambitions and close public-sector alignment scaled about as well as those without; what consistently separated winners from losers in both groups was having multiple value propositions or diversified revenue streams. Successfully scaled firms also showed congruence between their green ambitions and their public-stakeholder alignment.

**Why it matters:** It pushes back on the assumption that sustainability commitments are a drag on growth, while deflating the idea that they are themselves a growth engine — the commercial fundamentals still do the work.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For leaders weighing how hard to lean into environmental positioning: this evidence suggests genuine green ambition need not cost scalability, but it also won't substitute for portfolio breadth and revenue diversification — and half-committing (green claims without matching public-sector alignment) sits in the failure configurations.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A useful lens for consumers evaluating shared-mobility services: the ones that survive tend to be those selling several things to several customer types, not necessarily the greenest or the most digital.

**Caveats:** Very small sample of firms (21) in one sector (sharing mobility); configurational analysis identifies patterns of co-occurrence, not causation; no consumer-level data.

### 6. PR agencies are quietly using AI — but in Arabic, humans are still doing the hard part

**Journal:** *Journal of Communication Management* | **Published:** 2026-10-14
**Work ID:** W7221812755 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jcom-02-2026-0066

**The study:** Through 20 semi-structured interviews with senior PR practitioners, association leaders and academics across six Arab countries, plus a directed content analysis of global and regional industry guidelines, researchers mapped where AI is actually being used in public relations. Adoption is early-stage and narrow, concentrated in content assistance, media intelligence and reputation monitoring, driven by efficiency pressure and client expectations. The main brakes are skills gaps, thin Arabic-language tooling and unclear governance. Global industry discourse converges on "augmentation" with disclosure and human oversight, but the authors argue that in under-resourced languages, maintaining cultural and linguistic fit is itself substantial hidden human labor.

**Why it matters:** Reputation work depends on tone and cultural nuance, and this suggests the efficiency case for AI in communications weakens sharply outside English — a gap that global agencies and in-house teams rarely budget for.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Communications leaders running multilingual teams could audit where AI output actually requires rewriting rather than editing, treat localization as a staffed production stage rather than a tool setting, and consider making sociolinguistic review an explicit step in human-in-the-loop policies.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It explains why brand messaging in some languages can read as oddly flat or off-key: the tools behind it were built for English, and the cultural correction is done by people whose work is largely invisible.

**Caveats:** Qualitative interview study with a small, purposively selected senior sample (N=20) in one region; cross-regional comparison rests on documents rather than primary data; practitioner perceptions, not measured AI performance or audience response.

### 7. What makes communicators feel like crisis leaders: ethics support and equity resources

**Journal:** *International Journal of Strategic Communication* | **Published:** 2026-09-07
**Work ID:** W7211909650 | **DOI:** 10.1080/1553118x.2026.2722034

**The study:** An online survey of 1,044 public relations practitioners — nearly 43% with more than a decade of experience — examined how pandemic-era work challenges related to their sense of crisis leadership. Perceived access to ethics resources and organizational equity support both mediated the link between work strain and crisis leadership perceptions, and perceived effectiveness of the organization's crisis communication further shaped those indirect effects.

**Why it matters:** It points to concrete organizational investments — ethics guidance and equity support infrastructure — as levers that may strengthen how communications teams perform and see themselves under pressure, rather than treating crisis leadership as an individual trait.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Comms leaders could treat ethics counsel and equity support as crisis-readiness infrastructure, not HR nice-to-haves, and test whether teams with documented ethics resources report stronger confidence and better-rated crisis output than those without.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Limited direct relevance for consumers, beyond the reminder that how well a company talks to the public in a crisis partly reflects how well it supports the people writing the statements.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional self-report survey — cannot establish causation; all variables are practitioner perceptions, including the outcome; retrospective pandemic framing; sample recruitment and country coverage not specified in the abstract.

### 8. Talking up AI wins positive sentiment but loses audience — unless you tie it to social good

**Journal:** *Public Relations Review* | **Published:** 2026-09-05
**Work ID:** W7208830817 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.pubrev.2026.102764

**The study:** A mixed-method study of corporate AI communication in Korea combined a computational analysis of companies' social media posts (Study 1) with in-depth interviews with communication practitioners (Study 2). Corporate AI messaging was overwhelmingly instrumental — AI as business value, operational enabler or brand positioning — with little attention to societal or ethical implications. Although AI posts drew generally positive sentiment, they attracted less engagement and fewer positive comments than non-AI posts; framing AI within a corporate social responsibility context erased much of that engagement penalty, and AI messaging landed better at conventional firms than at high-tech firms where AI is simply expected.

**Why it matters:** Companies are racing to announce AI capabilities, but this evidence suggests the default "look how efficient we are" frame is the least engaging option and that the payoff depends heavily on whether audiences already expect AI from your category.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Communications leaders could audit their own AI announcements for the instrumental-versus-societal balance and A/B test CSR-anchored framing against efficiency framing; the Korean pattern also hints that AI claims are a differentiator for legacy brands and a non-event for tech brands — worth checking in your own channel data before assuming it transfers.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For readers, the study is a window into how little of corporate AI talk addresses the questions the public actually asks — jobs, ethics, accountability — and how practitioners themselves admit they stay cautious about those topics even when aware of them.

**Caveats:** Single country (Korea) with a distinctive state-guided tech sector; social media engagement metrics are correlational, not causal; interview sample size not reported in the abstract; findings may not generalize to Western markets or to regulated industries.

### 9. PR practitioners say using AI is no longer a choice — it is the price of staying competitive

**Journal:** *Public Relations Inquiry* | **Published:** 2026-10-16
**Work ID:** W7222613543 | **DOI:** 10.1177/2046147x261499381

**The study:** Interviews with 22 public relations practitioners explored how they are deploying AI bots and agentic systems for campaign development, decision-making and stakeholder research. Practitioners consistently described AI as compulsory for remaining competitive, as a form of self-optimization, and as a productivity multiplier — narratives the author reads as market logic shaping choices that practitioners experience as autonomous. At the same time, respondents leaned hard on professional judgment, context and a sense of responsibility, pointing to human oversight, risk management and formal governance as what keeps the work defensible in the absence of regulation.

**Why it matters:** It documents how AI adoption in reputation work is being driven less by evidence of better outcomes than by competitive anxiety — a dynamic that leaves governance to individual practitioners' discretion.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Agency and in-house communications leaders can use this as a prompt to formalize what is currently informal: written AI governance, defined human sign-off points, and a candid internal conversation about whether AI use is being justified by results or by fear of falling behind.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The press releases, statements and stakeholder research shaping brand messages are increasingly AI-assisted, with oversight resting on individual professionals' judgment rather than any rulebook.

**Caveats:** Small qualitative interview sample (N=22); self-reported practice rather than observed outputs or performance data; heavily theory-driven interpretation (Foucault, Bourdieu) that readers may weigh differently; no measurement of whether AI use actually improved campaign results.

### 10. In CSR posts, inviting people in beats who's doing the talking

**Journal:** *Journal of Communication Management* | **Published:** 2026-10-23
**Work ID:** W7223321733 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jcom-02-2026-0100

**The study:** Researchers ran a 2x2 between-subjects experiment with 406 active Facebook users in India, varying who posted a corporate social responsibility message (a company vs. an NGO) and whether the post contained strong or weak co-creation cues — language inviting audiences to participate rather than simply informing them. Posts with high co-creation cues significantly raised perceived source credibility, engagement, and intent to share (e-WOM), and largely wiped out the independent effect of who the sender was. NGO-attributed posts outperformed corporate ones on e-WOM, but only when the post also invited participation; engagement partially mediated the path from credibility to sharing.

**Why it matters:** Brands spend heavily on NGO partnerships to borrow credibility for social-impact messaging, but this suggests the format of the message — participatory versus broadcast — does more work than the logo attached to it.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** A cheap test for communications teams: run matched CSR posts, one informational and one explicitly inviting audience contribution, and compare shares and comments. The experiment suggests participatory framing may be the bigger lever — and that NGO co-branding only pays off on sharing when the post actually asks people to take part, not when it just carries a partner logo.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Skepticism toward corporate do-gooding is widespread, yet this study shows what makes people believe and pass along a social-good message is less about who signed it than whether it treats them as a participant rather than an audience — a useful tell when deciding whether a cause post deserves your share.

**Caveats:** Single country (India, which has a mandated-CSR regime) and a single platform (Facebook); online experiment with stated engagement and sharing intentions rather than observed behavior; stimuli were constructed posts, not live campaigns.

### 11. Who really built the new golf league's brand? Content creators the league doesn't control

**Journal:** *Sport Marketing Quarterly* | **Published:** 2026-11-01
**Work ID:** W7225053690 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10616934261499879

**The study:** Using netnography of league and stakeholder social media content across the creation and first season of the TMRW Golf League — the indoor, tech-driven, extended-reality golf competition — the researchers combined structural topic modeling with qualitative content analysis to trace how brand meaning was negotiated. They find that external new media content creators, not the league's own communications, did much of the work of legitimating and framing the league's novelty, appeal and competitive credibility.

**Why it matters:** For any brand launching something genuinely new, the narrative that sticks is substantially authored by third-party creators, which makes creator relations a core part of launch strategy rather than an add-on to paid media.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Treat independent creators as co-authors of the launch narrative: map which themes they amplify versus which ones your own channels push, and watch for gaps. The lesson from this case is that legitimacy for an unfamiliar product category tends to be conferred by outsiders — worth building into launch measurement rather than assuming owned media sets the frame.
- **For general readers and consumers:** When a new sport, format or product suddenly feels credible, it is often because a cluster of creators decided it was worth talking about — a reminder of how much of what we accept as 'a real thing' is manufactured in feeds rather than press releases.

**Caveats:** Single-case study of one new league over one season; observational social media content analysis with no causal test; findings are interpretive and may not generalize to other categories or markets.

### 12. A century of NFL marketing, read through Black-owned magazines

**Journal:** *Journal of Historical Research in Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-11-02
**Work ID:** W7225126842 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jhrm-07-2025-0036

**The study:** This viewpoint article assembles 72 media meta-texts — 20 articles from the Crisis (1910–1960), 25 from Ebony (1945–2026), 21 from Jet (1950–2015) and six transcripts of NFL-branded audio/visual content (2014–2026) — and analyzes them thematically and semiotically across four eras of American racial politics. The authors argue the league repeatedly deployed what they term "racelighting": branded entertainment that reads as neutral or progressive on the surface while repurposing Black cultural and political capital to serve commercial and hegemonic interests.

**Why it matters:** It puts a century of archival evidence behind a question every brand leader now faces — whether social-justice-adjacent marketing advances a cause or absorbs and defuses it — and shows how the pattern can persist across very different political eras.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** A reputational stress test for purpose marketing: compare what your campaign claims with how the communities it invokes actually describe it in their own media. The historical pattern here suggests campaigns that align activist imagery with commercial interests tend to be recognized as such by the constituencies involved, long before the mainstream press catches on.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The piece offers a lens for reading sports and brand activism generally — asking who gains cultural power from a gesture of solidarity, and whether a campaign's progressive framing survives contact with the archive of how the same institution behaved in earlier decades.

**Caveats:** Labeled a viewpoint article: interpretive historical and semiotic analysis of a curated archive, not hypothesis testing; single league in a single country; explicitly argued from a critical theoretical standpoint (Afro-pessimism, Racial Formation Theory), so conclusions are contestable readings rather than measured effects.

### 13. The "terroir paradox": why famous local food can still be invisible in the nearest big city

**Journal:** *British Food Journal* | **Published:** 2026-10-27
**Work ID:** W7223808860 | **DOI:** 10.1108/bfj-04-2026-0545

**The study:** Researchers conducted an interpretivist qualitative case study of the food system linking Ödemiş, a rural production district in western Türkiye, with the urban gastronomy market of nearby İzmir, interviewing 17 purposively selected stakeholders (producers, cooperatives, traders and institutional actors) and triangulating with official agricultural statistics and geographical-indication documents. Despite protected-origin potatoes and olive oil and a strong dairy sector, Ödemiş products are barely represented in İzmir restaurants and retail. The barriers were not product quality but rising input costs, logistics gaps, distrust of cooperatives, weak promotion, reliance on informal personal networks instead of institutional channels, and the erosion of knowledge between generations. The authors call this the "terroir paradox": strong production terroir, weak distribution and representation terroir.

**Why it matters:** Origin certification and product reputation are often treated as the finish line for regional food branding, but this case suggests the real bottleneck sits in distribution, collective institutions and storytelling — the parts no certificate covers.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For brand and category managers working with regional or origin-certified suppliers, this is a prompt to audit the gap between a product's reputational assets and its actual shelf and menu presence — and to ask whether certification budgets would do more work if redirected to logistics, cooperative capacity or trade-facing promotion. One district's experience is a hypothesis to test against your own supply base, not a general law.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For shoppers and diners, it explains why the celebrated specialty from the region an hour away is so hard to find in your own city: the bottleneck is usually trucks, middlemen and broken local institutions, not farming.

**Caveats:** Qualitative case study of a single district–city pair in one country; 17 interviews; no quantitative market or sales data; findings are propositions for comparative testing rather than generalizable estimates.

### 14. Students say they care about sustainable eating — but convenience and company decide where they eat

**Journal:** *British Food Journal* | **Published:** 2026-10-20
**Work ID:** W7223028679 | **DOI:** 10.1108/bfj-01-2026-0069

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 610 students at the Universities of Bologna and Parma in northern Italy (a convenience sample) and used PLS-SEM to model what drives perceived value of university canteens, combining the motivation–opportunity–ability framework with an eating-related eco-concern questionnaire and a sustainable healthy diet index. Contextual "opportunity" factors — accessibility, convenience and the chance to socialize — were the dominant drivers of perceived value. Individual pro-environmental motivation and personal dietary orientation contributed comparatively little.

**Why it matters:** It is a concrete illustration of the gap between green values and everyday food choices: in an institutional setting, the environment people eat in outweighs the attitudes they bring to it.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For anyone marketing sustainable food — campus caterers, workplace canteens, grocery retailers — the implication worth testing is that values-based messaging may underperform structural changes to access, speed, price and sociability. Consider running communication and layout/convenience interventions as competing arms rather than assuming persuasion does the heavy lifting.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A useful mirror for consumers: your eco-concern may be real, yet it is probably not what determines today's lunch. Where you eat is largely decided by what is close, quick and shared with friends.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey, so no causal claims; convenience sample of students in a single Italian region; measures perceived value and self-reported orientation rather than observed food purchases or waste.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- **Brand meaning is authored by outsiders.** "Who really built the new golf league's brand? Content creators the league doesn't control" (W7225053690) lands on the same conclusion as last month's "Residents are branding your city whether you invite them to or not" (W7202171291): the entities paying for the brand are not the ones setting its meaning. One is a city, one is a new sports property, and both find that unplanned, third-party meaning-making outweighs owned channels.
- **Information-deficit thinking keeps failing.** "Consumers say they'd rather be regulated than lectured on plastic" (W7225111854) and "Students say they care about sustainable eating — but convenience and company decide where they eat" (W7223028679) both locate the binding constraint outside the individual's head, echoing "Why free pads don't get used" (W7214428578) and "Not all opposition is persuadable" (W7214777517). The prior digest found the non-informational barrier to be social (stigma, moral orientation); this batch finds it material (no refill point, no proximity, no time).
- **CSR communication audited against what the organization actually does.** "A century of NFL marketing, read through Black-owned magazines" (W7225126842) extends the argument of "India's bidi industry borrowed Big Tobacco's playbook" (W7220403023) — social-impact communication as reputational cover — from supply-chain evidence to a century of archival evidence.

**Advances**
- **AI in PR moves from role identity to market compulsion and governance vacuum.** The previous digest established that AI is reshaping PR roles toward judgment and ethical stewardship ("The PR manager's job is quietly becoming 'ethical advisor to the machine,'" W7213310447) and that organizational support, not individual training, predicted effectiveness ("AI training didn't move the needle," W7205004055). The new batch explains *why* adoption is happening anyway: "PR practitioners say using AI is no longer a choice" (W7222613543) documents competitive anxiety rather than measured results as the driver, with governance left to individual discretion — the precise gap W7205004055 implied mattered. "PR agencies are quietly using AI — but in Arabic, humans are still doing the hard part" (W7221812755) adds a cost dimension the earlier work missed: the efficiency case thins sharply outside English.
- **Crisis communication capability as organizational infrastructure.** "What makes communicators feel like crisis leaders: ethics support and equity resources" (W7211909650) advances "Why crisis playbooks break down in practice" (W7203759226). The earlier study found practitioners knew the right response and were overruled; this one identifies what institutional backing appears to strengthen their standing under pressure.
- **Sustainability claims versus the system behind them.** "Why companies cannot count the emissions hiding in their supply base" (W7224156801) and "When populism rises, green packaging quietly disappears" (W7214508893) push the previous digest's CSR-credibility thread (W7220403023) from labor exposure into measurement and political contingency.

**Counterpoints**
- **Does formal apparatus do anything?** The previous digest's running theme was that paperwork underperforms visible behavior ("Compliance programs barely work on their own," W7213244866; "AI training didn't move the needle," W7205004055). "What makes communicators feel like crisis leaders" (W7211909650) complicates that: here, perceived *access* to ethics resources and equity support mediated the relationship between work strain and crisis leadership. The outcome is a self-reported perception rather than observed behavior, so the two sit in tension rather than contradiction — worth watching whether formal resources matter more for practitioner capability than for employee conduct.
- **Does green positioning pay?** "Green business models scale just as well as the ordinary kind" (W7224854055) deflates both the cost and the growth-engine stories about environmental ambition, where the previous digest's "symbolic promotions" work (W7215933723) suggested values-based appeals could carry real commercial lift. The distinction appears to be identity-based appeals (which moved sales) versus environmental ambition as a business-model attribute (which did not move scaling either way).

**New themes**
- **Audience response to corporate AI talk**, not just internal AI practice: "Talking up AI wins positive sentiment but loses audience" (W7208830817) is the first study across either digest to measure how publics react to AI messaging.
- **Message format over source identity** in CSR: "In CSR posts, inviting people in beats who's doing the talking" (W7223321733) challenges the NGO-partnership credibility-borrowing model, which neither digest had tested before.
- **Epistemic credibility as an engagement driver**: "Big Pharma's pandemic tweets: dry facts beat upbeat framing" (W7223996694).
- **Distribution as the missing half of origin branding**: "The 'terroir paradox'" (W7223808860) introduces place-of-origin reputation economics, absent from the previous run.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Five studies in this batch, approaching from completely different directions, find that green commitments are governed by politics, logistics and infrastructure rather than by consumer conviction or corporate intent. Firms cut back on eco-friendly packaging when the political signal turned ("When populism rises, green packaging quietly disappears," W7214508893); most of a firm's footprint sits in a supply base it structurally cannot count, with sourcing diversification inflating it ("Why companies cannot count the emissions hiding in their supply base," W7224156801); consumers name missing refill and return systems rather than ignorance as the barrier and rank regulation above information campaigns ("Consumers say they'd rather be regulated than lectured on plastic," W7225111854); canteen users' choices are driven by access, speed and sociability rather than eco-concern ("Students say they care about sustainable eating," W7223028679); and strong environmental ambition neither helped nor hindered scaling, which turned on revenue diversification ("Green business models scale just as well as the ordinary kind," W7224854055).

**Why pitch this now:** Scope 3 disclosure is moving from voluntary to mandatory across several reporting regimes just as "greenhushing" spreads and political hostility to climate policy intensifies — so brands are being required to publish numbers they cannot verify about a footprint they do not control, while the consumer-education campaigns that filled the gap turn out to be aimed at the wrong constraint.

**Angle:** A feature on sustainability marketing as a structural problem misdiagnosed as a persuasion problem — written for brand, packaging and corporate affairs leaders deciding what to promise next, with a strong secondary read for consumers who have been told their choices are the lever.

**Potential headlines:**
- Green Packaging Tracks Politics More Closely Than It Tracks Shoppers
- Consumers Keep Asking to Be Regulated. Brands Keep Sending Them Leaflets.
- The Climate Promise on Your Packaging Rests on Numbers Nobody Can Check

**Potential outlets:**
- **Marketing Week** — its readership is actively defending sustainability positioning to finance directors, and the finding that green ambition is commercially neutral rather than a growth driver directly reframes that argument.
- **Retail Dive** — the refill-and-return infrastructure gap and private-label packaging retreat are retail operations stories, not just messaging ones.
- **Bloomberg Green** — the Scope 3 measurement study and the Paris-exit packaging retreat fit its corporate climate-accountability reporting, which already tracks disclosure quality.
- **Grist** — runs consumer-facing work skeptical of individual-responsibility framing, and the plastics and canteen studies hand it empirical backing for that case.

## Story Ideas by Study

- **When populism rises, green packaging quietly disappears (Work ID W7214508893):** A difference-in-differences study showing CPG firms quietly dialed back eco-packaging after the Paris exit announcement — sustainability as a political variable, not a brand value — for a CMO-audience column or a business-section piece on greenhushing.
- **Big Pharma's pandemic tweets: dry facts beat upbeat framing (Work ID W7223996694):** 6,466 tweets suggesting that amplifying what is true outperforms rebutting what is false, and neutral framing beats positive framing — for a PR-trade analysis on misinformation response playbooks.
- **Why companies cannot count the emissions hiding in their supply base (Work ID W7224156801):** Supplier diversification, usually sold as resilience, appears to inflate the emissions a brand will have to report and defend — for a supply-chain or sustainability-trade explainer, or a corporate-finance piece on disclosure risk.
- **Consumers say they'd rather be regulated than lectured on plastic (Work ID W7225111854):** Shoppers ranked incentives and rules above information campaigns, suggesting that publicly backing regulation may be less reputationally risky than brands assume — for a public-affairs or packaging-trade piece, or a consumer column on the misplaced burden of individual virtue.
