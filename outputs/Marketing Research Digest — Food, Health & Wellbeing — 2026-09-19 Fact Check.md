### Study 1: The most politically extreme customers are your most loyal — and the least likely to notice a price hike
**Work ID:** W7213535017 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline paraphrase of price insensitivity** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the least likely to notice a price hike"
- **Abstract says:** "they are also less sensitive to price increases by their chosen brands, even when viable alternatives exist"
- **Problem:** Lower price sensitivity is a response measure, not evidence that extreme consumers fail to perceive or notice price increases; the abstract makes no claim about awareness.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "the least likely to walk away from a price hike" or "the least price-sensitive"."

**Issue B. Number of studies unspecified** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Across a multi-study program, consumers with more extreme political identities showed higher repurchase likelihood"
- **Abstract says:** "The abstract does not state how many studies were run or what methods were used."
- **Problem:** "Multi-study program" implies a known design the abstract does not describe; the digest's own caveat acknowledges this, but the body states it as fact.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say "the research reports" and note that the abstract gives no design details."

### Study 2: "High protein" doesn't make shoppers think a food is healthier — it makes them think it's more processed
**Work ID:** W7208834717 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Relative importance of prior attitude overstated** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "since prior attitude did more work than the label itself"
- **Abstract says:** "Attitudes toward protein products moderated the label's effect across nutritional judgments, whereas drive for muscularity did not moderate labeling effects."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports moderation, not a comparison of effect magnitudes between attitude and label; "did more work" implies an ordering the abstract does not support.
- **Suggested fix:** "Change to "since prior attitude shaped how people read the label"."

**Issue B. Dropped null moderator** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "existing attitudes toward protein products moderating responses throughout"
- **Abstract says:** "Attitudes toward protein products moderated the label's effect across nutritional judgments, whereas drive for muscularity did not moderate labeling effects."
- **Problem:** The abstract's contrast with drive for muscularity (measured, non-significant) is omitted, which is informative for segmentation advice.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add that drive for muscularity was measured but did not moderate the label's effect."

### Study 8: Consumers file "cell-based sausage" by how it was made, not by what it is
**Work ID:** W7211919306 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Consumer angle overreads the sorting data** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "told something was grown from cells, shoppers stopped thinking "sausage" and started thinking "lab.""
- **Abstract says:** "the participants' categorization of the presented written product concepts was primarily influenced by the production method labels and biological source descriptors along three dimensions"
- **Problem:** The abstract reports categorization dimensions, not that participants formed a "lab" category, and the participants were 154 German-speaking Swiss omnivores rather than shoppers in general.
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "these participants grouped the concepts by how they were produced rather than by food type"."

**Issue B. Separate-category inference stated as result** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "these results indicate explicit "cell-based" labeling does the opposite, carving out a separate mental category"
- **Abstract says:** "Prior research has suggested that plant-based alternatives may be more likely to be adopted when they are perceived as belonging to the same category as their animal-based counterparts... results revealed that the participants' categorization... was primarily influenced by the production method labels and biological source descriptors"
- **Problem:** The abstract stops at describing categorization dimensions and offers the acceptance link only as background; it does not conclude that cell-based labels create a separate category that harms adoption.
- **Suggested fix:** "Frame as an implication the authors raise for future research rather than a demonstrated result."

### Study 9: Free vegetable-rich school lunches changed nothing — because the kids didn't eat them
**Work ID:** W7202165920 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Headline states explanation as established** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Free vegetable-rich school lunches changed nothing — because the kids didn't eat them"
- **Abstract says:** "Insufficient meal consumption, driven by low meal acceptance, social dynamics, and cultural unfamiliarity with warm lunches at school, is the most plausible explanation for the absence of effects."
- **Problem:** The abstract offers low consumption as the most plausible interpretation, not a measured cause; the body correctly attributes it to the authors, but the headline asserts it.
- **Suggested fix:** "Add hedging, e.g. "— most likely because the kids didn't eat them"."

**Issue B. Unsupported descriptors** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "A well-intentioned, well-funded healthy-food program"
- **Abstract says:** "The abstract describes a school-based lunch programme offering vegetable-rich meals on 3 school days per week but says nothing about cost to families or funding."
- **Problem:** "Free" (headline) and "well-funded" are not verifiable from the abstract.
- **Suggested fix:** "Drop "free" and "well-funded" or mark as unverifiable from the abstract."

### Study 10: Ads run during wartime were judged more trustworthy — and lifted brands more than peacetime ads
**Work ID:** W7212377582 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal language in headline** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "and lifted brands more than peacetime ads"
- **Abstract says:** "wartime advertising was perceived as more reliable and was associated with stronger brand evaluations"
- **Problem:** This is an observational comparison of two periods; "lifted brands" asserts a causal effect on brand outcomes that the abstract describes only as an association with brand evaluations (not sales).
- **Suggested fix:** "Reword to "— and were associated with stronger brand evaluations than peacetime ads"."

**Issue B. Clutter explanation not in abstract** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the survivors in the break faced less clutter and were received more credibly"
- **Abstract says:** "Advertising spending sharply declines during wartime... wartime advertising was perceived as more reliable"
- **Problem:** Reduced clutter as the reason for higher perceived reliability is the digest's interpretation; the abstract attributes the pattern to a shift toward informational and socially oriented communication.
- **Suggested fix:** "Attribute the mechanism to the content shift the abstract reports, and present the clutter idea explicitly as a hypothesis."

### Study 11: When an AI 'doctor' gives health advice, numbers beat stories — up to a point
**Work ID:** W7212302631 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Moderator overstated** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "The advantage disappeared when the information came from a 'mirrored' digital human (a digital replica of a real person) rather than a purely virtual one."
- **Abstract says:** "However, when health information is delivered by mirrored rather than virtual digital humans, this mediating effect becomes non-significant."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports that the mediation through perceived deceptiveness became non-significant for mirrored digital humans, not that the persuasive advantage of statistical evidence itself vanished. The digest's phrasing, and the angle claim that "replicas of real humans can carry anecdote," go beyond what is reported.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say the mediating route through perceived deceptiveness was no longer significant for mirrored digital humans, and soften the angle to note the mechanism did not hold rather than asserting anecdote works for digital twins."

**Issue B. Definition of 'mirrored' digital human** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "a 'mirrored' digital human (a digital replica of a real person)"
- **Abstract says:** "when health information is delivered by mirrored rather than virtual digital humans"
- **Problem:** The abstract does not define what a mirrored digital human is; the gloss is an assumption.
- **Suggested fix:** "Attribute the definition or note it is the authors' terminology, unverifiable from the abstract."

### Study 14: The most effective men's health program may be the one that never mentions health
**Work ID:** W7213513131 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Comparative effectiveness implied** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "The most effective men's health program may be the one that never mentions health"
- **Abstract says:** "The findings suggest that effective gender-responsive health promotion for men may be dependent on creating cultural conditions which reduce social risk and are familiar to men."
- **Problem:** The study is an interpretive qualitative analysis of one initiative; it does not compare programs or measure effectiveness, so "most effective" (even hedged) overstates the design.

### Study 15: Kids know sugar is bad for them. They just really like it
**Work ID:** W7213326632 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Prescriptive inference from qualitative data** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the takeaway is that education-only messaging to children is likely to fail; the actionable variables are where sugary products appear in a child's day"
- **Abstract says:** "These findings indicate that intake can be facilitated (or hindered) by capability (e.g., knowledge of sugar sources), opportunity (e.g., family influences, related learning at school), and motivation (e.g., sensory appeal, affective attachment to sweets)."
- **Problem:** The abstract frames knowledge/capability as both barrier and facilitator and makes no claim about the failure of education-based messaging; the digest converts exploratory focus-group insight into a directive about what will not work.
- **Suggested fix:** "Soften to "suggests information alone may not be enough" and note the study informs, rather than tests, intervention design."

**Issue B. Children's age** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "Children as young as nine can explain why sugar is bad for them"
- **Abstract says:** "Six focus groups were conducted across two schools, involving 44 fourth-grade students."
- **Problem:** The abstract gives grade level, not ages; the specific age is an inference. Unverifiable from abstract only.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say "fourth-grade children" rather than specifying age."

### Study 16: Can a VR headset make people want to volunteer abroad?
**Work ID:** W7171788770 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Association read as causal backfire** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "guilt-based moral appeals may actively backfire"
- **Abstract says:** "Studies 3 and 4 reveal that self-congruence positively predicts willingness to engage, whereas moral obligation is negatively associated with it."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports a negative association between a measured sense of moral obligation and willingness, not an experimental test of guilt-based appeals; saying such appeals "backfire" implies a manipulated causal effect on messaging. The consumer angle repeats this ("feeling obligated makes people less willing to sign up").
- **Suggested fix:** "State that felt moral obligation was negatively associated with willingness, and that whether obligation-framed appeals reduce sign-ups remains untested."

**Work ID:** W7170190396 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Causal headline for survey data** — Severity: Minor

- **Abstract says:** "Results show that opaque algorithms and poor governance potentially amplify PRDL and FNAX, eroding institutional trust and promoting withdrawal behaviour."

### Study 20: Financial anxiety hurts men and women's wellbeing in exactly the same way
**Work ID:** W7203676845 | **Verdict:** ⚠️ Minor issues

**Issue A. Mediation share overstated** — Severity: Moderate

- **As written:** "it helps mostly by making you less anxious"
- **Abstract says:** "financial capability was negatively associated with financial anxiety and positively associated with financial well-being, both directly and indirectly... its mediating role in the capability–well-being link was significant."
- **Problem:** The abstract reports significant direct and indirect effects but does not say the indirect (anxiety) route accounts for most of the relationship; "mostly" asserts a magnitude not in the abstract. The body's "anxiety carrying a significant share of the effect" is similarly quantified.
- **Suggested fix:** "Say financial capability is linked to wellbeing both directly and partly through lower anxiety, without ranking the routes."

**Issue B. Source of level difference** — Severity: Minor

- **As written:** "the difference appears to be in levels of anxiety, not in how anxiety operates"
- **Abstract says:** "Historically, empirical evidence has suggested that women report higher levels of financial anxiety than men"
- **Problem:** The higher anxiety levels among women come from prior literature cited as background, not necessarily from this study's own comparison.
- **Suggested fix:** "Attribute the level difference to prior research and confine this study's contribution to the gender-invariant mechanism."
