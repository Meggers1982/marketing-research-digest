### 1. Robots that show feelings win customers — unless the service involves something private

**Journal:** *International Journal of Research in Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-01
**Work ID:** W7215001822 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.ijresmar.2026.09.008

**The study:** Researchers studied real users of service robots across multiple settings, combining exploratory interviews, a field survey, and a series of field and lab experiments with Japanese robot users plus international consumers. They found that when customers perceive a robot as empathetic, they become more absorbed in the experience (what the authors call escapism) and more likely to use the service again — in both fun and purely functional settings. The effect reverses in contexts where privacy sensitivity is high: there, emotional expressiveness from the robot suppresses engagement rather than building it.

**Why it matters:** Companies deploying service robots and AI agents typically dial empathy up as a universal design default, but this evidence suggests emotional warmth is context-dependent and can actively backfire where customers feel exposed.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Treat robot empathy as a setting, not a feature. The study points to a testable segmentation: build emotionally resonant, imagination-triggering behaviors into low-privacy touchpoints (retail greeting, hospitality, entertainment) while shifting to neutral, professional competence in high-privacy ones (health, finance, anything involving personal data). Worth piloting both scripts in parallel on the same robot fleet before committing to one personality.
- **For general readers and consumers:** There's a reason a chirpy, warm machine feels charming in a hotel lobby and faintly creepy at a pharmacy counter. The research suggests consumers unconsciously read a robot's emotional display as a signal about how much of themselves they're exposing — and warmth from a machine that is also collecting sensitive information reads as intrusion, not care.

**Caveats:** Much of the field work was conducted with Japanese robot users, where familiarity with service robots is unusually high, so cultural generalization is uncertain; the package mixes field and lab studies and the abstract does not report sample sizes or effect magnitudes.

### 2. Why 'do you trust AI?' is the wrong question to ask shoppers

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing* | **Published:** 2026-10-01
**Work ID:** W7220664143 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretai.2026.10.001

**The study:** A multi-study, multi-method research program in the *Journal of Retailing* built and validated a Customer Acceptance of Artificial Intelligence (CAAI) scale specific to retail, rather than borrowing general technology-acceptance measures. Acceptance broke into eight distinct dimensions: personalization, accuracy, safety, speed, convenience, humanization, friendliness, and emotionality. Tested across varied retail settings in 32-item and 8-item versions, higher CAAI scores tracked with more word of mouth, greater share of usage, higher willingness to pay, and higher likelihood of adopting AI-enabled retail tools — and the scale out-predicted established technology acceptance measures.

**Why it matters:** It gives retailers a diagnostic that separates functional acceptance (accuracy, speed, safety) from social acceptance (humanization, friendliness, emotionality), so a struggling AI deployment can be traced to a specific failure rather than written off as generic customer resistance.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Audit your customer-facing AI against all eight dimensions instead of a single satisfaction score. The interesting managerial question the scale raises is whether the warmth dimensions pay off at all in your category, or whether customers would trade friendliness for accuracy — and the 8-item short form makes it cheap to run that comparison across chatbots, recommendation engines and in-store tools.
- **For general readers and consumers:** When people say they don't like a store's AI, they usually mean something specific: it's slow, it's wrong, it feels invasive, or it's trying too hard to be your friend. This research suggests those are genuinely separate complaints, and lumping them together is why so many retail AI rollouts fail to improve.

**Caveats:** This is primarily a measurement paper; the link between CAAI scores and outcomes such as willingness to pay is correlational rather than causal. The abstract does not disclose sample sizes, recruitment sources, or countries, and outcomes appear to be self-reported rather than observed purchases.

### 3. The hidden cost of heavy AI use at work: employees start cutting corners

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-10-06
**Work ID:** W7220733006 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105149

**The study:** Researchers combined a scenario-based experiment with a three-wave field survey of employees to test how generative AI use in daily work relates to "corner-cutting behavior" — skipping steps, lowering quality thresholds, shipping good-enough work. They found an inverted U-shaped path: as GenAI use rises from low to moderate, employees report more performance pressure, which in turn drives more corner-cutting; at very high usage levels the pressure effect reverses and declines. The curve was sharpest for high-status employees and flattest for low-status ones, with the relationship fully reversing only at extremely low workplace status.

**Why it matters:** Companies rolling out GenAI tools typically measure output speed, not whether the prompt-response grind is quietly pushing staff to take shortcuts — and the employees most at risk are the senior, visible ones whose work carries the most weight.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Marketing and agency leaders deploying GenAI across creative and analytics teams should consider tracking quality-control metrics, not just throughput, during the awkward middle stage of adoption — this research suggests the danger zone is partial, inconsistent use rather than either light or fully fluent use, which argues for pushing teams through the learning curve quickly rather than leaving them half-trained.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If you feel more harried since your employer added AI tools, not less, you are not imagining it: the effort of writing prompts and fixing mediocre output can raise the bar on what's expected of you faster than it raises what you can actually deliver.

**Caveats:** Scenario-based experiment plus self-reported survey measures of corner-cutting rather than observed work product; sample size, country and industry are not specified in the abstract; the inverted-U result depends on modeling choices and the "extremely low status" reversal rests on a narrow slice of the sample.

### 4. How a manufacturer actually became data-driven — a three-stage roadmap from inside one firm

**Journal:** *Industrial Marketing Management* | **Published:** 2026-09-22
**Work ID:** W7213979252 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.indmarman.2026.09.006

**The study:** Researchers conducted an embedded longitudinal case study of a European manufacturing SME, tracking 16 distinct data-centric marketing practices as they emerged and matured. They found the transformation unfolded in three cumulative phases — data-enabled practices, analytics-driven practices, and integrated customer intelligence — each governed by a different innovation logic: adopt–implement–refine, then explore–experiment–scale, then reframe–integrate–institutionalize. Different mixes of internal and external change agents drove each phase.

**Why it matters:** Most firms treat AI and analytics adoption as a technology purchase; this case suggests it is a sequence of organizational practice changes that must be built in order, with capabilities accumulating rather than arriving at once.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** B2B and industrial marketers can use the three-phase model as a diagnostic: identify which phase your organization is actually in, and resist jumping to enterprise-wide customer intelligence before the basic data-enabled and experimental layers have been institutionalized — but treat it as a hypothesis to test against your own firm, since it comes from a single company.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Limited direct relevance for consumers, beyond showing that the "AI-powered" personalization many companies promise usually sits on years of unglamorous internal plumbing work that most firms haven't finished.

**Caveats:** Single firm, single country, single sector (European manufacturing SME); qualitative case study with no quantitative outcome measures, so the claimed link to stronger marketing capabilities is interpretive rather than tested; findings may not generalize to large firms or consumer categories.

### 5. What makes a voice assistant feel "smart": more things to control, fewer steps to do it

**Journal:** *Marketing Intelligence & Planning* | **Published:** 2026-08-28
**Work ID:** W7204460029 | **DOI:** 10.1108/mip-10-2025-0782

**The study:** Researchers combined field data from 481 Amazon Alexa skills, more than 47,000 user reviews, expert coding and two user surveys to test what drives perceptions of a voice skill's intelligence. Skills that let users control a wider variety of product functions (controllability variety) were rated as more intelligent, while skills that made control more complicated (controllability complexity) were penalized. Perceived intelligence was the main driver of overall user evaluations. The penalty for complexity was worse when the skill was invoked under a licensed brand name rather than a co-branded one.

**Why it matters:** Voice and agentic AI products are judged less on underlying technical capability than on the felt experience of control, and a brand that puts its name alone on the invocation absorbs more of the blame when the interaction is clunky.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For brands building voice skills, in-car assistants or AI agents: audit the breadth of functions a user can actually reach by voice versus the number of steps required, and consider whether a co-branding arrangement with the platform spreads reputational risk when the experience gets friction-heavy. These are observational associations across one platform's skills, so treat them as hypotheses to test in your own product analytics.
- **For general readers and consumers:** When a smart speaker feels "dumb," the issue is often not the AI's intelligence but how many hoops the designer makes you jump through — and reviews show users attribute that frustration to whichever brand's name they said out loud.

**Caveats:** Single platform (Amazon Alexa) and a correlational field-data design; user surveys supplement but do not establish causality; expert coding introduces judgment calls.

### 6. PR professionals describe generative AI as "parasitic": 29 interviews on what the tools are doing to their jobs

**Journal:** *Journal of Communication Management* | **Published:** 2026-10-07
**Work ID:** W7220835940 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jcom-01-2026-0063

**The study:** Researchers conducted 29 semi-structured in-depth interviews with public relations practitioners about their experience of adopting generative AI in daily communication work. Six themes of vulnerability emerged: technological, professional value, emotional, relational, governance and developmental. Rather than a straightforward productivity story, practitioners described "strategic interruptions" — uncertainty about their own worth, eroded client relationships and ethical guidelines that lag the tools.

**Why it matters:** Agency and in-house communications leaders rolling out generative AI are managing a workforce well-being and professional-identity problem, not just a software deployment, and current industry ethics codes do not address it.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For communications and marketing leaders: ask what your AI rollout is doing to junior staff development and to the perceived value of craft work, and whether your ethics policy covers disclosure, client relationships and emotional load — not just accuracy and plagiarism.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The people writing the press releases and pitches you read are themselves unsure how much of their work is now machine-generated — and they worry their clients will notice before their employers do.

**Caveats:** Qualitative interview study with a small sample (N=29); self-reported experiences, not measured outcomes; findings describe perceptions within one professional field and may not generalize across markets or seniority levels.

### 7. Poetic AI text needs emotional images; plain AI text needs logical ones

**Journal:** *Service Industries Journal* | **Published:** 2026-09-30
**Work ID:** W7214946351 | **DOI:** 10.1080/02642069.2026.2740556

**The study:** Across three experiments plus sentiment analysis of platform data, researchers tested how the modality of generative AI output affects what users contribute back on video platforms. AI-generated text alone shifted user input through two competing routes — a sense of novelty that encouraged engagement and cynicism that discouraged it. In multimodal settings, the effect depended on cross-modal congruence: ordinary, straightforward text paired best with logical, informational images, while poetic or expressive text paired best with emotional imagery.

**Why it matters:** As platforms bolt generative AI into captions, recommendations and prompts, mismatched tone between AI copy and accompanying visuals may quietly suppress the user contributions those features are meant to stimulate.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For social and content teams using AI-generated copy: treat text style and image style as a matched pair rather than independent decisions, and A/B test "poetic plus emotional" against "plain plus informational" before assuming more creative AI writing lifts engagement. These are controlled experiments, so effect sizes in a live feed may differ.
- **For general readers and consumers:** There's a reason some AI-written captions feel off: when the words strain for poetry but the picture is a plain product shot, people read it as hollow rather than charming — and cynicism, the study finds, is a real and measurable reaction to AI output.

**Caveats:** Experimental designs with sample sizes and participant pools not specified in the abstract; platform context is narrow (video platforms); effects measured on user input behavior in a study setting rather than long-run retention.

### 8. Your sentiment score may depend mostly on which word list you happened to pick

**Journal:** *Marketing Letters* | **Published:** 2026-08-13
**Work ID:** W7202341073 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11002-026-09835-4

**The study:** Researchers examined widely used lexicon-based text analysis practices and replicated prior findings on the perceived usefulness of consumer reviews across several commonly adopted sentiment dictionaries. Results shifted depending on which lexicon was chosen and on the common practice of subtracting negative from positive word counts to produce a single sentiment score. They provide guidelines — compare lexicon coverage rates, limit when difference scores are appropriate — and show that lexicons can serve as contextual anchors in few-shot prompting of generative AI models.

**Why it matters:** A great deal of commercial voice-of-customer, social listening and review analytics rests on exactly these techniques, meaning two vendors analyzing the same reviews can reach contradictory conclusions for purely technical reasons.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For insight and analytics leaders: ask your social listening or review-analysis vendor which lexicon they use, what share of words it actually covers in your category, and whether the headline "sentiment score" is a net difference — then test whether conclusions survive a swap to a different dictionary or a GenAI-assisted approach.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Limited direct relevance for general consumers, beyond a reason to be skeptical when a brand or news story cites a precise "sentiment" figure about online opinion.

**Caveats:** Methodological paper demonstrated on consumer review data rather than new behavioral evidence; a replication exercise in a single data context; conclusions about GenAI few-shot prompting are illustrative rather than benchmarked at scale.

### 9. Communicators Say Generative AI Saves Them Time — and Misrepresents the People They Speak For

**Journal:** *Public Relations Review* | **Published:** 2026-08-24
**Work ID:** W7204089014 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.pubrev.2026.102759

**The study:** Researchers interviewed 22 social change communicators — public relations and advocacy professionals whose work depends on building relationships with communities — about how they actually use generative AI day to day. Participants reported using it to draft messages, handle routine tasks and act as a "thought partner," with efficiency named as the dominant benefit. But the same practitioners described recurring problems: factual inaccuracy and, more pointedly, outputs that flattened or misrepresented the publics they advocate for. The authors propose a "participatory (AI) public relations" approach that keeps affected communities in the loop rather than letting the model speak for them.

**Why it matters:** It puts a specific, nameable risk on the efficiency case for generative AI in communications: when a model drafts copy about a community, it draws on training data rather than that community, and the practitioners closest to the work can see the difference.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If your agency or in-house team is using generative AI for audience-facing copy, test whether the outputs survive review by someone from the audience being described — these interviews suggest the failure mode is not obvious factual error but subtle misrepresentation that only insiders catch. Worth building a human checkpoint into the workflow precisely where AI feels most time-saving.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The brand and nonprofit messages addressed to you about your community are increasingly drafted by a machine that has never met anyone in it — and the people sending them are already uneasy about how that lands.

**Caveats:** Small qualitative sample (22 interviews) from a single professional niche; self-reported practice and perception, not measured output quality or audience response; findings are not statistically generalizable.

### 10. Across 22 Countries, Fear of AI Misinformation Tracks With Feeling Worse Off Economically

**Journal:** *Journal of Macromarketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-07
**Work ID:** W7220834813 | **DOI:** 10.1177/02761467261490922

**The study:** Using multilevel analysis of survey data spanning 22 nations, researchers tested whether consumers' perceived threat from AI-driven misinformation and their perceived "automation inequality" — the sense that AI's economic gains are unevenly allocated — are linked to lower confidence in democratic performance and, in turn, to a weaker sense of personal economic wellbeing. The institutional-confidence pathway was supported, and national "AI interpretive climates" — shared collective beliefs about AI in a country — amplified how strongly those threat perceptions translated into skepticism or optimism. The framing treats AI not as a firm-level tool but as market infrastructure that shapes how exchange is coordinated.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the reputational risk of AI isn't contained to individual brands: consumer sentiment about AI operates at a societal level and spills into how people feel about institutions and their own prospects, which sets the backdrop for every AI-powered product launch.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Consider that your market's baseline attitude toward AI may be a national-climate variable, not a brand variable — the same AI feature announcement may read as progress in one country and as threat in another. Worth tracking country-level AI sentiment alongside brand trackers before rolling out AI-branded features globally.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Unease about AI-generated misinformation and about who captures automation's gains appears to bleed into something more personal: how secure people feel about their own finances.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey data, so associations cannot establish causation; relies on perceived rather than objective measures of misinformation, inequality and wellbeing; multilevel country comparisons across 22 nations can mask within-country variation.

### 11. Europe's AI Rulebooks Are Not One Rulebook: A Three-Tier Map for Global Campaign Teams

**Journal:** *Journal of Global Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-10-10
**Work ID:** W7221335140 | **DOI:** 10.1080/08911762.2026.2739546

**The study:** Researchers conducted a comparative content analysis of the National AI Agendas of every EU member state, treating the bloc as a regulatory laboratory whose standards spread internationally via the "Brussels Effect." They derive an AI Governance Maturity Model that separates Analytical AI — insight and forecasting that inform marketing judgment — from Decisional AI, generative systems embedded directly in marketing operations such as automated customer interactions and dynamic pricing. Member states sorted into three clusters — AI Pioneers, Adopters and Followers — each presenting a different mix of opportunity and constraint for multinationals.

**Why it matters:** Multinational marketers often treat "EU AI rules" as a single compliance item; this mapping suggests the practical constraints on deploying generative systems in live marketing operations differ materially by member state.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** The Analytical versus Decisional distinction is the useful operational line: AI that informs a human marketer's decision sits in a different risk tier than AI that sets prices or talks to customers unsupervised. Worth auditing your AI stack against that split, market by market, before scaling generative content or dynamic pricing across Europe.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Whether an airline's prices or a retailer's chatbot responses are set by a machine with no human in the loop may depend on which European country you happen to be buying from.

**Caveats:** Analysis of policy documents rather than observed firm behavior or enforcement outcomes; EU-only; the clusters are an interpretive classification, not a validated measurement; no evidence yet on whether governance maturity actually changes marketing performance.

### 12. Giving Generative AI to Expert Creators Can Backfire — a Model Says Platforms Should Sometimes Hold Back

**Journal:** *Internet Research* | **Published:** 2026-09-28
**Work ID:** W7214651072 | **DOI:** 10.1108/intr-10-2025-1768

**The study:** Using a calibrated two-sided market model of a subscription content platform, researchers modeled what happens when generative AI is offered to creators who differ in skill. Novices gain, but experts can suffer an Einstellung or "expertise-fixation" effect — AI suggestions pull skilled creators toward familiar, lower-value solutions, creating cognitive friction. The analysis derives cost thresholds: cheap deployment can lift platforms, creators and consumers together, while costly deployment can cut profits and welfare by amplifying expert-side friction. Maximizing AI adoption is not optimal even when AI raises average productivity.

**Why it matters:** It gives a formal argument for strategic restraint on AI rollouts: more content supply at higher average productivity can still mean worse outcomes if your most valuable contributors disengage or produce more conventional work.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Before a blanket AI rollout to creators, contributors or even internal teams, segment by expertise and watch whether your best people's output becomes more generic — the model predicts the damage shows up on the expert side, not the novice side. An opt-in or workflow-compatible deployment may outperform a universal one.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If the shows, newsletters and channels you subscribe to start feeling blander even as there is more of them, the economics here offer an explanation: AI tools lift the floor while potentially nudging the most skilled creators toward the obvious choice.

**Caveats:** This is an analytical modeling paper, not an empirical study — no creators or consumers were observed; creator quality is treated as exogenous and only one platform is modeled; the Einstellung effect is assumed as a parameter rather than measured in this setting.

### 13. When AI streamers should sell with facts — and when they should gush

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Behaviour* | **Published:** 2026-10-04
**Work ID:** W7219885583 | **DOI:** 10.1002/cb.70255

**The study:** Across three scenario-based experiments, researchers tested how AI-powered virtual streamers in live-shopping should talk, pairing two language styles (informational vs. emotional) with two product types (search goods whose attributes can be verified up front, like a phone spec sheet, vs. experience goods you have to try, like skincare or food). Informational language worked best for search products because it reduced uncertainty about the product itself; emotional language worked best for experience products because it reduced uncertainty about the streamer. The advantage of emotional language for experience goods strengthened in busy, high-activity live rooms and faded in quiet ones.

**Why it matters:** Virtual streamers are already scaled across Chinese and increasingly global live-commerce platforms, and the script is the one variable brands fully control — this says the same avatar should sound different depending on what it is selling and how crowded the room is.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** A testable hypothesis for anyone deploying synthetic hosts or AI-generated product video: A/B the tone of the script by product category rather than settling on one brand voice, and check whether warmth only pays off when the chat is lively. These were scenario experiments, so treat it as a hypothesis to validate against real live-stream conversion data.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It's a useful lens on your own susceptibility: when a product is hard to judge in advance, enthusiasm from the seller functions as a substitute for evidence — and an AI avatar can perform that enthusiasm on demand without feeling any of it.

**Caveats:** Scenario-based experiments with stated purchase intention rather than real purchases; no sample sizes or participant details reported in the abstract; single-context (live-streaming e-commerce) and likely a single national sample.

### 14. Carbon labels on food move only the people who already care

**Journal:** *Food Quality and Preference* | **Published:** 2026-10-05
**Work ID:** W7220360598 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.foodqual.2026.106137

**The study:** A randomized online experiment compared three ways of presenting the carbon footprint of food: a personalized total of the participant's own emissions, a generic ranking of food categories, and both together. All three formats cut projected emissions on average, with the simple ordinal ranking producing the largest point estimate. But the effects were sharply split: people already concerned about climate change reduced projected emissions and revised their self-assessments toward the displayed numbers, while the unconcerned barely budged — and the gap was largely explained by whether they trusted the information.

**Why it matters:** Retailers and food brands rolling out carbon labels may be measuring success on an average that hides the real story: the labels are preaching to the converted, and trust, not comprehension, is the binding constraint.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Before investing in complex personalized footprint calculators, test whether a plain category ranking does as much or more work — and treat source credibility as the design problem, since skeptics discount the numbers rather than misunderstand them.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Most of us badly underestimate the emissions in our own groceries, and seeing a number does correct that belief — but only if we already believe the person showing it to us.

**Caveats:** Online experiment measuring projected/intended dietary choices rather than observed purchases; short time horizon; heterogeneity analysis by climate attitudes is correlational, and the trust mechanism is an association rather than an established cause.

### 15. Gen Z bonds with AI game characters — and that bond spills into real-world luxury spending

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-27
**Work ID:** W7204474051 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jcm-05-2025-7936

**The study:** Researchers surveyed 348 actual Gen Z metaverse platform users in the US (from 400 approached) and modeled how parasocial relationships with AI-controlled non-player characters affect brand behavior, separating the NPCs' emotional intelligence from their cognitive intelligence. Both forms of NPC intelligence fed a sense of social connectedness in the virtual world, which predicted preference for digital luxury NFT brands. Crucially, that connectedness predicted not only in-world purchase intentions but real-world luxury purchase intentions and social-media brand endorsement — a virtual-to-real spillover.

**Why it matters:** It suggests the AI characters populating virtual worlds and games are not set dressing but potential relationship-building assets, and that digital and physical luxury lines may need distinct strategies rather than one shared campaign.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For brands weighing in-game and virtual-world placements, the question to test is whether emotionally responsive AI characters — not just branded items — drive affinity, and whether that affinity converts offline. Note this is a cross-sectional survey of intentions, so a controlled pilot with tracked conversion is the next step, not a budget commitment.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A reminder of how porous the line has become between playing and buying: feeling socially connected inside a virtual world appears to carry over into what young consumers want to own in the real one.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey, so it cannot establish causation; measures stated purchase intentions rather than observed behavior; single country (US) and a single generational cohort; results come from a PLS-SEM path model.

### 16. The brand chatbot that acts like it loves you back — and structurally cannot

**Journal:** *Journal of Advertising* | **Published:** 2026-10-02
**Work ID:** W7215774667 | **DOI:** 10.1080/00913367.2026.2735425

**The study:** This is a conceptual paper in the Journal of Advertising proposing 'human–AI asymmetric relationship theory' (HAART) to describe consumer–brand relationships mediated by generative AI. Its core argument: AI agents convincingly perform relational behaviors — linguistic, paralinguistic and interactional cues that simulate care and engagement — but cannot experience relational benefits, costs or commitment, so perceived mutuality can be high while actual reciprocity is zero. The authors lay out six propositions covering upsides (enhanced support) and downsides (consumer vulnerability, asymmetries in dependence, risk and control).

**Why it matters:** As brands replace campaigns with conversational agents, this reframes the central risk: the better the AI performs intimacy, the greater the regulatory and reputational exposure when consumers discover the relationship was one-sided.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** A framework worth stress-testing against your own AI touchpoints: where does your agent perform warmth it cannot back up, and what happens to trust when a customer realizes it? Note that this is theory-building with no new data — the propositions are hypotheses for research and internal audit, not validated effects.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Explains the uncanny pull of chatbots that remember your name and ask how your week went: the emotional cues are real, the emotion isn't, and the dependence runs in only one direction.

**Caveats:** Purely conceptual — no empirical evidence, no sample; the six propositions are untested.

## Research Trends & Continuity

**Convergent findings**
- **Warmth from a machine is a setting, not a default — and the moderator keeps being context.** "Robots that show feelings win customers — unless the service involves something private" (W7215001822) and "When AI streamers should sell with facts — and when they should gush" (W7219885583) land squarely on the previous digest's conditional-humanness thread: "Chatbots that flatter you — or brag about themselves — get you to reveal more" (W7203665707) found flattery paid in hedonic settings and self-promotion in utilitarian ones, while "Give your voice assistant an accent and people warm to it" (W7214067855) carried its own boundary condition where seeming human is unwanted. Privacy sensitivity and product type are now two named switches on the same dial.
- **Transparency and emotional cues both backfire when the system already looks like it knows too much.** The privacy-sensitivity reversal in W7215001822 is the service-robot analogue of "The privacy disclosure paradox" (W7214342499): salience of data exposure, not the cue itself, is what flips the sign.
- **The employee side of AI keeps producing bad news that productivity dashboards miss.** "The hidden cost of heavy AI use at work: employees start cutting corners" (W7220733006) converges with "AI wrote more code. Then the bottleneck moved to the humans reviewing it." (W4400488941) and with "Employees trust AI more when their boss lets them complain about it" (W7201971570): throughput rises while quality, coordination and morale absorb the cost.
- **Analytics methods are only as good as their validation.** "Your sentiment score may depend mostly on which word list you happened to pick" (W7202341073) extends the instrument-skepticism thread opened by "'Silicon respondents' aren't interchangeable" (W7214279921) and BRIDGE (W7210288362) — in all three, the apparent finding moves when the tooling moves.

**Advances**
- **From "AI acceptance is context-dependent" to a measure of it.** "Why 'do you trust AI?' is the wrong question to ask shoppers" (W7220664143) gives an eight-dimension instrument that separates functional from social acceptance — the diagnostic the previous digest's positioning-dependent results implied but could not supply ("'Designed by AI' cuts both ways in fashion," W7214017195).
- **A theory for the extraction findings.** "The brand chatbot that acts like it loves you back — and structurally cannot" (W7215774667) names the asymmetry that W7203665707 demonstrated empirically: perceived mutuality can be high while reciprocity is zero.
- **Voice interfaces, second look.** "What makes a voice assistant feel 'smart'" (W7204460029) advances W7214067855 by moving the lever from vocal humanness to felt control breadth — and adds a branding twist, that a solo licensed brand name absorbs more blame for friction than a co-branded one.

**Counterpoints**
- **Who actually gains from AI, novices or experts?** "Giving Generative AI to Expert Creators Can Backfire" (W7214651072) models experts losing ground to expertise-fixation while novices gain — the mirror image of the Copilot field evidence, where gains skewed toward core contributors and peripheral developers created extra work (W4400488941). Modeling versus observational data, but the tension is real and worth tracking.
- **Algorithmic reach versus relational pull, again — with a different answer.** The previous digest's RED study found algorithmic recommendation did not drive purchase (W7214221690); "Gen Z bonds with AI game characters" (W7204474051) finds machine-generated characters do build the social connectedness that predicts buying, including offline. Relationship beats recommendation in both, but here the relationship partner is itself synthetic.

**New themes**
- **AI as market infrastructure and regulated terrain.** "Across 22 Countries, Fear of AI Misinformation Tracks With Feeling Worse Off Economically" (W7220834813) and "Europe's AI Rulebooks Are Not One Rulebook" (W7221335140) lift the topic from interface design to national climates and compliance geography — absent from the previous run.
- **Professional identity in communications work.** "PR professionals describe generative AI as 'parasitic'" (W7220835940) and "Communicators Say Generative AI Saves Them Time — and Misrepresents the People They Speak For" (W7204089014) open a qualitative workforce strand with no prior-run antecedent.
- **Organizational sequencing of data capability** ("How a manufacturer actually became data-driven," W7213979252) and **cross-modal congruence of AI output** ("Poetic AI text needs emotional images," W7214946351) are both new.
- **Outlier:** "Carbon labels on food move only the people who already care" (W7220360598) is not an AI study and has no previous-digest counterpart, though its trust-not-comprehension mechanism rhymes with the disclosure work above.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Four studies in this batch converge on an uncomfortable asymmetry — generative AI lifts the floor while quietly eroding the ceiling. "The hidden cost of heavy AI use at work" (W7220733006) finds corner-cutting under AI-driven performance pressure sharpest among high-status employees; "Giving Generative AI to Expert Creators Can Backfire" (W7214651072) models skilled creators pulled toward familiar, lower-value solutions while novices gain; "PR professionals describe generative AI as 'parasitic'" (W7220835940) documents eroded professional value and stalled junior development; and "Communicators Say Generative AI Saves Them Time — and Misrepresents the People They Speak For" (W7204089014) shows the failures that matter are subtle misrepresentations only an expert insider can catch.

**Why pitch this now:** Agencies, in-house teams and content platforms are entering 2026 budget cycles having sold AI on headcount leverage, just as the first serious evidence arrives that the leverage comes disproportionately out of the senior and specialist work that firms bill as their differentiator.

**Angle:** A reported feature on what happens to expertise itself when AI is deployed universally rather than selectively — who still gets to do hard work, what the "good enough" threshold does to craft, and why deliberate restraint may outperform maximum adoption. Written for agency leaders, communications and creative directors, and readers interested in the future of skilled work.

**Potential headlines:**
- Senior staff cut more corners after AI arrives, four new studies suggest
- AI is helping the beginners. It's the experts who are getting blander.
- What your agency sells if the machine makes everyone average

**Potential outlets:**
- **Marketing Week** — its readership is in-house and agency leadership currently arguing over whether AI frees craft time or hollows it out, and this gives them evidence on which tier of staff absorbs the damage.
- **PRWeek** — two of the four studies are interviews with PR and advocacy practitioners describing exactly the professional-value erosion its readers are debating in public.
- **MIT Sloan Management Review** — the "maximizing adoption is not optimal" modeling result is squarely its register: a management argument for selective, expertise-segmented deployment.
- **The Atlantic** (Work/Business) — runs well-reported pieces on skill, status and what automation does to the meaning of a profession, which is the general-reader version of this thread.
