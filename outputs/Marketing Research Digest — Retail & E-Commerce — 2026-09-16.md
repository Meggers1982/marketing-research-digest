### 1. Store brands pay off less on their own shelf space than at the negotiating table

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing Research* | **Published:** 2026-08-21
**Work ID:** W7203936299 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00222437261483522

**The study:** Economists exploited a natural experiment in grocery retail — a drug patent expiration that coincided with waves of private-label entry — and built a structural model of consumer demand plus bilateral bargaining between retailers and manufacturers, using rare data identifying who actually manufactures each private-label product. Private-label entry raised retailer profits, but very unevenly: gains were largest where the retailer held a strong disagreement payoff (a credible walk-away position) against its private-label supplier. Crucially, much of the profit came indirectly, via better terms extracted from national brands, and the retailer's own brand equity shaped how much the store brand earned.

**Why it matters:** It reframes private label from a margin play to a bargaining weapon, and shows the value depends on the supply contract behind the product as much as on shopper demand.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Retail and category leaders should audit private-label sourcing through a bargaining lens: how replaceable is the contract manufacturer, and what does the store brand's mere existence buy in annual negotiations with national brands? The paper suggests the answer may matter more to profit than the private label's own sell-through.
- **For general readers and consumers:** When the store brand appears next to the name brand, part of its job is invisible to shoppers — it gives the supermarket leverage to squeeze a better wholesale price on the branded product you might buy instead.

**Caveats:** Grocery data from a single market setting built around one patent-expiration event; results come from a structural model with assumptions about how bargaining works, not from observed contract terms.

### 2. Who takes the salesperson's advice? Richer shoppers, consistently

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Psychology* | **Published:** 2026-09-10
**Work ID:** W7212164165 | **DOI:** 10.1002/jcpy.70038

**The study:** Across five studies (plus three more in an appendix), including analysis of the World Values Survey and a range of advice contexts, researchers found that higher-socioeconomic-status consumers were consistently more likely to follow advice from marketplace agents such as salespeople and advisers. The mechanism was how trust judgments are formed: high-SES consumers relied on sources that led them to see advisers as generally more trustworthy. Two interventions narrowed the gap — supplying an external trustworthiness cue (such as third-party credentials) and redirecting attention to the shopper's own needs and preferences.

**Why it matters:** Advice is a major sales lever in categories from finance to home improvement, and it appears to land unevenly across the income spectrum — a commercial problem and a consumer-welfare problem at once.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If advisory selling converts better with affluent customers, brands serving lower-income segments could test explicit, verifiable trust signals — certifications, reviews, transparent incentives — rather than relying on rapport, and see whether recommendation uptake moves.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Skepticism toward salespeople isn't irrational, but it has a cost: shoppers who tune out advice may end up with worse-fitting products. The research points to a middle path — judge the recommendation against your own needs rather than judging the person.

**Caveats:** Mostly online experiments alongside cross-sectional survey data; several contexts are hypothetical advice scenarios rather than observed purchases, and the World Values Survey component cannot establish causation.

### 3. "Sustainable" beats "green" on the label — because it hints the product will last

**Journal:** *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* | **Published:** 2026-09-04
**Work ID:** W7208768688 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11747-026-01194-w

**The study:** A series of experiments tested two unregulated eco-labels head to head and found that calling a product "sustainable" raised its choice share against an unlabeled conventional alternative more than calling it "green" did. The reason is semantic: "sustainable" carries associations of longevity, prompting consumers to infer a more careful design process and a more durable product. The advantage showed up most strongly when shoppers cared about how long the product would last.

**Why it matters:** Two words treated as interchangeable in packaging and ad copy produce measurably different consumer inferences — and one of them makes a durability promise the product may not keep.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brands should A/B test eco-terminology by category rather than defaulting to house style: "sustainable" may lift choice in durables, but it also sets a durability expectation that invites backlash if the product fails, which is where policymakers' greenwashing concerns bite.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The eco-words on packaging are largely unregulated, and they don't mean the same thing in your head. "Sustainable" quietly signals "built to last" — a claim about the product's lifespan that the label itself doesn't actually guarantee.

**Caveats:** Experimental choice tasks rather than real purchases; label effects were tested in selected product categories and the abstract does not report sample sizes or populations.

### 4. Hiring an ex-official to your board measurably improves government sales — except when procurement is truly competitive

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-06
**Work ID:** W7210266064 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00222429261489543

**The study:** Researchers assembled multisource panel data on 1,731 publicly traded US firms selling to government between 2005 and 2021 and tracked what happened when former government officials joined their boards. These "revolving door" directors were associated with significantly better customer acquisition, retention and cross-selling in government markets, with effects running through both connections and expertise for acquisition, but through connections alone for retention and cross-selling. The payoff shrank when government customers preferred competitive procurement processes, and grew when those customers faced more bid protests.

**Why it matters:** It quantifies a governance choice that is usually debated in ethical terms as a concrete customer-relationship asset — and identifies the procurement conditions under which it stops working.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** B2G firms weighing board appointments now have evidence on where the return sits: relationship continuity and cross-sell rather than one-off wins, and mainly in buying environments with procurement friction. Worth pairing with a hard look at the reputational and compliance exposure.
- **For general readers and consumers:** This is a rare dollar-value read on the revolving door: public data suggest that former officials on corporate boards help those companies keep and expand government contracts, and that open competition is what blunts the advantage.

**Caveats:** Observational secondary panel data from a single country; associations may reflect which firms choose to recruit such directors rather than a causal effect of the appointment.

### 5. Return fraud isn't driven by lenient policies — it's driven by peer norms and money pressure

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-09-09
**Work ID:** W7212072927 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jcm-07-2025-8088

**The study:** A sequential mixed-methods study of young Indian online shoppers — a survey of 417 Gen Y and Gen Z consumers followed by 30 semi-structured interviews — tested the criminology-derived "fraud diamond" against opportunistic returns. Social norms, financial pressure and trait cynicism predicted opportunistic return behavior, while policy leniency and knowledge of how return systems work did not. Responsive customer service weakened the link between social norms and opportunistic returns, and marginally weakened the financial-pressure link. Interviews pointed to situational triggers: feeling monitored, digital reputation signals, product type, return fees and explicit anti-wardrobing rules.

**Why it matters:** Retailers typically fight returns abuse by tightening policy, but this evidence suggests generous policies aren't the trigger — social permission and perceived anonymity are, which implies different countermeasures.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Before cutting return windows and risking conversion, e-commerce teams could test softer levers the study implicates: visible monitoring cues, account-level return signals, explicit anti-wardrobing language and faster, more responsive service contact.
- **For general readers and consumers:** "Wardrobing" and other free-trial-by-return habits spread mostly because people believe everyone does it. The study suggests the feeling that a retailer is paying attention does more to curb it than the fine print in the returns policy.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional survey (no causal inference) plus interviews; self-reported unethical behavior is prone to underreporting; sample limited to Gen Y and Gen Z consumers in India.

### 6. Plus-size mannequins next to standard ones lifted brand attitudes — and willingness to pay

**Journal:** *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science* | **Published:** 2026-08-19
**Work ID:** W7203777252 | **DOI:** 10.1007/s11747-026-01197-7

**The study:** Across seven studies, researchers tested how shoppers respond to store displays that mix traditional-size and plus-size mannequins versus displays using a single body type. Mixed-body displays produced more favorable brand attitudes and higher willingness to pay, working through two routes: a personal one (the brand feels more relevant, strengthening self-brand connection) and a social one (the brand is seen as more inclusive). The effect faded for luxury brands, was weaker among consumers with lower BMI, and reappeared when the diversity was in mannequin skin tone rather than body size.

**Why it matters:** Body-diverse merchandising is often framed as a values statement or a reputational hedge; this evidence suggests it can also move commercial metrics, including what shoppers say they will pay.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Visual merchandising is a cheap, testable variable: retailers could run split tests of mixed-mannequin windows against single-body windows in matched stores and watch conversion and basket value, rather than assuming the lab lift transfers to the shop floor. The luxury exception is the strategic warning — inclusivity cues may sit awkwardly with exclusivity positioning.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The finding hints that shoppers read store fixtures as signals about who a brand is for — and that seeing a body like yours in a window changes how personally relevant the brand feels, not just how ethical it seems.

**Caveats:** Multi-study designs in this literature typically rely heavily on online panels and hypothetical willingness-to-pay measures rather than observed purchases; the abstract does not specify samples, sizes, or countries, and boundary conditions (luxury, BMI) mean effects are not universal.

### 7. Customers who return groceries then spend more — unless the retailer looks at fault

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing* | **Published:** 2026-09-01
**Work ID:** W7208808437 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretai.2026.08.008

**The study:** Using matched consumer records from a South Korean grocery retailer, researchers examined spending after a product return. Returning something was followed by a substantial increase in spending, and the lift was larger for variety-seeking shoppers and those with smaller baskets — consistent with the return option acting as a risk reducer that encourages trying new products. But the lift shrank when the returned item was perishable and had been held at home only briefly, cases where shoppers plausibly blame the retailer for a bad product; the first return carried over, while repeated returns showed negative accumulation.

**Why it matters:** It reframes returns from pure cost center to a memory-building reassurance mechanism — but only when the return isn't evidence that the retailer sold something defective.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Retailers weighing tighter return rules could separate two very different events in their data: discretionary returns (which appear to unlock exploration and bigger baskets) and quality-failure returns (which don't, and may signal a supply problem). Worth testing whether actively reminding shoppers the return option exists produces the same spending lift as actually using it.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A generous returns desk may quietly change how you shop — making you likelier to gamble on an unfamiliar product. Conversely, bringing back spoiled produce doesn't leave you reassured; it leaves you doubting the store.

**Caveats:** Single grocery retailer in one country; observational transaction data with matching rather than a randomized experiment, so causal claims rest on the matching design; mechanisms (risk reduction, blame attribution) are inferred rather than directly measured.

### 8. Why some shoppers game the system: it's not greed, it's wanting to feel clever

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing Research* | **Published:** 2026-08-03
**Work ID:** W7172339845 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00222437261477110

**The study:** Nine studies (plus two supplementary) combining archival, correlational and experimental data examined why consumers take more than fair exchange warrants — grabbing extra samples, returning used items, exploiting policy loopholes. The driver the authors identify is power distance belief: consumers who accept societal hierarchy, including firms sitting above them in it, develop a need to feel smart relative to firms, which pushes them toward loophole-exploiting behavior. Higher-status consumers felt this need more strongly. Reminders of surveillance reduced opportunism, while reminders of the consumer's relationship with the brand paradoxically licensed it.

**Why it matters:** Opportunism costs retailers real money, and the study suggests the standard loyalty-and-relationship playbook may make it worse rather than better.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Test the counterintuitive part: loyalty messaging and relationship framing at the point of a return or promotion may increase abuse, while light, non-accusatory cues that a policy is monitored may curb it. Any such test needs care — surveillance cues carry their own brand risk.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The psychology of the person who returns worn shoes or pockets a fistful of free samples is less about saving money than about a small win against a big company — and it's often the better-off customer doing it.

**Caveats:** Mix of archival and experimental evidence, but the abstract does not report sample sizes, countries, or whether experiments used online panels; many measures in this literature are scenario-based rather than observed transgressions; power distance belief effects can be culture-specific.

### 9. AI-redesigned coffee packs won a shelf test — but only barely, and not for everyone

**Journal:** *International Journal of Retail & Distribution Management* | **Published:** 2026-09-03
**Work ID:** W7207745459 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ijrdm-06-2026-0473

**The study:** In a within-subject real-shelf experiment, 64 participants (after biometric quality exclusions from 72) viewed eight physical coffee packages side by side: four actual market designs and four AI-redesigned counterparts, with positions rotated via Latin Square. Mobile eye-tracking showed the AI-redesigned packs got faster visual entry and better evaluations, and they took 55.5% of the 256 consequential take-home choices versus 44.5% for the originals. The advantage held in clustered and fixed-effects choice models, but the largest single group of participants split their picks evenly — there was no universal AI superiority.

**Why it matters:** It is one of the few tests of generative-AI design work as a point-of-purchase intervention with real products and a real take-home choice, rather than a digital mock-up and an intention rating.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** AI packaging concepts are worth shelf-testing rather than screen-testing: the mechanism here was attention capture and processing fluency, so the useful question is whether a redesign wins the first glance, not whether it looks better in a deck. A roughly 11-point choice edge in a lab shelf is a hypothesis to validate in store, not a mandate to redesign a portfolio.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Package design works on you before you consciously evaluate anything — the eye-tracking shows which packs got looked at first, and those were the ones more likely to come home.

**Caveats:** Small sample (N=64) and a single category (coffee); lab shelf rather than a real store with prices, promotions and time pressure; effect heterogeneous, with the biggest participant segment showing no preference; country and panel recruitment not specified.

### 10. A bad delivery hurts less when it's not your van — but a great one only pays off if it is

**Journal:** *Journal of Service Research* | **Published:** 2026-08-09
**Work ID:** W7202034360 | **DOI:** 10.1177/10946705261464334

**The study:** Researchers analyzed more than 35,000 online retail reviews and ran four experiments plus a single-paper meta-analysis to see how the "moment of delivery" shapes word of mouth. Using a third-party courier buffered retailers from the damage of a botched delivery — customers blamed the courier — but the upside of an excellent delivery accrued only to established retailers that handled the last mile themselves. Pre-emptive "inoculation" messages warning customers that delivery hiccups can happen also reduced the word-of-mouth damage from failed deliveries.

**Why it matters:** Last-mile delivery is often the only human touchpoint in an e-commerce journey, and this shows the in-house-versus-outsourced decision is not just a cost question but a reputation-risk trade-off.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Retailers weighing vertical integration of delivery should consider that owning the van concentrates both the credit and the blame; the experiments suggest cheap communication fixes — setting expectations before dispatch — may be worth A/B testing against expensive fleet investment.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Shoppers apparently do keep score of who dropped the parcel: people are more forgiving of a retailer when a third-party courier bungles the handoff, which is partly why so many brands are happy to keep delivery at arm's length.

**Caveats:** Experimental studies likely used online panels with hypothetical delivery scenarios rather than real purchases; the review analysis is observational and the retailer/platform context is limited.

### 11. AR and VR don't sell in one way: immersion wins browsers, vividness closes sales

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing* | **Published:** 2026-08-01
**Work ID:** W7203672599 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretai.2026.08.003

**The study:** A meta-analysis pooled 126 independent samples (N=36,386) on augmented and virtual reality in online retail, using meta-analytic structural equation modeling and hierarchical linear models, supplemented by three original experiments. Four AR/VR attributes mapped onto different stages of the customer journey: immersion had the strongest link to prepurchase outcomes, vividness to purchase, and presence and interactivity to post-purchase outcomes. Hedonic and utilitarian gratifications explained much of the effect, with moderators at the customer, product, retailer, interface and time level.

**Why it matters:** Retailers have poured money into AR try-on and VR showrooms without clear guidance on what to optimize; this says the right design depends on whether you're trying to attract, convert, or retain.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Rather than buying "an AR experience," brand teams could spec features against a specific funnel objective — richer sensory detail if conversion is the bottleneck, deeper immersion if discovery is — and use the moderator findings to identify which product categories justify the spend.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For shoppers, this explains why virtual try-on sometimes feels genuinely useful and sometimes like a gimmick: the features that make a product feel real serve different purposes, and not every category benefits.

**Caveats:** Meta-analysis inherits the limits of its source studies, many of which are lab or panel experiments with hypothetical purchases; effects are correlational at the meta level, though three experiments add causal support.

### 12. How a customer first shops online predicts how profitable they'll be for years

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-09
**Work ID:** W7202042423 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00222429261478875

**The study:** Using transaction-level panel data from a major Brazilian pet supplies retailer, researchers tracked previously offline-only customers who moved online through four different routes — organic adoption, the COVID-19 pandemic, Black Friday promotions, and a loyalty program — and applied difference-in-differences estimation. All adopters spent more than comparable offline-only shoppers, but the pathway mattered: promotion-driven adopters showed forward-buying patterns and lower subsequent profitability, while pandemic-era adopters kept reverting to stores.

**Why it matters:** It challenges the standard assumption that a multichannel customer is simply a more valuable customer, and suggests promotion-driven channel migration can flatter acquisition metrics while destroying margin.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Before celebrating online-adoption counts from a Black Friday push, marketers should segment lifetime-value forecasts by how each customer was acquired and re-run breakeven math on promotions designed to drive channel switching.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Deal-driven shoppers who stock up during a sale aren't necessarily converts — the data shows many are simply timing their purchases, and habit keeps a lot of pandemic-era online shoppers walking back into stores.

**Caveats:** Single retailer, single category (pet supplies) and single country (Brazil); difference-in-differences on observational data can control for many but not all selection differences between adoption pathways.

### 13. Rude customers don't always damage staff performance — it depends on how employees read the insult

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing* | **Published:** 2026-08-01
**Work ID:** W7202124108 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretai.2026.08.004

**The study:** Researchers combined survey data from 241 frontline employees and 34 supervisors with archival performance records at a large retail banking chain. Employees who appraised customer incivility as a challenge engaged in more proactive work behavior and performed better, while those who appraised it as a hindrance engaged in more counterproductive behavior and performed worse. Empathy, grit and employee bonuses moderated how appraisals translated into behavior.

**Why it matters:** Retail and service employers currently treat customer abuse as a uniformly toxic input; this suggests the appraisal step — and the resources available to employees — is where management can actually intervene.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Rather than only screening out difficult customers, service leaders could test training and incentive designs that build the psychological and organizational resources linked to a challenge appraisal, and track whether they show up in performance records.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A useful counterweight to the "customer is always right" reflex: the study links real performance data to how staff interpret rudeness, a reminder that abuse at the counter has measurable consequences for both workers and firms.

**Caveats:** Cross-sectional multisource survey data — the appraisal-to-performance links are associations, not proven causation; single firm, single sector (retail banking), and only 34 supervisors.

### 14. Shoppers are likelier to donate loyalty points they can't spend — unless you tell them the points are expiring

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-09-10
**Work ID:** W7212193195 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105103

**The study:** Across three experiments, researchers tested when consumers will donate reward points at online checkout. Donation intention was higher when points were non-redeemable than redeemable, and this effect was moderated both by whether the points were about to expire and by descriptive norm information about what other customers do.

**Why it matters:** Loyalty programs sit on enormous unredeemed point liabilities, and routing them into checkout charity could generate social impact and goodwill — but only if the points are framed the right way.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Loyalty teams could pilot point-donation prompts targeted at balances below the redemption threshold, and test whether adding expiration warnings or peer-behavior messages helps or backfires before rolling out program-wide.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It reveals a quirk in how people value loyalty currency: points you could actually spend feel like money, while points stranded below a redemption threshold feel like something to give away.

**Caveats:** Experiments measured stated donation intention rather than actual donations; likely panel samples with hypothetical checkout scenarios; sample sizes and country context not specified in the abstract.

### 15. In Web3 markets, too much positive buzz can actually depress brand value

**Journal:** *European Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-07-31
**Work ID:** W7171935030 | **DOI:** 10.1108/ejm-10-2022-0773

**The study:** Researchers combined interviews with Web3 experts with econometric analysis of 13 million tweets about seven Web3-native brands and 315 million NFT transactions on OpenSea. Both the volume and the valence of electronic word of mouth independently raised awareness, desirability and marketplace prices — but their interaction turned negative: overwhelmingly positive chatter signaled hype and reduced perceived credibility.

**Why it matters:** It puts hard numbers on a marketing intuition — that relentlessly positive buzz can read as manufactured — in a market where community discourse essentially is the brand.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** For brands operating in community-driven or speculative digital markets, the finding argues against maximizing positive sentiment as a KPI; consider whether unmoderated, mixed-valence discourse is a credibility asset rather than a problem to manage.
- **For general readers and consumers:** A practical skepticism heuristic for consumers: when online discussion of an asset or product is uniformly glowing, that uniformity itself is information — and in this dataset it coincided with weaker credibility.

**Caveats:** Only seven Web3-native brands, all observed during a bull market; findings from a speculative NFT context may not generalize to conventional brands; observational data, so causal direction between buzz and price is hard to pin down.

### 16. "$10 off when you spend $50" backfires with cautious shoppers

**Journal:** *Journal of Business Research* | **Published:** 2026-08-19
**Work ID:** W7203672036 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jbusres.2026.116486

**The study:** Combining field data with experiments, researchers tracked how much consumers actually spent after committing to a minimum-purchase-requirement deal. Prevention-focused shoppers — those oriented toward avoiding losses and mistakes — consistently spent less than promotion-focused shoppers once they had opted into the deal, because they brought more persuasion knowledge and suspicion about the retailer's motives to the basket. That suspicion grew sharper when the benefit was vague, as in "up to £100 off" offers.

**Why it matters:** Threshold promotions are meant to inflate basket size, but the same offer can produce very different spending depending on the shopper's mindset — and vague "up to" framing appears to trigger the distrust that suppresses spend.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Retailers running spend-and-save mechanics could test replacing vague "up to X off" language with a fixed, concrete benefit, and consider segment-level framing: reassurance and transparency for cautious, deal-skeptical customers versus aspirational framing for bargain-seekers. Treat this as a hypothesis worth A/B testing on live promotions, not a settled rule.
- **For general readers and consumers:** If you've ever added a random item to your cart to hit a free-shipping or discount threshold and then felt vaguely manipulated, that instinct is measurable — and people who feel it most spend the least. Vague offers like "save up to £100" are exactly the ones that put shoppers on guard.

**Caveats:** Mix of field and lab studies; regulatory focus is often measured or primed in experimental settings rather than observed naturally, and the abstract gives no sample sizes or country detail beyond a British-currency example.

### 17. The same food looks healthier under one nutrition label than another — and a retailer's endorsement can hurt

**Journal:** *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services* | **Published:** 2026-08-21
**Work ID:** W7203930195 | **DOI:** 10.1016/j.jretconser.2026.105057

**The study:** Five randomized between-subjects experiments with 731 adults recruited via Prolific in Poland, Spain, Italy, Portugal and Greece tested how front-of-pack nutrition labels shape product judgments. Nutri-Score, the interpretive color-coded summary, produced higher perceived healthiness than NutrInform Battery, the nutrient-by-nutrient format — and that healthiness inference carried through to brand attitude and purchase intention for both private label and national brands. But retailer-authored "health commitment" framing did not amplify the effect, and an explicit retailer endorsement actually lowered perceived healthiness while raising brand attitude, with no net gain in purchase intention.

**Why it matters:** In voluntary-labeling markets, the choice of label format is effectively a marketing decision with brand-equity consequences, not just a compliance question — and adding a retailer's own stamp of approval can undercut the credibility the label was supposed to provide.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brand and private-label teams in voluntary FOPL markets should treat label format as a testable brand lever alongside packaging and claims, and be wary of layering own-brand health endorsements on top of an independent label — the study suggests the extra signal can dilute rather than reinforce perceived healthiness.
- **For general readers and consumers:** Shoppers read a simple color-coded grade as a verdict on healthiness, while a detailed nutrient breakdown of the identical product reads as less healthy. The label design, not just the food, is shaping what people think they're buying.

**Caveats:** Online panel (Prolific) with manipulated product stimuli rather than real purchases; stated purchase intention rather than observed behavior; five Southern and Eastern European markets only; total N=731 across five studies means modest cell sizes.

### 18. Eye-tracking shows busy product images make shoppers work harder — and feel less

**Journal:** *Journal of Consumer Behaviour* | **Published:** 2026-08-23
**Work ID:** W7204047411 | **DOI:** 10.1002/cb.70232

**The study:** Researchers ran two eye-tracking experiments plus one online experiment to separate three dimensions of visual complexity in e-commerce product images: layout, element count and color. All three reduced perceptual fluency (how easily the image is processed), but only element and color complexity hurt conceptual fluency (how easily the meaning is grasped); layout complexity did not. Lower perceptual fluency was linked to reduced emotional arousal and attentional effort, an effect especially pronounced for functional products.

**Why it matters:** "More going on" in a product image is not automatically more engaging: the specific kind of clutter matters, and for utilitarian products, visual busyness appears to dampen both attention and emotional response.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** E-commerce teams could audit product imagery by complexity type rather than treating it as one variable — trimming element count and color variety for functional items while accepting more layout structure, then validating with click-through and dwell data on their own catalog.
- **For general readers and consumers:** The cluttered product photos crowding marketplace listings may be quietly working against the seller: when an image is hard to parse, browsers feel less and look less.

**Caveats:** Lab and online experimental settings with manipulated stimuli rather than real purchase environments; abstract reports no sample sizes, country or participant details; short exposure horizons typical of eye-tracking studies.

### 19. Three ways brands cause cultural appropriation harm — and three ways to head it off

**Journal:** *Journal of Marketing* | **Published:** 2026-08-09
**Work ID:** W7202056329 | **DOI:** 10.1177/00222429261478848

**The study:** Drawing on an extended qualitative study of the K-pop industry plus social theory, researchers built a framework for brands that borrow cultural elements to create novelty and distinction. They identify three distinct harms — disconnection (failing to credit the source), distortion (misrepresenting it) and dispossession (capturing the economic benefit) — and three mitigation strategies: correcting (accountability after the fact), connecting (learning from and collaborating with source cultures up front) and championing (funding initiatives that address marketplace inequality).

**Why it matters:** Most brands still handle appropriation controversies reactively, in crisis mode; this offers a diagnostic vocabulary for identifying which specific harm a campaign risks before it launches, and what kind of response actually addresses it.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Brand and creative leaders can use the disconnection/distortion/dispossession split as a pre-launch screen for any campaign borrowing from another culture — and note that an apology (correcting) addresses only one harm type, while attribution and revenue-sharing address others.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It explains why some cultural-borrowing controversies are about credit, others about caricature, and others about who gets paid — and why a brand apology so often fails to satisfy critics whose objection was really about the last one.

**Caveats:** Qualitative and theory-building work grounded in a single industry (K-pop); no quantitative test of whether the mitigation strategies actually change consumer response or brand outcomes.

### 20. Two flavors of search ads on the same platform behave very differently when bad reviews pile up

**Journal:** *Marketing Intelligence & Planning* | **Published:** 2026-08-25
**Work ID:** W7204166660 | **DOI:** 10.1108/mip-11-2025-0869

**The study:** Researchers analyzed sponsored search advertising data from a single seller of internationally known hair care brands operating on Alibaba's platform, comparing ads run for Taobao (marketplace) storefronts with ads run for Tmall (brand-authorized) storefronts. Using two-way fixed effects panel regressions plus endogeneity and robustness checks, they found the two ad channels reinforced each other: spending on one lifted the sales return of the other. But their vulnerability differed sharply — Taobao-store ads lost effectiveness as negative reviews accumulated, both for that store type and for the same product sold through the other store type, while Tmall-store ads were essentially unaffected. A higher total volume of reviews helped both channels.

**Why it matters:** Brands that sell the same product through both marketplace and brand-authorized storefronts often treat search advertising as one budget; this suggests the returns on each are conditional on very different review environments, and that negative reviews can leak across storefront types.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** If you advertise the same SKU across multiple storefront formats on one platform, test whether ad returns track review sentiment differently by format — and consider shifting search spend toward the more trust-insulated storefront when negative reviews spike, rather than cutting the budget across the board. Note this is one seller in one category, so treat it as a hypothesis to test in your own account data, not a rule.
- **For general readers and consumers:** It is a glimpse of how the 'official store' badge does real work: shoppers appear to discount bad reviews when a listing carries a platform's authorized-brand credential, and to take them far more seriously on ordinary marketplace listings — the same product, the same complaints, different levels of doubt.

**Caveats:** Advertising data came from a single seller in one product category (hair care) on one Chinese platform, with data supplied by that seller; results may not generalize to other categories, platforms or markets, and observational panel data limits causal certainty despite the robustness checks.

### 21. Celebrity-founded brands on the shelf can raise how shoppers feel about the whole store — if three kinds of fit line up

**Journal:** *Journal of Product & Brand Management* | **Published:** 2026-07-23
**Work ID:** W7170143274 | **DOI:** 10.1108/jpbm-10-2025-6420

**The study:** Two surveys tested how celebrity-owned brands stocked by retailers spill over onto the retailer itself: Study 1 used 154 undergraduates, Study 2 used 433 adult shoppers, analyzed with PLS structural equation modeling and multigroup comparisons. Store attractiveness rose when three kinds of fit were perceived — celebrity to product category, celebrity to store, and celebrity brand to the store's wider assortment — and attractiveness in turn linked to store equity and then store loyalty. The relative weight of the three fit judgments differed between self-identified celebrity 'worshippers' and non-worshippers.

**Why it matters:** Retailers increasingly stock celebrity lines as traffic drivers, and this suggests the borrowed halo depends less on the celebrity's fame than on whether the pairing makes sense on three separate dimensions at once.

**Story angles:**
- **For marketers and brand leaders:** Before signing a celebrity line, pressure-test fit with your own shoppers on all three axes — celebrity-to-category, celebrity-to-banner, and brand-to-assortment — and segment by fan intensity, since devoted fans and casual shoppers appear to weigh those cues differently. Because this is survey evidence rather than sales data, treat it as a screening framework worth validating against actual store performance.
- **For general readers and consumers:** For shoppers, the finding hints at how store choice gets quietly shaped: a familiar face on a shelf can change how appealing a whole retailer feels, even though nothing about the store's prices or service has changed.

**Caveats:** Both studies are cross-sectional surveys measuring perceptions and stated loyalty, not purchases, so causal direction cannot be established; Study 1 relies on a student sample of 154; no sales or field data, and effects are modeled as paths in a structural model.

## Bigger Picture: Feature Pitch

**The pattern:** Across this batch, the binding constraint on retail persuasion is no longer attention or price — it is consumer suspicion, and brands' own attempts at reassurance keep backfiring. A retailer's health endorsement stamped on top of an independent nutrition label *lowered* perceived healthiness (W7203930195); uniformly positive buzz *reduced* brand credibility and marketplace value in Web3 markets (W7171935030); vague "up to £100 off" framing triggered persuasion knowledge and suppressed spend among cautious shoppers (W7203672036); and relationship-and-loyalty framing paradoxically *licensed* customer opportunism (W7172339845). What works instead is third-party or structural credibility: external trustworthiness cues closed the socioeconomic gap in who takes a salesperson's advice (W7212164165), and a platform's authorized-brand badge insulated ad returns from accumulating bad reviews while ordinary marketplace listings bled effectiveness (W7204166660).

**Why pitch this now:** Greenwashing and drip-pricing enforcement is tightening on exactly the vague, self-issued claims these studies show don't work, while retail media and marketplace consolidation are pushing more brands into storefronts where a platform badge — not the brand's own voice — carries the trust.

**Angle:** A feature on the collapse of self-issued credibility in retail: why the marketing industry's instinct to add another reassuring signal now subtracts value, and why the trust that converts is increasingly rented from third parties, platforms, and even unmoderated criticism. Pitch to a CMO/strategy readership, with a consumer-facing variant for readers who suspect they're being handled and, per the evidence, are right.

**Potential headlines:**
- The evidence against saying you're trustworthy
- Glowing reviews made a brand worth less
- Every reassurance on the label is costing you a sale

- **Marketing Week** — its strategy readership argues constantly about brand trust metrics; this reframes "positive sentiment" as a KPI that can invert.
- **MIT Sloan Management Review** — suits the governance-level argument that credibility must be sourced externally, with multi-study evidence rather than case anecdotes.
